# Chapter 33: The Calamity Flag (Discovery)

---

Thursday morning.

Four days until the duel.

Three days until I either master the Void Counter or die standing in a flowerbed, which, given my training history, was honestly the most likely venue.

I left the dormitory early.

Partly to walk the stance. Iris's instructions had been very clear: *carry the stillness with you. Walk like a door on a hinge.* Which was great advice if you were a legendary sword saint and deeply unhelpful if you were a man whose primary survival stat was horizontal acceleration.

But mostly I left early because three hours of sleep was apparently my new quota, and lying in bed staring at the ceiling practicing *not flinching* had diminishing returns around 4 AM.

The sun was barely up. The paths were empty. Dew on the grass, birds doing their thing, the whole peaceful academy morning package that in any other story would be called *calm before the storm* and in this story was called *the tutorial screen before the boss arena.*

I turned the corner past the fountain plaza, and my feet stopped.

Not because I decided to stop.

Because the autonomic threat-assessment firmware I'd been running since the library crisis fired a full-body **halt** command before my conscious brain had even finished rendering the scene.

Frost.

On the ground.

Patsches of it, white and thin and glittering, spread across the flagstones in a fan pattern leading away from the girls' dormitory wing.

I stared at it.

It stared back, in the way frost does, which is not at all, and yet somehow smugly.

'Okay.'

'Okay. It's late spring.'

'Late spring. Stellaris Royal Academy, temperate zone, southern campus. Last frost date: roughly six weeks ago. I know this because I checked the almanac mechanic in the game for a fishing side quest, which is a sentence about my old life I'm choosing not to unpack right now.'

'It is not frost weather.'

'There should be no frost.'

I crouched down.

Looked closer.

And my stomach dropped through the flagstones, through the campus foundation, and down into the plot itself.

Because it wasn't frost.

I mean, it was *frost-shaped.* Frost-flavored. To anyone else on this campus — a groundskeeper, a sleepy professor, literally any normal person — it was an unseasonal cold patch, weird but explainable, somebody probably spilled enchanted coolant, whatever, file it under "academy things."

But I had spent 347 hours in this game.

I had run the Alice Route four times.

I had gotten the bad ending where this exact texture covered a horizon-to-horizon screen.

'That's not frost.'

'That's abyssal crystal.'

The structure was wrong. Real frost grows random, chaotic, feathery. This grew in *patterns* — hexagonal lattices, each one a tiny perfect snowflake of crystallized mana, and each one radiating outward from a single line.

A trajectory line.

I stood up slowly.

I followed the line with my eyes.

It ran up the wall of the girls' dormitory wing. Past the second floor. Past the third. And terminated, with the inevitability of a quest marker, at a specific window on the fourth floor.

A window I knew.

A window I had, in my old life, clicked a dialogue option next to roughly eleven thousand times.

Alice's room.

'No.'

'No no no no—'

I said it out loud. Quiet. Under my breath. The word came out flat and small, the way words do when your brain has already finished the disaster math and is just waiting for your mouth to catch up.

I checked the calendar. The mental one. The one carved into my skull by 347 hours of obsessive completionism.

Day 24.

'The First Calamity Event.'

The word sat in my head like a stone in a shoe.

If you'd asked me a week ago to list the scripted events of the Alice Route, I could have recited them like patch notes. Easy. Automatic.

*Day 28. First Calamity Event. The Abyssal entity sealed inside Alice Laurent stirs during a night of high emotional stress — specifically, the closing ceremony of the academy's spring festival, when Alice, overwhelmed, exhausted, and socially destroyed, loses her grip on the seal. Partial containment breach. Localized cryogenic event. In-game term: "The Night the Dormitory Froze."*

*Player character Lucas Ashford notices her distress during the ceremony. Follows her. Reaches her room as the seal fractures. Channels his mana into the containment lattice — one of the first scenes that demonstrates just how monstrously powerful the protagonist actually is — and stabilizes her while she cries into his shoulder.*

*Affection +30. Route flag locked. One of the pivotal early scenes of the route.*

*Mandatory. Scripted. Cannot be missed in a standard playthrough.*

'And when,' I thought, my mouth dry, 'is the Mock Duel Festival's closing ceremony?'

I already knew.

I pulled up the schedule I'd been handed at registration, already knowing what it would say, needing to see it anyway, the way you bite down on a sore tooth to confirm it still hurts.

Day 28. Friday.

Closing ceremony: 8:00 PM, Grand Arena.

Final match of the tournament — the featured bout, the one with the most spectators, the one the upperclassmen actually showed up for:

**Derrick Holt vs. Lucen Gray. 8:30 PM.**

'Same night.'

'Same night.'

I stood in an empty path at sunrise, in the middle of patterns of impossible ice, and watched the disaster assemble itself in my head with the clean, horrible elegance of a speedrun route.

Item one: the closing ceremony is the emotional-stress trigger. The crowds. The attention. The noise. In the game, that's what cracks Alice's composure.

Item two: the resonance node's instability is *lingering.* Iris confirmed it. The library crisis didn't end, it paused. The ambient mana of the west wing is still off-key.

Item three: Alice has been *clingy.* Tactile. Desperately cheerful. I had been filing that under "affection gain" like an idiot.

Because that's what the game files it under too.

The game *tells* you Alice is falling for the protagonist — blushing sprite, brighter smile, clingier dialogue. And then, if you skip the ceremony scene on a modded playthrough, the game *also* tells you, about four in-game hours later, why she was suddenly so tactile and desperate.

It's not a crush.

It's a drowning person grabbing the nearest solid object.

The affection flag and the Calamity flag were the same flag. They were *always* the same flag. The route just dresses a containment breach in romantic lighting.

'Oh, you absolute bastards,' I whispered, at the developers, at the world, at myself for not seeing it sooner. 'You hid the boss fight inside the romance route.'

And item four — the one that turned a problem into an obituary:

In the game, *Lucas* stabilizes her.

Lucas, the walking mana reactor, seat 4-C, main character, the only entity on this continent whose mana signature the seal was arguably *built* around.

Lucas doesn't have a duel on Day 28. In the original game there was no Derrick Holt bout at the closing ceremony — there was no *reason* for Lucas to be anywhere except the stands, which is exactly why the event works. He notices. He leaves. He follows her. Twenty-second cutscene, problem solved.

But the timeline had shifted. Dragged sideways by the Butterfly Effect, by Derrick's challenge, by the butterfly flapping its stupid wings in the form of my continued breathing.

Now Lucas Ashford was booked through the entire closing ceremony — I'd seen his bracket, he had an exhibition bout in the *second-to-last* slot, and the duel bracket committee had been drooling over it for days.

He would be in the arena when Alice broke.

'So it has to be me.'

The thought arrived fully formed, the way the bad ones always do.

And immediately after it, the math.

'No. No, that's not possible.'

'I'm *fighting* at 8:30. The Calamity hits around 9:00. The arena is nine hundred meters from the girls' dormitory wing. Even if I forfeit the second the bell rings — which by the way means Derrick peels me like a fruit in front of the entire academy — even then—'

I did the traversal math.

I hated the traversal math.

'My best sprint over flat ground is approximately a jog that would embarrass a seated turtle. Nine hundred meters through festival crowds, in formal duel uniform, after evading a giant for however long I survive...'

The numbers came back the way they always came back.

Red.

*[Bad End No. 22: The Frozen Library — CALAMITY VARIANT — approaching.]*

There it was. The notification ghost-text burned across the morning air, visible only to me, cheerful as a funeral invitation.

The Frozen Library had been a *taste.* A contained preview. A twelve-room localized freeze that I'd survived through cheese, luck, and an emotional support princess.

The full event was the girls' dormitory wing.

Four floors. Two hundred students. Asleep.

I looked at the frost again. At the window at the end of the line.

'Right,' I said, to nobody, in the tone of a man accepting a game over he intends to reload. 'New schedule.'

---

I didn't go to homeroom.

Homeroom was a cutscene I could skip. There was exactly one location in this game where you went when you needed to find Alice Laurent before an event flag, and I'd found her there every run, every time, without exception.

The Frozen Greenhouse.

The academy's botanical annex on the eastern edge, half-forgotten, half-condemned, kept at temperatures that made normal plants file complaints. In the lore, Alice tended it because she was the only student the cold didn't bite.

In the meta, it was her safe zone. Her idle animation. Where the devs put the character model when she wasn't on-screen.

I pushed the door open.

The cold hit first.

Then the crystals.

I stopped in the doorway, and for a moment I didn't move, didn't breathe, because my brain was comparing two files side by side:

*Greenhouse, Chapter 19 visit:* fine frost on the glass panes, a few clusters of pale ice flowers in the corners. Pretty. Ambient. The world's most dangerous terrarium wearing its nice outfit.

*Greenhouse, now:* not that.

The crystals had grown.

They had grown the way mold grows — confidently, in directions, with intent. Dark-edged now, the translucent blue gone almost smoky at the tips, like ice that had been left too close to a fire and had decided it liked it. They climbed the glass walls in lattices. They had encased one entire workbench in a shell of geometric knives.

One of them had grown overnight along the door frame.

It was sharp.

It was *pointed at the door.*

At whoever might come through it.

'That's a trap formation,' I thought, and my skin did the thing. 'That's not ambient mana. That's *territorial.* The dungeon is setting up spawn traps.'

"Lucen?"

The voice came from the back of the greenhouse, from behind a wall of frozen roses, and it was bright and warm and wrong in a way that made everything worse.

"Lucen, hi! Oh wow, hi — I didn't hear you come in, these stupid crystals crunch so loud I can never hear anything anymore, I sound like a walking cereal bowl—"

Alice came around the roses.

Smiling.

Eyes bright.

A tartan ribbon slightly crooked in her hair, flour on her apron, a little tin box in her hands.

The most convincing performance of a normal girl I had ever seen.

I would have given it an award. I had, in my old life, *been* given this exact performance by this exact sprite and believed it, four playthroughs, one hundred percent, fell for it every time.

This time I was watching the tells.

The ribbon was crooked because she'd put it on forty times and her hands were tired. The smile reached her eyes because it *always* reached her eyes — she'd trained it to. The tin was held against her chest with both hands like a shield, and where her fingertips pressed the metal, the steel had gone white with hoarfrost.

The room was minus-something degrees.

She wasn't wearing a coat.

"I made you cookies," she said. "Well, I attempted cookies. The oven and I had a disagreement, and by disagreement I mean I think I won? The bottom ones are good! The bottom ones are *people* cookies. It's layered. It's a tier system."

"Alice."

"There's a layer that's basically coal but I scraped it and underneath is—"

"Alice."

My voice came out gentler than I felt. She stopped. The smile stayed on her face like a mask that hadn't been told the play was over.

I stepped past the door crystal — carefully, giving it the kind of clearance you give a sleeping predator — and into the cold of the greenhouse proper.

"How are you sleeping?"

The tiniest pause.

Half a beat. In dialogue terms, a skipped frame. In a fighting game, a punishable drop.

"Fine!" she said.

It was, I noted distantly, the exact pitch of *fine* that players of MMOs use when the raid leader asks if they're ready and they have seventeen unfinished debuffs.

"The truth, Alice."

The smile held.

The smile held for almost four seconds — I counted, because counting seconds was my whole life now, I was becoming the academy of counting seconds — and then it didn't break so much as *set down.* Carefully. Like a glass being placed on a table by someone whose hands are shaking.

She looked at the cookie tin.

She looked at the crystals crawling up the roses.

She looked at her own hands.

And suddenly the greenhouse was very quiet, and Alice Laurent — player-two heroine, abyssal vessel, smiler of smiles — looked exactly what she was: small, and tired, and scared out of her mind.

"Not great," she said.

The words came out barely above the hum of the cold.

"It's been... since the library. It's been loud."

"Loud."

"The thing." She didn't look up. Her thumb traced the lid of the tin, and frost bloomed under it like ink in water. "The thing inside me. Most of the time it's just... *there.* Like weather. You forget about weather. But since the library it doesn't sleep when I sleep. It's loud at night."

"How loud?"

She laughed.

It was a terrible laugh. It was the laugh of someone giving a status update on their own sinking ship. "It shows me things."

The frost on the tin spread.

"Beautiful things sometimes. Palaces of ice. Cities where nothing ever melts and nothing ever dies and everything is *quiet* forever." Her voice went thin. "It shows them to me like it's... showing off. Like it's proud. 'Look what I can make. Look what we could be.'"

My pulse was doing the thing. I made myself stay still. Door on a hinge. Point fixed. Listen.

"And terrible things?" I asked.

Alice looked up at me then.

And her eyes did it — the flicker. I'd seen it on the sprite, I'd seen it in the bad-end cutscenes, I'd never seen it in person: blue to dark, blue to dark, a light behind stained glass swinging in a wind.

"Terrible things," she whispered, "it shows me *you.*"

The greenhouse was cold. The floor of my stomach was colder.

"That's why it's worse now." Her voice kept going, small and fast, like if she stopped talking it would fill the silence. "It didn't used to. But now when I try to shut it out, it just puts on your face. You being kind. You reading in the library. You at the fountain. It knows — it *learned* — that I don't push away when it looks like you. So it wears you. Like a door it can open with a smile."

*[Note from the Evil Goddess: it found the correct key.]*

*[She's sorry. She did not pick this lock. The lock was picked by literature.]*

Shut up, I told the Goddess, with absolute sincerity, and with something else underneath I didn't examine.

Because here was the mechanism.

Here was the whole rotten engine, laid out like a speedrun skip: the Abyss wanted out. The Abyss was a hunger — a void, an absence wearing a cage. And I was the Void-touched boy, the zero-mana anomaly, the empty slot. Of *course* the entity fixated on me. Of course the thing that was nothing wanted the boy who was nothing. It probably thought I was family.

And it was using my face to sweet-talk its jailer into loosening the bars.

"Alice." I stepped closer. Ignored the crystals that angled toward me like compass needles. "Listen to me. The closing ceremony. Friday night. I want you to stay in your room. Don't go. Lock the door, put on every heating charm you can get, and do *not* let anyone in—"

"Why?"

"—except me."

She blinked.

"You'll... come?"

Her whole face did the thing. The thing that made four playthroughs worth of dopamine fire in a skull that no longer existed. Hope, naked and enormous, from a girl who'd just told me her inner darkness wore my face as a mask.

"I'm working on it," I said, which was the honest version. "It's complicated. But I need you to promise me. Stay in. Don't attend the ceremony. Wait for me."

"I'll wait," she said, instantly, too fast, the way drowning people say yes to ropes. "Of course I'll wait. Lucen, I—"

She reached out and took my hand.

Her hand was very cold.

The frost didn't bite me. It never did. In the game files there was a line of lore — *the cold does not harm what it cannot unmake* — and in 347 hours I'd never once stopped to think about what that implied about the boy holding the girl's hand.

I squeezed back.

"Four days," I said. "Hold it together for four days."

"Okay," Alice whispered.

The crystals in the room turned, all of them, a few degrees, toward us, like sunflowers toward a light.

'It heard,' I thought.

'It heard *me* promise.'

'Good. Then it knows the fight's booked.'

---

I walked out of the greenhouse into a fine late-spring morning and immediately put my back against the outer wall and breathed like I'd tanked a boss phase with no potions.

Okay.

Okay, inventory check. Information gained:

Calamity trigger: Day 28, ~9:00 PM.

My duel: Day 28, 8:30 PM. Arena. Nine hundred meters from target.

Required action: reach Alice's room before 9:00.

Obstacle: a Derrick Holt-shaped wall of scheduled violence directly between those two points.

The original strategy — the comfortable strategy, the strategy I'd been raising like a child — was the classic dodge-tank cheese. Evade for the full duration. Never get hit. Survive on points. A four-to-ten minute marathon of pure horizontal living.

That strategy was now dead.

Because surviving Derrick *slow* meant being in the arena when Alice broke. And the entity wouldn't wait for my match to wrap up. The Abyss did not respect the tournament bracket.

'I have to end the duel fast.'

The thought felt insane even forming. It was like deciding to beat the raid boss by speedrunning the cutscene.

'I have to beat Derrick Holt. *Decisively.* Not evade him. Not outlast him. *Beat* him, quickly, and then run nine hundred meters in formalwear like I'm late for the last train of my life.'

There was exactly one tool in my entire skillset that could produce a fast, decisive victory over a physically superior opponent.

One.

A single technique, four days old in my hands.

*The Void Counter.*

Iris's voice, in the lamplight: *a moment of perfect stillness. The blade reaches you. And you are not where the blade needs you to be — not because you dodged.*

*Because you were never fighting the blade at all.*

One shot. One perfect moment of zero flinch. Redirect the full force of Derrick's opening charge — and Derrick, to his credit as a boss, always, *always* opened with the charge — straight back into him.

If it worked: a first-touch knockout. Elegant. Fast. Cinematic, even.

If it didn't work: my four-second record against an *imaginary* sword became a zero-point-four-second record against a real one, delivered to my skull at combat velocity.

'Four days of training.'

'One technique.'

'One window of maybe forty-five seconds between the duel bell and the Calamity countdown.'

'And a sprint I cannot physically complete.'

The morning air was cool on my face. Somewhere a bell rang for first period. I thought about Alice's eyes going blue-dark-blue like a faulty lamp. I thought about two hundred students asleep in that dormitory. I thought about Lucas Ashford on a podium, holding a trophy he'd never know he'd traded two hundred lives for, if I let the schedule stand.

'Reload,' I thought. 'Not an option. Ironman mode. One save.'

'Okay.'

'We grind.'

The sun came up a little higher over the frozen ground by the dormitory wing, and the frost patterns caught the light, and for one half-second they looked almost beautiful — a whole wall of blue-white fire, blooming quietly toward the sky.

Then the System spoke, in text the color of a held breath.

```
[SYSTEM ALERT — TIMELINE CONVERGENCE DETECTED]

[First Calamity Event — Estimated Trigger: Day 28, approx. 9:00 PM.]
[Scheduled Duel: Lucen Gray vs. Derrick Holt — Day 28, 8:30 PM.]
[Required duel duration for intervention window: UNDER 15 MINUTES.]
[Required transit time, Arena → Girls' Dormitory: UNDER 15 MINUTES.]
[Distance: 900 meters (through festival crowds, in formal duel attire).]
[Assessed maximum sprint speed: 'moderately brisk walk.']
[Gap between required and available speed: HISTORIC.]

[Bad End No. 22: 'The Frozen Library' — CALAMITY VARIANT — approaching.]
[Bad End No. 63: 'Skin for Love' — CALAMITY VARIANT — converging.]
[Convergence probability: 61% and rising.]

[Note: The Evil Goddess has read your plan.]
[She would like you to know that it is, without exaggeration, the worst plan she has ever evaluated, and she has evaluated eleven thousand years of plans.]
[She would also like you to know that you are the first person to ever consider fighting the Calamity *at all,* let alone scheduling it between a duel and a footrace.]

[She is proud of you.]

[She has also begun drafting your epitaph. Working title: "He Died As He Lived — Stiff, Late, and Sprinting."]
```

I stared at the last line.

Then I laughed — one sharp bark of it, echoing off the empty cloister, startling some decorative academy birds out of a hedge.

'Good,' I thought, pushing off the wall, setting my shoulders down and back, feeling for the hinge.

'Draft all you want.'

'I've reloaded better gamers than you.'

The stance settled into my walk — crooked, fragile, four days from functional.

I headed for the training grounds anyway.

Four days.

One technique.

One flinch, deleted.
