# Chapter 4: Seat 7-F, Right Side, Window

Morning of Day Two came at me like an unpaid bill.

Which is to say, exactly on schedule, and with zero regard for how little sleep I'd gotten.

I hadn't slept. Obviously. Sleeping in a world where six hundred and thirty-six documented Bad Endings were actively hunting my neck seemed irresponsible, like napping inside a mousetrap because the cheese smelled nice.

So I'd spent the night in my new basement room — acquired successfully, thank you very much, because apparently nobody else in the entire academy wanted to room in the haunted cellar — reviewing my mental walkthrough of Year One and trying not to think about the way Elara Voss had looked at me.

'Page she hadn't written.'

I shoved the thought into a mental drawer. Nailed it shut. Added a padlock for good measure.

'School. Focus. School is survivable. School has rules.'

That was a lie I was choosing to believe today.

---

The homeroom of First-Year Class A occupied the third floor of the main lecture wing, and I had to give the game developers credit — they'd built a nice room.

Tiered seating rose up from the front in gentle arcs, forty desks arranged in five ascending rows, each one polished dark wood with an inlaid mana-channel that supposedly amplified light-attribute note-taking.

Don't ask me why note-taking needed mana amplification. Magic academies are where budget goes to die.

A blackboard stretched across the whole front wall, enchanted to reproduce the instructor's handwriting in real time, so the students in the back could read it. Thoughtful. In the game, this detail came up exactly once, in a background event where the board glitched and spelled out a death threat, and everyone laughed, because apparently this world's tolerance for ominous signs is calibrated to "weird Tuesday."

And the windows.

Three enormous windows ran down the right side of the room, arched, leaded glass, overlooking the Starfall Courtyard — the famous courtyard where, in Year Two, a chunk of a dead god's meteor was scheduled to land.

On that courtyard.

'Which means these windows are scheduled to become a glass shrapnel delivery system.'

I made a note to never, under any circumstances, be sitting next to those windows in Year 2.

In Year 1 they were just... nice. Sunlight, flower beds, distant towers. Extremely scenic.

Which is exactly why I was sitting next to one.

---

Let me explain my logic, because I can hear the goddess laughing already.

Every classroom in every school story ever written has a protagonist seat. Window side, second row from the back. You know the one. The seat where the camera lives. The seat where a boy can gaze out at the clouds while the narrative monologues.

That seat was 6-F.

I did not sit in 6-F.

I sat in 7-F.

Row seven. Column F. Back row, far right, window side, dead corner.

In the game, this seat belonged to an entity named — I am not joking — "Student F." Capital S, capital F, as if some developer had typed a placeholder into the asset list at 3 AM and nobody ever circled back.

Student F had no lines. Student F had no events. Student F had no romance flags, no combat flags, no death flags. Student F was so thoroughly, transcendently irrelevant that in a game with over four hundred named characters, three separate fan wikis, and a seven-year content lifespan, somebody once posted on a forum "has anyone ever noticed the empty seat in Class A homeroom cutscenes" and the top reply was "what empty seat."

There is no invisibility cheaper than being corner furniture.

So I claimed it. I was in that room forty minutes early specifically to claim it, because you better believe every Korean webnovel reader instinct in my soul understood that the Protagonist Seat was a trap and the background NPC seat was the true endgame gear.

'Achievement Unlocked: Most Irrelevant Man in Alphabetria.'

I sat down. Rearranged my coat. Surveyed my domain of nothing.

---

My uniform, by the way, was doing me zero favors.

Standard academy black, cheap cut, provisional student issue — which meant no house crest, no silk trim, no embroidered sigil, nothing. A void where my social status should be.

Every other student funneling into the room was wearing variations on the same uniform, but theirs had *stuff* on it. Gold thread. Silver thread. House crests rendered in colors so saturated they looked wet. One boy had a crest that was actively glowing, which seemed excessive, but I respected the commitment to identifiable expensive-ness.

I looked like a funeral attendee who had wandered into a fashion show by taking a wrong turn at the casket.

'Good. Perfect. Blend into the wallpaper, you magnificent gray goblin.'

The bruises from yesterday's... everything... were mostly hidden under the collar and cuffs. Mostly. The ones on my face were doing a decent impression of "pale boy who lost a fight with a staircase," and I'd decided to just let the rumor write itself.

Stairs. Final answer.

---

The room filled up.

I did something then that I would not have believed a month ago, back when I was a warm man in a soft apartment who thought the height of danger was a bad ending in a visual novel.

I memorized every face.

Not out of social interest. Out of threat assessment.

Baron Holt's son — that was the fat one, second row, middle. Bren Holt. Chapter 3 minor antagonist, the archetypal fat noble bully who trips the protagonist in the hallway, gets humiliated publicly by the hero, holds a grudge for three volumes, and eventually becomes comic relief after his father's treason is exposed. Threat level: a wet sandwich. Avoid on general principle, because even wet sandwiches can give you food poisoning.

The girl in the blue crest, front row — that was Sylvi Meredith, mage family, background character with about six lines, all of them variations of "amazing!" during tournament arcs. Harmless.

Two boys in the back left whispering about horses. Harmless. One girl reading ahead in the textbook. Harmless. One kid already asleep at his desk. *Understandable.* Harmless.

I ran down the roster like a customs agent stamping passports at an airport open to... okay it was a bad metaphor because airports are the actual worst.

Point was: thirty-seven students, and I could account for thirty-six of them from the script. Minor antagonists, background faces, one-shot quest-givers. Manageable. Calculable. Safe.

Then the door opened, and the thirty-seventh walked in, and my threat assessment engine blew a fuse.

---

"Ah—sorry, am I late?" a boy said from the doorway.

He had a voice like someone had melted honey over a Sunday morning.

Instructor Kaelin — a granite-faced, gray-bearded, retired-knight of a man who had been scowling at the attendance ledger like it owed him money — actually smiled. It looked painful, like his face had to file paperwork to allow it.

"Not at all, Mister Ashford. You saved my carriage yesterday. I believe you've earned three minutes of grace."

'Three minutes of grace.' Gagging noises.

No, wait, no, that was unfair.

*Instructor Kaelin* was the one saved?

I ran the script in my head. The game opening: carriage, runaway horses, Professor Merith—

Merith. It was supposed to be Merith. The plot had drifted *again*. The dominoes were falling in a slightly different order.

I filed that away under 'problems' and turned my attention to the boy in the doorway.

Lucas Ashford.

The genuine, sealed-in-the-box, factory-original Protagonist.

---

The game had praised him as "a boy of simple origin and extraordinary destiny." Which is developer-speak for "fresh-out-the-box chosen one, some assembly required."

Seeing him in person was... jarring. He was *glowing*. Not magically. Just... the light in the room filed a formal complaint about being upstaged.

Sun-gold hair, still faintly tousled from whatever heroic thing he had presumably been doing five minutes ago. Clear blue eyes that had never once scrolled a gacha game's terms of service. A jawline chiseled by the gods as a personal favor, on a body that had been "lifting fenceposts since childhood" as a cheap excuse for abs.

The whole package radiated an aura so straightforwardly, blindingly *good* that I felt my own eyes water, like standing too close to a beautiful bonfire.

Everybody in the room turned to look.

A few of the noble girls straightened. Bren Holt's lip curled — already at the 'snooty glare' stage of his very predictable arc. The sleeping kid actually woke up, rubbed his eyes, looked at Lucas, muttered something about "handsome," and went back to sleep.

Lucas smiled at the class. He fully did. He unleashed the weapon. The full-wattage, guileless, I-saved-a-carriage-yesterday-and-I'd-do-it-again protagonist smile.

The room's ambient warmth felt, objectively, about two degrees kinder.

'There it is.'

'The Hero's Aura. The main character's passive buff.'

I felt almost nostalgic. Here was the guy. The guy whose story I had played, from beginning to end, four times. I knew his stat sheet. I knew his skill tree. I knew which dialog choices made him blush and which moral dilemmas broke his heart.

I had steered him through every heartbreak and triumph. I had wept during the Year 3 throne-room scene when he buried—

Stop. Stop. No attachment. No investment.

'He's the series' leading man, and I am unqualified background scenery. Our contracts are very different. Let us not cross the streams.'

I busied myself with my desk.

Which was when, because the universe finds my suffering *hilarious*—

The golden retriever protagonist *looked directly at me.*

---

His eyes found mine across the room with the uncanny accuracy of a homing missile.

And his expression... flickered.

It was the strangest, smallest thing. A crease between the brows. A momentary dimming of the sunshine, as if a cloud had passed over something inside him.

I knew that look. I had seen it, four playthroughs ago, rendered in sprite-art, in the *opening sequence*.

The game begins on a road. A carriage passes a ditch. And in the ditch, half-buried in roadside weeds, Lucas Ashford finds a dead boy. A servant boy, gaunt, starved, died fleeing someplace worse. Lucas buries him. Swears over the grave to enroll in the academy and change the world that lets things like that happen.

That dead boy's face is the first face the player ever sees.

That face, canonically, belonged to *Lucen Gray*.

I was looking at the hero of this world... from inside the skin of his object lesson.

'Stop staring at your own corpse, you idiot.'

Something unreadable passed through those blue eyes — an itch of déjà vu, a guilt with no address. He tilted his head slightly, like a dog hearing a frequency no human can hear, lips parting as if to speak—

'NO. Absolutely not. Disengage.'

I dropped my gaze to my desk with the mechanical smoothness of a slot machine lever and began studying the absolutely fascinating, mindbogglingly compelling grain of the wood. Ah yes. Oak. Planks. Incredible. Riveting. There were *lines* in it. Several of them.

From the corner of my vision, I saw him hesitate... then let it go, turning back to the front, sliding into a seat near the middle of the room — 4-C, the *actual* designated Protagonist Seat, exactly on script.

I exhaled through my nose.

'Crisis averted. We're fine. We're good. That was nothing. A blip. He saw a gaunt stranger and remembered a dead stranger. Humans do that.'

**[Note: The Evil Goddess wishes to know if you *always* sweat through your shirt before the first class of the day.]**

'It was eighty degrees in the stairwell.'

**[Note: She notes it is autumn and sixty-one degrees.]**

'I run warm.'

**[Note: She doubts this intensely.]**

---

Kaelin began lecture.

It was syllabus stuff — code of conduct, rule structure, the prohibition on unauthorized dueling in the west gardens. I half-listened. I knew the rulebook better than he did; I'd beta-tested the "Excessive Dueling" Bad End.

I was relaxing. Actually, genuinely relaxing. Thirty-six accounted-for students. Protagonist successfully seated. Zero incidents. The goddess was quiet. My heart rate was almost inside the accepted range. Seat 7-F: flawless investment.

Maybe, just maybe, I could ride the background all the way to the safe ending. Let Lucas have the glory. Let the plot chug along its rails. Let Elara Voss file me under "that weird boy I shouldn't think about" and never think about me again.

It was moments like this, I would later reflect, that separated optimists from survivors.

Because at exactly twenty minutes past the bell, with the stealth of a tidal wave sneaking up on a beach—

The classroom door opened.

---

There was no knock.

No request for entry. No apology for lateness.

The door simply opened, with the unhurried arrogance of a thing that knows every room in the world is on loan to it.

And the entire temperature of the room changed.

I don't mean metaphorically. Several students physically shivered. A lantern flame in the corner guttered and straightened, like a servant coming to attention.

Kaelin stopped mid-sentence.

Every face turned toward the door except mine. Mine stayed nailed to the wood grain, because some deep hindbrain instinct older than language, older than gaming, older than everything, screamed at me in a voice like falling rock:

*Do not move.*

*Do not breathe.*

*Do not be noticed by that.*

She walked in.

---

I'd seen her sprite a hundred times. I'd triggered her scenes. I'd chased — and failed — her route.

None of that — *none* of it — had prepared me.

In the game, she was art. Red-and-gold pixels, a sharp chin, a frowned-at-the-camera portrait on the character select screen. You could look at her and think: *hmm, spicy, high difficulty, bet she has a secret soft side.* You could minimize the window and go make ramen.

But here she was *real*, and the part the game had failed to communicate — the part that could not fit into a 640x480 sprite, the part that no amount of "WARNING: Do not provoke" tooltip could convey — was the *pressure*.

She entered like weather.

Copper-red hair, straight as a drawn blade, spilling to her waist and catching the windowlight until it looked less like hair and more like liquid fire wearing the *shape* of hair. A face carved with the kind of fine, cold precision that makes sculptors weep — high cheekbones, a severe and perfect mouth, features so exactly composed they felt like a threat.

And her eyes.

Great burning Mercury, her *eyes.*

Gold. Not honeyed, not amber, not pretty metaphor gold — *molten*, forge-hot, circulating gold, irises that looked like you could dip a coin in them and it would come out screaming. They swept the room, once, lazy and total, the way a lighthouse beam sweeps water, indifferent to everything it touches because everything it touches is *less* than the light.

Sixteen years old. First in line to the Imperial Throne of Vessia. The Empire's Dawn, the papers called her. The future Empress.

Iris Aurelia.

And in the blithe language of the game's marketing copy: *"Route 4 Heroine. Difficulty: Nightmare. Bone count of previous players: beyond tallying."*

---

What the game knew, and what I knew, and what apparently the *actual* world knew, was that this girl had personally ordered and attended the executions of two traitorous nobles before her sixteenth birthday.

Not signed off on. *Attended.* Watched. With her chin on her fist and that same bored, golden, glaze of disinterest.

In Year 3 of the game, if you fail her route, there is a Bad Ending called **Sunrise Eternal**, in which the entire capital city is burned to smooth orange glass under an artillery barrage of dragonfire magic, so scarred and melted that the sun never sets off it — a city that gleams, dawn-bright, *forever,* because nothing lives there to cast a shadow.

She caused that.

Over a *rejection.*

The player's affection meter wasn't high enough. She'd grown *bored.*

Her love is a wildfire. Her heartbreak is a sunset you cannot survive.

The fandom called her "the Final Boss who happens to be romanceable."

The fandom praised her character writing as "so toxic, ten out of ten, would get incinerated again."

I had called her that too, in my past life, from the safety of a fold-out chair and a cooling bag of chips.

In person, standing twenty feet from her, the only thought my brain could produce was a dry, desperate, gibbering:

*'Keep very, very still.'*

---

"Your Highness," said Kaelin, recovering, bowing at an angle that approached 90 degrees. A man who'd fought in two wars bowed to her like she had a knife to the nation's throat, which she essentially did and eternally would. "You grace us."

"I was held up," Iris Aurelia said.

Her voice. It was *quiet.* That was the terrifying part. I'd expected a whip-crack, an imperial declamation, some booming lacquer of authority. Instead her voice was soft. Level. Warm, almost. The warmth of banked coals you could fall asleep next to and never wake up from.

She did not explain what had held her up. Of course she didn't. You don't explain traffic when the traffic *moves for you.*

She stood at the head of the room and let her gaze do its lazy, total sweep. I watched from the corner of my eye — my face still aimed at my desk, my body language screaming, with every ounce of method-acting I possessed, *I am furniture, please sit on me and forget that I am alive.*

Her gaze passed over Sylvi Meredith — *dismissed.* Over the horse-whispering boys — *dismissed.* Over the reader — *dismissed.* Over Bren Holt, who had half-risen out of his seat in Pavlovian noble reflex and froze, bowing too low, his fat cheeks going blotchy — *flicker of contempt, dismissed.*

Down the rows it went.

Like the angel of death reading the phone book.

*Please. Please let it slide past. I'm furniture. I am the desk. We are one desk-being. The wood has claimed me—*

Her eyes stopped.

---

Everything stopped.

Not dramatically. Not with a gasp or music. The lecture didn't pause, the lantern didn't flicker. One second her gaze was moving down the row and the next it just... *wasn't* anymore. It had parked. It had come to rest, with the soft inevitability of a piano lid closing on fingers.

On me.

The boy in Seat 7-F. The nothing seat. The invisible seat. The seat the game had built specifically because even the *camera* ignored it.

The gold eyes fixed on my face, and the weight of them was so immediate and physical that I felt the hair at my nape twitch, felt my lungs forget their job, felt — distinctly, humiliatingly — my heart slam a fresh dent into my ribs like it was trying to tunnel out the other way and abandon ship.

'Don't look up don't look up don't look—'

I looked up.

I don't know why. Horror-movie rules. The victim always looks.

Her eyes met mine.

Red hair. Gold eyes. Pale skin. A still, predatorial composure that wouldn't have been out of place on a lion deciding whether the gazelle was *worth* the running.

Three full seconds.

One. *She's looking.*

Two. *Why is she looking.*

Three. *What could she possibly be looking at, there is nothing here, there has never been anything here—*

A tiny, minute, fractional shift. The smallest imaginable thing. Not a smile. The *idea* of the beginning of the concept of a smile, shelved at the corner of her mouth, filed away.

She broke eye contact. She turned, unhurried, and walked down the central aisle to her seat.

Row 1. Dead center. The seat directly in front of the blackboard, beneath the highest light, in the eye of the room and the eye of the instructor and theoretically the eye of *God* — the seat that the entire room's feng shui existed to frame. Of course. *Of course.* The main character seat was for *heroes.* The *villainess empress* seat was front and center, where nothing could happen behind her back.

She sat.

She did not look back.

Kaelin cleared his throat. "Ahem. As I was saying. The rules of conduct..."

The lecture resumed. The world resumed. The lantern straightened. Somewhere a bird started singing again, idiot bird, it didn't know, it hadn't *seen*—

Nobody else had noticed anything at all.

---

I sat very still for about a minute.

I was aware of my hands under my desk. They were doing a fine, high-performance tremble, the kind usually reserved for small dogs and nervous Chihuahuas. I gripped my kneecaps to stabilize them and stared holes into the oak.

In my skull, something had gone very quiet and flat, the humming static of a man whose operating system has encountered a fatal error and is just displaying wallpaper until he deals with it.

*She looked at me.*

*Three seconds.*

*That is the exact duration of a player-character "noticed by heroine" event flag in the game engine. I timed it once. Three seconds.*

*No. No, it's a coincidence. Coincidences happen. One hard stare. That's all. Maybe I have something on my face. Maybe she memorizes the faces of commoners as a hobby. Maybe—*

The air in front of my eyes *chimed.*

Softly. Too softly. The chime of a small silver bell being rung inside my skull by a sadist.

And a window unfolded in the air between me and my desk, in shimmering cipher only I could see:

```
[SYSTEM ALERT]

[High-Priority Entity Detected: IRIS AURELIA]
[Route 4 Heroine — Difficulty Class: Nightmare]

[Status: This entity has taken note of you.]

[Reason: Unknown.]
[Reason: UNKNOWN.]
[Reason: UNKNOWN???]

[Bad End No. 12: 'The Tyrant's Curiosity']
[Proximity flag has been initialized.]
```

I stared.

The window, as if aware of my emotions, pulsed gentler, almost comforting, and appended:

```
[A Note From Your Sponsor:]

[The Evil Goddess would like to know,]
[and she is asking in her "calm voice,"]
[which has historically preceded the destruction of several minor religions:]

["You absolute moron."]

["What. Did. You. DO?"]
```

Under the desk, my hands stopped trembling.

Mostly because they'd gone numb.

*What did I do?* What did I *do?* I'd sat. In a chair. An empty chair. In the back. Next to the window. I was wearing the academy equivalent of a beige plastic bag. I'd said zero words, made zero eye contact, cast zero spells, breathed at regulation volumes. I had executed my plan to be *human background noise* with the cold, flawless perfection of a grandmaster.

And it had taken her literally three seconds to find me anyway.

*Route 4 heroine. Exactly like Route 1 heroine. Elara Voss appeared off-script and stared at me like a typo. Now the Imperial Doomsday Device in a school uniform walks in late and pins me like a butterfly.*

*Either the script has holes in it that the game never showed me...*

*...or there is something about me.*

The window pulsed, one more time, kindly, in the gentle lilac text of the goddess:

```
["Your 'staying unnoticed' success rate: still zero percent,"]
[she wants me to relay.]

["Also I have upgraded your memorial."]
["It now has a fountain."]
```

Kaelin droned on about dueling regulations. Outside the window, the sun shone on the doomed courtyard. Lucas Ashford sat in his seat, destiny humming. Iris Aurelia sat in hers, back straight, attention forward, all that catastrophic attention filed neatly away — *for now.*

Six hundred and thirty-five Bad Endings to go.

And the second day of school wasn't even halfway through.

'I'm going to die at this school,' I thought, with the quiet, fatalistic calm of a man being fitted for a coffin.

'At this rate, before lunch.'
