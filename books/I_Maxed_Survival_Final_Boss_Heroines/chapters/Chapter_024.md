# Chapter 24: Decryption Session #4: She Starts Asking Personal Questions

---

The door to Iris Aurelia's private study room opened with the soft, well-oiled precision of everything else in this girl's life.

I stepped inside.

And froze.

Something was wrong.

For three sessions, the layout had been identical. Table. Two chairs. My notebook, dead center between us, positioned with the geometric obsession of a lands surveyor. Candlelight. Silence. Interrogation.

A routine.

A pattern.

A *system.*

And if there's one thing 347 hours of speedrunning Eschaton Hearts taught me, it's this:

**When the pattern breaks, someone is about to die.**

But the notebook wasn't on the table.

In its place sat two plates.

Two plates of actual, honest-to-god food. Roasted chicken with herbs. Root vegetables glazed in something golden. Fresh bread that was still steaming, still *warm*, giving off a smell so illegal it should've required a noble permit.

My stomach made a sound that I can only describe as a war horn.

Iris Aurelia sat on the far side of the table, hands folded, watching me with those pale, unreadable eyes.

"Sit," she said.

I did not sit. Sitting was what you did right before the cutscene started.

"...Where's the notebook?"

"On the shelf." She nodded toward the wall. "We'll get to it."

'We'll get to it.'

Those four words hit my brain like a patch note that says *"rebalanced stealth mechanics"* — technically neutral, practically catastrophic.

I sat down.

Slowly.

Like a man lowering himself into a pit he has personally counted the spikes of.

---

"You've been eating bread rolls for every meal," Iris said.

I blinked.

"The provisional stipend is insufficient for proper nutrition," she continued, in the tone of someone reading a weather report. "Forty-two copper per week covers either adequate food or adequate supplies. Not both. You've chosen supplies."

My mouth opened.

Closed.

Opened again.

"...How do you know what I eat?"

"I observe."

'I observe.'

She said it the way other people say 'I breathe.' Like surveillance was a biological function. Like of *course* the Imperial Princess had a running spreadsheet on the caloric intake of a provisional student with a Mana Index of 0.009.

"That's—" I searched for a word. "—concerning."

"It's thorough."

"That's a synonym problem, not a defense."

The smallest pause.

Then — and I need the record to show I am not making this up — the corner of her mouth moved.

Maybe two millimeters.

For Iris Aurelia, that was the emotional equivalent of a standing ovation.

"Eat," she said. "Before it gets cold. Cold food is inefficient."

...

Ah, yes. Inefficiency. The true villain of this story.

---

Reader, I want you to understand something.

I had a survival plan. I had protocols. I had mental flowcharts for every contingency this academy could throw at me, from demon exposure to dragon sightings to accidentally making eye contact with a named character.

Nothing — *nothing* — in my planning accounted for being handed a plate of hot food.

I was so hungry.

The chicken was so warm.

My resistance lasted approximately the length of time it took to pick up the fork.

'This is fine,' I told myself, taking the first bite and nearly ascending to a higher plane of existence. 'This is just a meal. Meals aren't flags. People eat food all the time without triggering romance routes.'

This was, I would later reflect, the most naive thought ever produced by a human brain.

---

We ate in silence for a while.

And by "in silence," I mean Iris ate with surgical precision — each portion cut to identical size, each movement economical, like she was defusing the chicken — while I performed the delicate balancing act of eating fast enough to satisfy three weeks of bread-based suffering and slow enough not to look like a starved raccoon.

I failed.

I absolutely looked like a starved raccoon.

Iris watched me with the expression of a scientist observing a raccoon.

Then she said, without preamble:

"Do you sleep well?"

My fork paused halfway.

'Interrogation. This is interrogation. She's pivoting. New angle. Softening the subject through personal rapport — I read about this in a spy novel once. Or a wiki. One of those.'

"Define well," I said.

"More than five hours. Without waking."

"Then define 'well' as something else."

Her eyes narrowed a fraction. "How much do you sleep?"

"A reasonable amount."

"That's not a number."

"It's a philosophy."

'Four hours,' said the truth, screaming from inside a locked room in my skull. 'Four hours a night in a converted storage closet, on a mattress with the structural integrity of a prayer, waking up at 3 AM from nightmares about [Bad End No. 12: Ashes of the First Dawn] and [Bad End No. 29: The Hollow Crown] and my personal favorite, [Bad End No. 34: The Butcher's Banquet], which involved a lot of fire and exactly zero survivors.'

"I sleep fine," I said.

Iris looked at me.

It was the look of a woman who had catalogued seven distinct lies in my speech patterns over three sessions and was filing this one under *"obvious, flagrant, will revisit."*

She said nothing.

That was worse.

---

"Do you have friends?" she asked next.

Here we go.

"There's Finn," I said. "He's... loud."

"Loud is not a personality."

"You haven't met Finn."

"Fair."

'Wait, did she just— did the Imperial Princess just make a joke? At a conversational tempo? Is this allowed? Is there a permit?'

I shoveled in vegetables to buy time.

"Lucas Ashford seems to have taken an interest in you," Iris said.

My chewing slowed.

Of course she knew. Of *course* she knew about Lucas. The protagonist. Seat 4-C in Homeroom 1-A, golden-haired, sunlight-personified, walking social gravity well — already the most talked-about first-year at Stellaris Royal Academy after arriving on Day 2, already forming his canonical friend group right on schedule.

And somehow, inexplicably, deciding that I — the background-extra-coded man in Seat 7-F, back corner, by the window, spiritual camouflage incarnate — was worth befriending.

Which was the single most off-script thing happening in this entire world, and I did *not* want Iris Aurelia examining it too closely.

"He's just friendly," I said. "He's friendly to everyone."

"He is not friendly to everyone."

"He's friendly to almost everyone."

"He ignored an introduction from Duke Herzog's son. Politely. But completely."

'How does she know that?! That happened in a hallway! For thirty seconds! Does she have a network? A briefing? A morning newsletter — "The Lucen Gray Daily, Volume 18"?'

"He's just... social," I finished weakly.

"Mmm," said Iris, in the tone of a woman stamping a file **UNRESOLVED**.

---

Then:

"And Rhea Valtor?"

My fork stopped.

Actually stopped.

Mid-air, mid-bite, a piece of potato just hanging there like it was also waiting for an answer.

'Rhea Valtor. Dawn training. Five in the morning. The western training grounds past the old wall, the corner of campus so remote that the maintenance staff have never heard of it. Nobody goes there. Nobody *knows* about it. That's the entire reason we train there.'

'That was a covert meeting spot. I chose it specifically for its covertness. I ran a threat assessment on that location.'

'So how—

'—how—

The silence stretched.

Iris watched me with something that might, in a more expressive woman, have been amusement.

"My attendant Sera," she said, "is an early riser."

'Your attendant Sera is a *spy*, Iris. Let's use the precise terminology. Sera is an intelligence asset. Sera has a ghillie suit.'

"That's..." I set my fork down. "You've been having me watched."

"I observe," she said again, mildly.

"People. You observe *people.* With other people. That's not observation, that's an apparatus."

"Semantics."

"That's not semantics, that's the dictionary!"

She tilted her head, a few degrees. "You're upset."

"I am *calibrated.*"

"Why do you train with her at dawn? If it bothers you that I know, it must matter."

And there it was.

The pivot.

This wasn't casual conversation. This was a funnel — friendly questions at the wide end, and at the narrow end, the thing she actually wanted: *why does a boy with no mana train at 5 AM with the daughter of the most disgraced general in the Empire?*

The smart move was a lie.

A clean, small, forgettable lie.

"She's teaching me to survive long enough to run away," I said instead. "That's the whole arrangement. She's very good at fighting. I'm very good at needing to run."

Not a lie.

Not the whole truth.

A survivable truth — the kind I'd been living on.

Iris considered this for a long moment.

"The daughter of the executed general," she said quietly, "teaching a boy with no mana how to run away. In the dark. Before anyone wakes up."

"That's a dramatic phrasing of it."

"Is it inaccurate?"

"...It's dramatic."

Another flicker at the corner of her mouth.

I hated that I'd started measuring them.

---

And then Iris set down her fork.

Precisely. Aligned with the plate's edge. A full stop rendered in silverware.

"Lucen."

First name. No preamble. My spine informed me that we were now entering the boss phase.

"You're training with the daughter of the executed general."

One finger rose from the table. She started counting on her own hand, like laying out charges.

"You're being befriended by the highest-ranked student in our year."

Two.

"You broke a measuring crystal during the entrance assessment — a crystal rated for an Index of two million."

Three.

'It was a defect,' I did not say, because she would know I was lying, and also because I wasn't sure it was a defect.

"You read languages that the translation department has spent decades failing to decode."

Four.

"You knew the contents of a sealed restricted archive. You know passages under this academy that are not on any map."

Five.

"And you fight like a man expecting to die."

Her hand lowered.

She leaned forward, and the candlelight did something to her pale eyes that made them look less like ice and more like the sky an hour after sunset — still cold, still vast, but holding the last of the light.

"And you're sixteen years old," she said, "living in a storage closet, eating bread rolls."

The room went very quiet.

"Someone should be taking care of you."

---

...

...

I—

Huh.

'That's.'

'That's not in the script.'

I'd had lines ready. I had deflections prepared for accusations, evasions for deductions, strategic retreats for traps. I had rehearsed, in my head, at 3 AM on my miserable mattress, exactly what I'd say if Iris Aurelia ever cornered me about the notebook.

I had nothing prepared for *this.*

"Someone should be taking care of you."

When was the last time anyone had said something like that to me?

In this world: never. Not once. In this world I had been a provisional student, a statistical error, a survival project, a rat in the walls of Stellaris.

Before that—

—*Min-jun-ah, you sound tired. Are you eating actual meals? Not convenience store stuff, actual meals—*

Mom. Before I moved out. Before the calls got shorter. Before "I'm fine, Mom, I'm busy" became the entire conversation. Before Kang Min-jun, 23, unemployed, told himself he'd visit for the holidays and then ground a game for 70 hours instead because the game's families were simpler than real ones.

'Don't.'

'Do not do this. Not here. Not in front of the most dangerous woman in the Empire.'

My throat was tight.

My eyes were doing a thing.

Absolutely not. I refused. I was a grown man — mentally — and grown men do not get misty-eyed because a princess fed them chicken and said one (1) sincere sentence.

I looked down at my plate, so that my face could do whatever it was doing in private.

Get it together, Lucen.

She cannot know what she just did.

---

Across the table, Iris had gone very still.

I risked a glance up.

And for a moment — one heartbeat, maybe two — the mask was gone.

I don't know how else to describe it. Iris Aurelia's face was normally a sealed document: every expression authorized, reviewed, and filed. But right then, the seal was off, and underneath was something raw and strange and almost young.

She was looking at me — a boy who couldn't answer, whose throat was working around a sentence he couldn't get out — and she *recognized* it.

Not intellectually. Personally.

The way only one drowning person can recognize the exact splash pattern of another.

Then the mask slid back on. Smooth. Efficient. Like it had never been anywhere.

"The food deliveries will continue," she said, and her voice was back to census-taking cadence, crisp and final. "Twice a week, to begin. Sera will handle logistics."

"Iris—"

"This is not a request." She reached for the notebook on the shelf. "Consider it a condition of our arrangement."

"Our arrangement is about the notebook."

"The arrangement," she said, setting it before her and opening it with a scholar's reverence, "requires the decryptor to be alive. Your current lifestyle is a threat to the asset."

'Did she just call me an asset to my face so she wouldn't have to call me a friend?'

"...Did you just hide concern inside a logistics statement?"

"I made an efficient decision. Now." She turned the pages with care that bordered on tenderness. "Page seven."

And just like that, the interrogation resumed.

But the room didn't feel like an interrogation room anymore.

That was the problem.

That was exactly the problem.

---

Page seven took two hours.

I decoded a passage about something the old text called "the covenant of the first silence" — which sounded like exactly the sort of cheerful, definitely-not-apocalyptic material this notebook specialized in — while Iris took notes in her flawless handwriting and occasionally asked questions that were too sharp to be only academic.

When I finally stood to leave, she held out a wrapped cloth bundle.

"Leftovers," she said. "Waste is inefficient."

There it was again. The word she used as camouflage.

I took the bundle. It was still faintly warm.

"Goodnight, Lucen."

"...Goodnight, Iris."

I made it three steps down the corridor before the full weight of the evening landed on me like a collapsing ceiling.

'Okay.'

'Okay. Let's do the post-session threat assessment. Standard procedure.'

Bread surveillance. Sleep audits. Friend inventory. Food deliveries. "Someone should be taking care of you."

And me — sitting there, throat tight, three seconds from crying into aristocratic chicken.

'In the game, Iris's affection events were hard-gated. Trigger conditions: courage demonstrated in her defense. Kindness shown despite her hostility. Moments where the protagonist saw her as a person when the whole world treated her as a weapon.'

'I have done NONE of those things. I have been a frightened cryptid who stutters and takes detailed notes.'

And the sessions were getting—

God.

The sessions were getting *easier.*

Worse: I had spent the walk over here tonight not reciting my evasion protocols, but wondering if the study room would be warm like last time.

'I'm not surviving the Decryption Sessions anymore.'

'I'm starting to look forward to them.'

My feet stopped in the middle of the empty corridor.

That was, without exaggeration, without hyperbole, without a single gram of my usual dramatic self-indulgence —

— the most dangerous thought I had ever had since waking up in this world.

My blood went cold.

I had seen it. Once. Late in a completion run, buried in the datamine, flagged with warnings the developers had clearly never expected a player to reach.

No survivors wasn't the right description for that one.

The right description was *no world left to have survivors in.*

The window faded.

I stood alone in the dark corridor of Stellaris Royal Academy, holding a warm bundle of food given to me by a girl who might someday hold the sun hostage, if the wrong person ever made her cry.

"...

...The chicken was really good, though," I whispered to absolutely no one.

Somewhere far away, in an empty study room, Iris Aurelia sat alone with a notebook and realized, with the dawning horror of a general who has marched confidently into perfect terrain, that she was already looking forward to Friday.
