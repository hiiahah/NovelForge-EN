# Chapter 22: The Frozen Greenhouse (Alice Route — Entry Point)

I did not sleep.

Technically, I lay down.

Technically, my body performed the physical motions associated with rest: horizontal posture, closed eyes, blanket up to the chin like a corpse that had read about comfort in a pamphlet.

But every time my thoughts drifted, I heard it again.

Click.

The door closing behind me in Iris Aurelia’s private study.

Soft. Polite. Civilized.

A guillotine wearing perfume.

I stared at the ceiling until dawn painted the dormitory window gray.

Then I got up, washed my face, looked into the mirror, and gave myself the kind of pep talk only a former unemployed completionist could produce.

“Congratulations, Kang Min-jun,” I whispered to the boy who wasn’t quite me anymore. “Three hundred forty-seven hours in Eschaton Hearts. Every route mapped. Every death catalogued. Every heroine disarmed, romanced, or survived.”

The reflection stared back with dark circles under its eyes.

“And now you are collectible flora.”

Great.

Fantastic.

Ten out of ten character development.

Thursday arrived with the cheerful violence of a game event I had not selected.

Stellaris Royal Academy looked almost innocent in the morning light.

Lawns trimmed into geometric obedience. Towers catching sun like smug chess pieces. Students crossing the courtyards in clusters, laughing as if the world wasn’t one bad dialogue option away from peeling open.

Lucas Ashford was already somewhere on campus, doing protagonist things with protagonist hair and protagonist destiny, because of course he was.

Day 17.

The narrative was no longer clearing its throat.

It was saying my name.

I made it through morning lectures by reducing my existence to furniture.

Seat 7-F.

Back right corner.

Window to my left, escape route to my right, social interaction directly ahead and therefore geographically avoidable.

I took notes.

I did not make eye contact.

I did not answer questions unless the probability of death from silence exceeded the probability of death from speech.

Survival wasn’t glamorous.

Survival was spreadsheet logic disguised as cowardice.

Then afternoon Botany posted the assignment board, and my life once again proved that God had a sense of humor and I was the joke.

“Partner assignment?” someone groaned near the front.

“Mandatory greenhouse practicum,” the instructor said, tapping the board. “Catalog three species of cryo-flora from the Frozen Greenhouse. One specimen sketch, one resonance note, one handling-risk annotation. Pairs are fixed by provisional roster adjacency. No swaps.”

A line of students surged forward.

I stayed back.

Let the protagonists and side characters and tragically confident extras cluster first.

Let them absorb the initial aggro.

When the crowd thinned, I drifted toward the board like a room-temperature ghost and scanned the list.

Gray, Lucen —

My eyes stopped.

Not because my name looked strange in this world’s elegant script.

Because of the name printed beside it.

Rimehart, Celestia.

For approximately thirty seconds, I forgot how lungs worked.

The board kept existing.

The letters kept being letters.

Celestia Rimehart.

Alice.

“I was wondering when the universe would stop using tragedies,” I said under my breath, “and start using punctuation.”

No one heard me.

Good.

In Eschaton Hearts, Alice didn’t make noise when she entered a route.

That was the terrifying part.

No battlefield. No blood moon. No imperial decree signed in the tears of peasants.

Just a warm smile.

Cold fingers.

A hand reaching for yours because holding it felt natural.

And somewhere behind that hand, something ancient opening one eye.

---

The Frozen Greenhouse was attached to the western conservatories like a secret the building had accidentally grown.

From the outside, it looked delicate.

Frosted glass panes. Silver framing. A pale blue dome with condensation feathering across the surface in crystal veins.

Beautiful.

Also wrong.

Too quiet.

Too still.

Even the afternoon sun seemed to hit the glass and reconsider.

I stood outside the door, staring at the brass plaque.

FROZEN GREENHOUSE — AUTHORIZED PRACTICAL USE ONLY.

Beneath it, a smaller line:

Temperature regulated for cryo-flora. Do not tap glass. Do not sing to specimens. Do not accept gifts from unidentified roots.

I blinked.

“…Do not what?”

The plaque offered no clarification.

Of course it didn’t.

Stellaris Royal Academy loved rules that sounded unhinged until they saved your life.

I touched the handle.

Cold bit my palm.

Not normal cold.

Not metal-after-shadow cold.

This cold had intention.

It recognized me.

Or rather, it recognized the absence inside me.

Mana Index: 0.009 / 100,000.

Less magical than an enchanted wooden training dummy.

A hole shaped like a person.

The door opened before I could decide whether pushing it was brave or stupid.

Warm air did not rush out.

Nothing rushed.

The greenhouse simply allowed me in.

The smell hit first.

Ice. Wet stone. Crushed mint. Something sweet under it all, like vanilla left out in snow.

Then the light.

Blue-white, refracted through frosted glass and hanging prisms of ice.

Rows of plants rose from black soil beds and crystal planters: silver ferns with leaves like frozen fireworks, translucent moss that glowed when breathed on, pale orchids blooming inside geometric shells of ice.

Beautiful.

Absolutely lethal in approximately seven different timelines.

I knew this room.

Not from memory of standing here.

From maps. Event backgrounds. Route CGs. A late-game Alice scene where the camera panned too slowly across frozen petals while the music dropped all its drums and left only heartbeat.

A hidden Abyssal resonance point slept under this place.

Not dangerous if untouched.

Not active unless provoked.

A patch note written in architecture.

Alice was already inside.

Of course she was.

Kneeling among frost-orchids near the center aisle, surrounded by a ring of blooms that leaned toward her without wind.

Pale blue hair fell around her shoulders like melted glacier given morale.

Her eyes, when she looked up, were the color of lake ice under noon sun.

Her uniform sleeves were rolled to the elbow.

Tiny crystals formed and dissolved on her skin as if her body couldn’t decide whether to be jewelry or weather.

She was humming.

No melody I recognized.

No melody at all, maybe.

Just a bright, absent sound, the kind people make when the world has gone briefly merciful.

Then she saw me.

Her face lit up.

Not in the dangerous way.

Not visibly, anyway.

“Oh!” she said. “You must be my partner.”

My soul attempted to leave through the ceiling.

I kept it inside by force of habit.

She rose in one smooth motion, brushing soil from her knees, and crossed the aisle with quick, buoyant steps.

Too close.

Too fast.

Too ordinary.

She stopped in front of me and smiled so warmly the greenhouse should have lost structural integrity.

“Hi! I’m Celestia,” she said. “But everyone calls me Alice. Well, no one calls me anything because I haven’t really talked to anyone yet, but I want them to call me Alice. So—Alice. I’m Alice.”

She extended her hand.

Her fingers were cold even before I touched them.

Her smile was warm even after.

This was the part where a normal boy blushed.

This was the part where a normal boy thought, pretty girls don’t usually talk to me.

I had also been a normal boy once.

Then I had filled out a completion checklist for Apocalypse Girlfriend Simulator: Institution Edition.

I shook her hand.

Cold met skin.

A tiny crystal bloomed on my knuckle where we touched, no bigger than a tear.

It unfolded with impossible delicacy.

Five points.

A miniature star.

Then it melted into my skin and vanished.

Alice’s eyes widened in delight.

“Oh! It liked you.”

No.

No, no, no.

Objects in Eschaton Hearts did not “like” people unless hidden variables were being rolled behind the screen.

“Lucen Gray,” I managed.

“I know!”

She said it brightly.

Terribly.

“You’re the crystal-breaker. And the mystery language boy. And the guy who dodged Derrick Holt’s crazy attack. You’re kind of famous.”

Each title felt like a nail designed by midwits.

“I really wish I wasn’t.”

Alice laughed.

A real laugh.

Startled and clear, like ice cracking in spring to reveal running water underneath.

“I know exactly what you mean,” she said.

No.

You really don’t.

Unless you’ve ever had an empress decode your soul in real time and a dragon consider whether you taste better marinated.

I kept that behind my teeth.

She tilted her head.

“Should we start? I already tagged the easy ones, but Professor wants independent cataloging, so I promise not to cheat off your terrifying aura of competence.”

“I don’t have an aura.”

“That’s what makes it terrifying.”

I stared at her.

She grinned.

The frost-orchids rustled.

All at once.

Like applause from people who disapproved.

We worked.

That was the obscene part.

We simply worked.

Specimen one: A mirror-leaf fern whose fronds reflected not your face but your current emotional trauma.

Alice held the glass loupe. I noted resonance.

“Don’t look at it too long,” she warned cheerfully. “It gets needy.”

“It gets what?”

“Needy.” She turned the fern toward me. “If it thinks you’re lonely, it starts showing you the version of yourself that apologized better.”

I slowly moved my face out of frame.

“Botany is cancelled.”

Alice laughed again.

Specimen two: A candle-bloom that released spores shaped like tiny bells whenever someone lied nearby.

I stood very still and thought honest thoughts.

I am terrified.

I am hungry.

I miss cheap ramen and the ability to uninstall reality.

The plant remained quiet.

Alice leaned over my shoulder to read my notation.

Cold air came with her.

Vanilla and frost.

“Your handwriting is precise,” she said.

“I optimize under pressure.”

“Is that a compliment or a symptom?”

“Yes.”

She bumped her shoulder lightly against mine.

Friendly.

Casual.

Abyssal entity instincts presumably taking minutes.

Every touch made my nerves perform casualty projections.

Hand here: safe.

Shoulder here: safe.

Hair brushing sleeve: escalation possible.

Distance under thirty centimeters sustained: flag probability rising.

I hated that affection had become fieldcraft.

I hated more that some small, stupid part of me was relieved she was kind.

In Eschaton Hearts, kindness was never evidence of safety.

It was often the bait on the trap with the softest teeth.

Specimen three waited on the deep shelf.

Cryo-aster cluster, sealed behind a lattice of blue ice.

Rare. High handling-risk. Canon site of two Alice bad ends and one extremely cursed dating sim CG involving accidental hand-holding inside a crystalline cocoon.

Alice reached for the tongs.

I reached faster.

“I’ll stabilize the base,” I said. “You read the core.”

She glanced at me.

For a moment, something sharp passed behind her eyes.

Not suspicion.

Recognition.

Like a musician hearing another musician count rests correctly.

“Okay,” she said softly.

We moved together.

Bad idea.

Excellent coordination.

Terrible narrative planning.

I anchored the lattice with the insulated clamp while Alice leaned in, breath fogging, loupe raised. Her hair brushed my wrist. Cold sparks danced over my cuff.

“Core pattern is hexagonal,” she murmured. “Secondary rings unstable. It’s beautiful.”

“It’s a landmine with petals.”

“That’s also beautiful.”

“Please stop agreeing with danger.”

“I’m not agreeing.” Her voice softened. “I’m being polite.”

I looked at her.

She wasn’t joking.

That was worse.

Her fingers reached deeper than they should have.

Not recklessly.

Instinctively.

Like a pianist reaching for a note in a song her body remembered.

The cluster answered.

Light pulsed.

Bluewhite, then violet at the edges.

“Alice,” I said. “Pull back.”

“I almost have—”

The frost-crystal cluster flared.

No scream.

No explosion.

Just pattern.

Geometry unfolded around her hand in perfect silence.

A circle.

Three rings.

Nested angles locking into triangles, then seals, then script I could not read and absolutely recognized from late-game asset tear-downs.

Abyssal Circle.

Third-tier.

Containment class.

It existed for two seconds.

One.

Two.

Gone.

The greenhouse exhaled frost.

Alice yanked her hand back.

Color drained from her face so fast it looked edited out.

For an instant, the temperature spiked downward. My breath became a solid thing in my chest. Every ice crystal in the room turned toward her like iron filings remembering north.

Then it stopped.

Perfectly.

Obediently.

As if embarrassed.

“Sorry,” Alice said too quickly.

Too bright.

Too smiling.

“Sometimes my magic acts up in cold environments. It’s nothing.”

Nothing.

The word did push-ups in the air and died.

That circle had not been attack magic.

It had been a cage drawn by the prisoner.

A containment ward.

The Abyssal Witch suppressing itself.

Which meant the entity inside Alice was awake enough to need brakes.

Alice cradled her hand against her chest.

The skin between her thumb and forefinger shimmered, frost blooming in a starburst scar, then fading.

She forced a laugh.

“You should see me around soup,” she said. “Total disaster.”

I should have said something normal.

Something like, are you okay.

Something human-shaped.

Instead my mouth ran the route file before my fear could patch it.

“That ward wasn’t random,” I said.

Her smile stayed.

Her eyes didn’t.

For one breath, Alice looked at me the way a locked door looks at the sound of the correct key.

Then the brightness returned, sealed over the crack.

“Ward?” she said lightly. “You say the strangest things.”

I had done it again.

Talked too fast.

Too precise.

Too like a man standing over the design documents of reality.

Iris’s voice slid through memory, velvet over a blade.

You’re doing it again.

I swallowed.

“I read a lot,” I said.

Alice blinked.

Then she leaned closer.

Close enough that I could see individual frost crystals dissolving in the blue of her irises.

“Bad habit,” she whispered.

“Terminal,” I whispered back.

She smiled.

Not the bright public one.

Smaller.

Truer.

“Mine too,” she said.

The greenhouse lights dipped.

Somewhere under the floor, something old turned in its sleep.

Alice looked down before I could.

“Let’s finish the catalog,” she said.

“Right,” I said.

Neither of us mentioned the circle.

Complete lie.

Full marks.

She insisted on cleaning up.

Of course she did.

Kind people were always the most dangerous when they decided taking care of you was part of their personality.

We finished the assignment in record time because fear is an excellent project manager.

My notes were neat enough to qualify as scripture.

Alice’s sketches were disgustingly good—quick strokes, exact angles, tiny smiling faces drawn beside unsafe roots as if warning labels needed morale support.

At one point she reached across me for an empty vial and her sleeve brushed mine.

Static snapped cold between us.

A thread of abyssal pressure licked across my null field like a tongue tasting a glass wall.

I froze.

Alice noticed.

“Sorry,” she said softly.

That time, it sounded real.

I hated that.

Kind monsters should not be allowed to apologize convincingly.

When we stepped outside, afternoon had mellowed toward gold.

Normal students crossed normal paths under normal trees.

Birds did bird things without triggering flags.

A bell chimed somewhere, distant and academic.

It all felt obscene.

Alice shut the greenhouse door behind us and immediately linked her arm through mine.

I became furniture with panic.

“What,” I said eloquently.

“It’s cold,” she announced, pressing closer. “I’m using you for warmth. Don’t be weird about it.”

“You are made of winter.”

“Medical condition.”

“You just flash-frozen a botanical crime scene.”

“Then you should be honored I chose you as my emergency heater.”

I could have pulled away.

Should have.

Twelve separate survival protocols voted yes.

But pulling away would make it a thing.

And making it a thing would give it a shape.

And giving it a shape would give the ancient void behind her ribs a noun to hold.

So I walked.

About ice formations.

About how she liked greenhouse glass because it made the sun look honest.

A pulse.

Rhythmic.

Steady.

Like a heartbeat.

Not hers.
