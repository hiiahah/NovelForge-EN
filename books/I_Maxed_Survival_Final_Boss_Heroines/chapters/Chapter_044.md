# Chapter 44: Preparing for the Shadow Banquet

The envelope arrived with breakfast, which was rude, because nothing that ruins your life should have the courtesy to wait until you've had toast.

It was cream-colored. Embossed. The Stellaris Royal Academy crest sat in the corner looking expensive and judgmental, and my name was written across the front in handwriting so elegant it probably charged tuition.

'Oh no.'

Finn looked up from across the cafeteria table, porridge halfway to his mouth. "What's that? The fancy kind of mail?"

"The fancy kind of mail," I confirmed.

"Cool. Open it."

I opened it.

---

*Dear Lucen,*

*I've arranged for a special academic mentorship beginning next semester. I believe your unique abilities deserve focused guidance. I look forward to helping you reach your full potential.*

*Warmly,*
*Elara Voss*

---

I read it once.

I read it twice.

I read it a third time, the way you re-read the death recap screen after a boss one-shots you out of some mechanic you didn't know existed, searching for the frame where you could've dodged.

'Let's parse this, shall we. Speedrunner brain, engage.'

"Mentorship." One-on-one access to the school's only known zero-mana anomaly. Check.

"Unique abilities." Vice-Headmistress Elara Voss, whose official file I'd skimmed in a past-life wiki dive and whose *unofficial* file involved roughly four thousand forum posts titled **[THEORY] VOSS IS THE [REDACTED]]**, had correctly identified that I was not a normal student. Check.

"Reach my full potential." My full potential was currently *dying in escalatingly creative ways at the hands of four romance-target-turned-final-boss heroines.* Did she want to help me *reach* that or *facilitate* it? Unclear. Both readings were grammatically defensible.

"Warmly." No one dangerous ever signs anything "warmly." Warmly is the signature of a woman who collects mentees like skins and has a 100% completion rate.

I folded the letter, put it in my pocket, and ate my toast.

You don't make it through 347 hours of *Eschaton Hearts* without learning one fundamental survival skill, and it isn't combat.

It's compartmentalization.

One crisis at a time, sorted by threat level, with hard DPS checks.

And I had three.

---

**CRISIS ONE: THE DUEL.**

Rhea Valtor, Vice-President of the Disciplinary Committee, heir to a ducal military house, and the single person at Stellaris who had personally watched me fight enough times to build a data model of me in her head.

In game terms, Rhea was the Speedrun-Killer. She adapted. She studied. She was the only final boss in *Eschaton Hearts* who got *more* dangerous the more of your footage existed.

Every fighting style I had, she'd seen.

So I needed one she'd never seen anyone have.

Which brought me to the academy archive on Thursday afternoon, breathing paper dust in the restricted precollapse section, where the books were old enough to legally be seniors.

Three hours. Three hours of cross-referenced combat treatises, half of them rotting, a quarter of them written by people who believed mana was "the sky's opinion."

And then, wedged between a farming almanac and a cookbook that described roasting something illegal—

A slim, water-damaged folio.

***On the Empty Stance: Meditations of the Seventh Danaid.***

I opened it carefully, like disarming a trap.

*The swordsman who stands in a stance can be read. The swordsman who stands in nothing cannot be read, for there is nothing there to read. Hold no posture. Hold no pattern. Hold no home. The river does not return to the same bend. The wind does not rehearse.*

I stared at it.

'That's...'

*A master with no habit cannot be anticipated, for anticipation requires a pattern, and the Empty Stance is the disciplined refusal of pattern. It is not a technique. It is the absence of technique, held with intention.*

'That's literally fighting like a mana-void.'

Someone, centuries ago, had written a whole philosophy for it. Not because they couldn't use mana — the Danaids apparently swore it was a *deliberate ordering* — but philosophically, it was the same skeleton.

I don't have mana to channel.

I don't have patterns to exploit, because my entire combat approach is *frantic improvisation powered by panic and massive game knowledge.*

Someone had taken my survival style and made it a *martial art.*

"Beautiful," I whispered to the moldy folio. "You're ugly and there are water stains and the author was a lunatic. And you're beautiful."

I committed as much of it to memory as I could. Not techniques — there were barely any — but *concept*. The Empty Stance wasn't a thing you *did*. It was a thing you *refused: to be predictable, to be readable, to be a repeating data point in Rhea Valtor's beautiful lethal head.*

I'd have to be nonsense.

Luckily, 'being nonsense' was my single highest-leveled passive skill.

---

**CRISIS TWO: THE BANQUET.**

The Shadow Banquet. Thursday's school-wide flyer. Saturday night's formal event. Attendance mandatory for certain students.

Apparently my name was on the list, which was a bureaucratic war crime I'd be filing a complaint about never, since the complaint office would just be a polite way of asking Voss's network to learn more about me.

My strategy was elegant. Battle-tested. Optimized.

1. Arrive.
2. Stand near wall.
3. Maintain expression of *dignified commoner grateful to be included* for the minimum socially-required duration.
4. Watch Rhea duel (from a distance cardiovascularly safe).
5. Extract with zero incidents.

Five steps. Clean. Like a stealth section where you just go *past* the fight instead...

**CRISIS THREE: THE CLOTHES.**

"Formal attire," Finn had said, Thursday, with the crushing simplicity of a man who owned formal attire. "You have formal attire, right?"

I looked at my closet, which contained: three sets of the academy uniform, exercise clothes, one (1) extremely soft blanket Alice had once complimented, and a jacket with a hole in it that I had considered, in low moments, to be 'character.'

"Define 'formal.'"

"Oh no," Finn said.

Banquet invitation-level dress codes required *tailoring*. Custom noble formal wear cost more than— and here I did some quick conversion math— approximately a Korean student-budget monthly rice allowance times the number of meals I'd skipped this semester.

The Stellaris tailor on the high street looked at me, looked at my shoes, and quoted a figure that made my soul briefly exit through the ceiling.

So Friday evening, me and Finn went shopping off-grounds, into the artisan quarter of the lower town, where the secondhand shops smelled like cedar and history and other people's poor decisions.

And that's where we found it.

On a mannequin in the window of *Brewser's Finery (Pre-Owned & Preloved)*: a long formal coat in charcoal, high-collared, severe, and deeply, gloriously out of current fashion by about twenty years.

"This one," I said.

Finn tilted his head. "It's... big."

Just a little big. The shoulders landed wrong. The sleeves needed rolling. The waist could have accommodated a second, smaller me, standing in there for emotional support.

"It's distinguished," Finn decided.

"It's a crime against fabric," I said.

"It's on discount."

"Sold."

And here's the thing about tragedies: they sneak up on you.

Because when I tried it on in Brewser's cloudy mirror, rolling up the cuffs, straightening my posture the way you do in fitting rooms out of pure human reflex—

Finn went quiet.

"What," I said.

"Lucen."

"What's wrong? Is it worse than I thought? Be honest, I can take it."

"...No. Lucen. Look."

So I looked.

And I understood what had gone wrong.

The wrong size didn't read as *wrong size*. The long, hanging, severe lines of the coat read as *aesthetic choice* — on my frame, on me specifically, a months-underfed, overtrained, sleep-deprived commoner with sharp cheekbones, a gray stare, and shadows under his eyes that no amount of napping seemed to fully clear.

The bagginess read as restraint. The austerity read as intent. The dark circles read as *brooding*.

I looked like the penniless, mysterious transfer student from the game's own marketing art, except the marketing art hadn't known it was forecasting *me* and it should have been charged with a crime.

I looked like a protagonist.

The back at the dormitory, Saturday evening, stood in front of the closet's little square of mirror, and Finn, who had appointed himself my valet with no prior discussion, adjusted my collar like a man defusing a bomb made of silk.

"You look," Finn said, "like a dark horse prince from a romance novel."

"Please never say that ever again."

"The mysterious commoner who sweeps the princess off her feet—"

"Finn."

"Right. Shutting up."

He was about to give his opinion on my hair when there was a knock.

One knock. Deliberate. Not hurried.

'Everybody who knocks on this door is either a heroine, a conspiracy, or a delivery. Odds say all three.'

I opened it.

No one there.

My hallway, my closet-adjacent coffin of a hallway, was empty in both directions. Just lantern light and quiet and—

Four packages.

Lined up against the wall below my door. Neat. Unmarked. Untraceable. Waited like four cats that had arranged themselves to be noticed.

"That's not horrifying," Finn whispered.

"That's a physically manifested harem route flag," I said.

"What?"

"Nothing. Help me carry them in."

Each one was different. That was the scariest part. Four deliveries, one evening, zero notes on three of them, no coordination — four separate people had independently decided *today* to send me something.

Four separate people had been *thinking about me this week.*

Incorrigible. A romcom protagonist would find this heartwarming. A speedrunner recognizes a route convergence when he sees one.

**Package One.**

Plain paper, twine, militarily precise corners. Inside: a pair of dress boots. Combat-grade, black leather, supple, and *exactly my size*, not the size I'd written down, not the size on file — the size of my actual feet, measurable only to someone who had watched me walk and fight and move with the attention of a predator memorizing terrain.

No note.

'Rhea.'

Of course Rhea. Of *course* it was boots. *You're going to be on your feet in my presence. Be adequately equipped.* Practical. Unsentimental. Devastating.

**Package Two.**

A small jewelry box. Inside, two cufflinks: worked silver, each one holding a tiny crystal, cut and polished into a miniature formed ice crystal that caught the lamplight and fractured it into blue.

Pinned beneath, a note, in handwriting with neat tiny loops:

*"For luck! — A"*

Just that. The exclamation point. The initial. The cufflinks, which must have taken an agonizing amount of fine detail work to make.

I thought about a girl in a dawn kitchen, frosting four hundred cookies before anyone woke up.

'Oh, Alice.'

**Package Three.**

A velvet box this time, midnight-blue tissue, and inside, a formal cravat. Deep, deep blue — nearly black in shadow — with a single line of gold thread worked along the edge, subtle, expensive, the kind of detail you only notice when you're standing close.

No note either, but the outer wrapping had a seal: the Ashford household crest, second daughter's branch. Sera had delivered it. The gold thread, I realized with a sinking stomach, was exactly the shade of Iris Ashford's eyes.

A gift from a girl pretending she hadn't sent a gift, color-matched to her own gaze so I'd carry it into a crowd.

'That's not a cravat. That's a territorial flag made of silk.'

**Package Four.**

The smallest. No box. A twist of waxed paper, folded over once, sitting in my palm.

Inside: a single piece of golden butterscotch candy.

I actually laughed. Quiet, involuntary.

No note. No sizing to deduce. No message to decode. Just candy. Just Lyra, who operated on a logic system as relaxed and wander-loving as a cat in a sunbeam, who must have thought *banquet food is fancy and bad* and remedied it with the exact thing she always remedied everything with.

It was six copper coins of hard sugar and it hit me harder than the cufflinks.

Four gifts. Four routes. Four brightly-colored'alarm flags dressed as kindness.

And here came the calculation I hated, the one that tastes like ash every time:

I could refuse them. Take them back. Sender unknown — "I can't accept unknown gifts." Perfect excuse.

And then Rhea would register the refusal. Alice, small and frost-hearted, would see her cufflinks missing and quietly record the failure. Iris would note the empty collar. Lyra wouldn't say anything at all, in a way that would be worse than saying something.

Refusing any one of them was more dangerous than wearing all four.

Which meant—

I put on the boots.

I fastened the cufflinks.

I tied the cravat, gold thread against my collarbone.

I put the candy in my pocket, where I could feel the waxed paper through the lining.

Then I stood in front of the mirror, and looked at what four extremely dangerous final-boss romance heroines had made of me.

Charcoal coat. Black boots. Silver frost at my wrists. Midnight blue and gold at my throat. Butterscotch against my heart.

'I'm wearing four women's claims on me like armor,' I thought. 'And the worst part is, the fit's better than anything I've managed on my own.'
