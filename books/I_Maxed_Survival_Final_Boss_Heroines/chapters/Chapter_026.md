# Chapter 26: The Library Incident — Part 2: The Void and the Ice

The library was dying.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean the frost on the windows was spreading at a rate I could actually watch, like time-lapse footage of mold devouring bread, and the temperature was dropping with the steady, patient inevitability of a countdown timer.

Professor Maren had collapsed by the reference desk.

Hypothermia. Her lips were a color I'd previously associated exclusively with bad DLC skins. Finn — good old Finn, survivor's instinct intact — had dragged her behind the circulation counter, piled every cloak and coat in the room on top of her, and was rubbing her hands to keep circulation going.

"Lucen—!" Finn's voice cracked across the frozen hall. "The doors won't open! Something's— something's *wrong* with the air—!"

'Yeah, buddy. Something's wrong with the air. Great observation. Truly top-tier analysis.'

The three other students — first-years, by the build of them, kids who'd picked the absolute worst Saturday in academic history to catch up on reading — were huddled by the main entrance, taking turns slamming their shoulders against doors sealed by ice and System-mandated lockdown.

It wasn't working.

Obviously it wasn't working.

I'd read the lockdown description in the game. *"The Library seals itself to contain what stirs below. No exit until the resonance ends — one way or another."*

One way or another.

Fantastic phrasing, devs. Really comforting. Ten out of ten for ominous ambiguity.

And in the center of it all stood Alice.

She was glowing.

The containment circles orbiting her wrists and arms had become shaky, flickering things — like power grids failing neighborhood by neighborhood. Her hands were wreathed in layers of eldritch ice, forming and re-forming in geometric patterns too perfect to be natural. Her hair floated slightly, weightless, defying gravity with the casual confidence of a final boss.

Her eyes were the worst part.

Half ice-blue. Half void-black. Splitting and re-splitting like oil on water, like her soul couldn't decide which tenant was holding the lease.

"Lucen," she said, and the floor groaned under the harmonic in her voice. "I can't— it's pushing, Lucen, it's *pushing*—"

'Okay. Okay. Think. Think, think, think—'

My brain flipped through the game files like a panicking librarian — fitting, given the location — slapping open route guides, event flags, walkthrough wikis I'd memorized at 3 AM during my 347-hour completionist crusade.

Alice's Route. Chapter Event: *The Stirring Below*.

In the game, when Alice's entity resonates with the node beneath the library, Lucas Ashford — protagonist, golden boy, walking mana reactor — descends into Sub-Level 3 and channels his enormous mana reserve into the stabilization circuit built into the original architecture.

The builders knew about the node. They built the whole library as a containment apparatus. The stabilization circuit was basically a giant magical "shhh" button.

It required mana.

A *lot* of mana.

I checked my status window out of sheer masochistic habit.

```
[Mana Index: 0.009 / 100,000]
```

'Right. Cool. Amazing. I have the magical output of a decorative candle. I couldn't power the stabilization circuit if I melted my entire body down and poured myself into the slot.'

But—

'Wait.'

'Wait, wait, wait.'

Something had been nagging me since the resonance started. Since the moment that deep, contented *note* bloomed in my bones.

The node wasn't just waking up.

It was *resonating*. I'd felt it — that weird gravitational tug, that sense of being *noticed* by something vast and ancient. And in the game lore, what does the Abyss resonate with?

Absence. Void. The space between things.

In my very first playthrough, I'd read a throwaway lore book in Sub-Level 3 — *Treatise on Abyssal Resonance* — that said the entity amplifies whatever it touches. Feed it mana, and it becomes a bomb of mana. Feed it emotion, and it becomes a storm.

Feed it *nothing*...

'I'm not a battery. I'm a black hole. I'm not a power source — I'm a power *sink*.'

The anti-mana. The human loophole. A void-signature so complete that even the System struggled to see me.

If I touched the node directly, there was a chance — a completely theoretical, untested, statistically suicidal chance — that my nothingness would act as a dampening field. The node would reach for something to amplify and find... an empty room.

Theory.

Completely untested.

Probably lethal.

"*HOW?!*"

I didn't wait for his response. I crossed the frozen floor to Alice, took her cold, glowing, apocalyptically dangerous hand, and looked up into her split-colored eyes.

"Alice. I need you to come with me."

"...Down?" she whispered. Both of her voices said it. The girl and the thing wearing the girl like a winter coat.

"Down."

"Lucen, if I go near it, it'll— I can feel it *calling*—"

"I know. That's why you're coming." I squeezed her hand. "Your circles are the only light source we've got, and I can't see in the dark. So either you come with me, or I trip down three flights of ice stairs and die of embarrassment before I die of the Abyss."

A flicker.

Just for a second — a tiny, staggered beat — the blue won. Alice, actual Alice, stared at me like I'd grown a second head.

"You're joking," she said. "*Now*? You're joking *now*?"

"I joke when I'm scared. I'm *extremely* funny at funerals."

Something that might have been a laugh cracked out of her. It came out as frost on my collar.

"Okay," the layered voice said. "Okay. Don't let go."

"Wasn't planning on it."

---

Sub-Level 1 was a freezer.

Sub-Level 2 was an ice sculpture showroom curated by something with no concept of mercy.

The stairs were coated in half an inch of glassy frost, and I — a man with the agility stat of damp laundry — slipped almost immediately.

Twice.

The first time, I caught the railing and only bruised my pride.

The second time, my feet went out completely, and Alice caught me.

One-handed.

By the collar.

Effortlessly. Mana-reinforced strength, the casual physics-breaking grip of someone whose personal stat sheet had commas in it. She lifted me back onto the step like a cat relocating a kitten, and her fingers were so cold they *burned* — that deep, brand-level cold that feels hot until your nerves figure out the scam.

"Thank you," I wheezed.

"Walk better," said two voices at once.

'Great. Even the ancient horror in her voice is disappointed in my mobility build.'

We descended.

The cold deepened with every step, but it was a strange cold — not dead, not still. *Breathing*. In and out, slow and vast, synced to the pulse of light now shining up from below like a heartbeat made visible.

"Where are we going?" Alice asked.

"Somewhere I can help."

"How do you know there's somewhere down here that can help?"

'Excellent question. Devastating question. A question with the raw destructive power of a siege weapon.'

"I read about it."

"In what book?"

"A really, really thorough one."

"...Lucen."

"Extremely thorough. Like, suspiciously thorough. The kind of thorough where you suspect the author made some questionable life choices to obtain the information."

That last part was technically true. The author was the dev team, and the questionable life choice was making this cursed game.

We reached the bottom.

Sub-Level 3.

---

It was a circular chamber.

Big. Far bigger than the library above should have allowed, which told me immediately that the laws of architecture had given up down here centuries ago. The walls were old stone — *original* stone, the stuff from before Stellaris Royal Academy was Stellaris Royal Academy, carved with stabilization runes that had gone dark one by one over the centuries like lights in an abandoned city.

And in the center of the room stood the node.

A pillar of crystallized Abyssal energy, floor to ceiling, three meters wide. It didn't glow so much as *insist* — a deep, slow, radiating pulse of blue-black light that pushed through my eyes and rang directly in the hollow spaces of my skull.

Frost spread from its base in geometric patterns.

Perfect, intricate, fractal patterns.

I looked at the frost on the floor.

I looked at the circles orbiting Alice's arms.

They matched.

Exactly.

Alice saw it at the same moment I did. She made a small, horrible sound — not fear, recognition. The sound of someone finding the other half of a scar.

"That's—" Her voice shook. Both voices shook. "That's what's inside me. That's the same frequency. Lucen, that's the *same song*—"

"I know."

"How do you *know*?!"

'Because I cleared your route thirty-one times and watched you die in fourteen of them, and I read every lore book in this basement because I am a completionist gremlin with no girlfriend and too much free time.'

"Lucky guess," I said.

"That's not a lucky guess, that's a *forensic* guess—"

"Alice. I need you to stay here for a second."

I let go of her hand.

She grabbed for me immediately — fast, desperate, circles flaring — but I stepped back, raising both palms.

"Three minutes. Give me three minutes. If it goes wrong, freeze the stairs behind me so it can't spread upward, and get everyone out through the steam vents on Sub-Level 1 — the lockdown's focus is on the doors, not the maintenance channels."

She stared at me.

"How," she said slowly, "do you know about the *steam vents*?"

"Same book."

"*Lucen—*"

"Three minutes."

I turned around and walked toward the node.

The first step was fine.

The second step, the cold tripled.

The third step, I felt it — the thing I'd been dreading and banking on simultaneously.

*Pull.*

The node reached into me. Not physically — there was no hand, no hook, nothing so crude. It was more like standing on the edge of a cliff and feeling gravity lean forward in interest. Something vast pressed its attention against my chest and squeezed, looking for the mana it expected to find, ready to drink, ready to amplify, ready to turn a mage into a bomb.

It found 0.009.

It pulled again.

0.009.

Again.

0.009.

'That's right, you eldritch parasite. Dig all you want. The well is empty. The pantry is bare. You have broken into a house with no furniture.'

A magnet pressed against wood.

A fisherman hauling up an empty net, over and over, growing — if an ancient crystallized abyssal resonance node could be said to have emotions — *confused*.

I reached the pillar.

Up close, it was beautiful in the way that deep ocean trenches and nuclear fireballs are beautiful — aesthetically flawless, lethally indifferent. Something moved inside the crystal. It had a shape in the same way a nightmare has a shape: absolutely, the moment you looked away.

'Here goes everything.'

I put both hands on the node.

---

The effect was immediate.

Cold punched up my arms like a voltage spike gone wrong. The node's pulse — that deep, contented, world-creaking rhythm that had been building toward crescendo for the last ten minutes — *hit me*.

And stuttered.

My void-signature flooded into the resonance circuit like water down a drain. The node had been amplifying, feeding, building pressure against its failing seal like a tidal wave rearing back—

—and the wave hit an absence.

A hole in the world shaped exactly like Kang Min-jun's unemployable, zero-mana, 347-hours-wasted-on-an-otome-game soul.

The rhythm *broke*.

The pulse that had been coming every four seconds came at five. Then seven. The deep note in my bones wavered, like a singer who'd forgotten the next word.

The frost on the walls stopped spreading.

I watched a tendril of ice actually halt mid-creep across the floor, six inches from Alice's shoe, and just... sit there. Confused.

'IT'S WORKING.'

'IT'S ACTUALLY WORKING, YOU BEAUTIFUL, WORTHLESS NOTHING-BODY.'

Behind me, Alice made a sound like a dropped glass.

"The glow— Lucen, it's *receding*— my head, I can hear myself think again, what did you— what are you—"

"Dampening it," I gritted out.

Because here was the thing — the fun little asterisk that the game lore had buried in a footnote of a footnote.

The node wasn't *beaten*.

It was confused. My void was choking its rhythm, yes, but I was *small*. I was so small. I was a single human-shaped loophole pressed against something the size of a cathedral, and I could feel it probing, adapting, feeling around my absence like fingers searching a keyhole.

It would cycle down on its own — the lore said a fully interrupted node required time to settle back into dormancy. The question was whether I could hold it long enough.

I knew the number from the game.

Thirty minutes.

Thirty minutes of physical contact with an Abyssal resonance node that was actively trying to extract mana from a well that had never held water.

"Alice," I said through my teeth. "It's working. But I have to hold it. Thirty minutes."

"Thirty— Lucen, your hands—"

I looked down.

My hands, pressed flat against the crystal, were turning white. Not frostbitten white — *drained* white, bloodless, wax-pale, like the color was being politely escorted out of my skin.

Every second of contact felt like holding my breath underwater.

Not pain. That's the thing about void stuff, about the anti-mana nature of my whole cursed existence. It didn't hurt. It was worse than pain. It was the *sensation of being slowly emptied*. Like the node was sandblasting away at the thin film of Something that separated me from Nothing, grain by grain.

'Cool. Cool cool cool. I'm a human plug in the dam of an eldritch apocalypse, and I'm rated for maybe ten percent of the load.'

"Lucen." Alice's voice again, closer. "I'm going to help."

"Bad idea— you touch this thing, the entity could—"

"The entity is *quiet* right now for the first time in my entire life," she said. "Because of you. So don't you *dare* tell me it's a bad idea."

She sat down beside me in the frost.

Cross-legged. Settled. The way someone sits down next to you on a long train ride, like this was just a place to be.

And she placed her hands over mine.

On the node.

Her containment circles spun down her wrists, between her fingers, and sank into the crystal around our touch — layer after layer of her perfect geometric ice, wrapping my ragged little void-dampening field in a structure, a scafold, giving my nothing something to lean on.

The node's pulse faltered again.

So we sat in the dark together.

Hands intertwined on a pillar of ancient evil.

Her ice and my void, holding something terrible at bay, while somewhere above us a professor thawed out and three terrified first-years and one panicking Finn waited for the library to decide whether it was a building or a tomb.

The node's light pulsed slower. Slower. Each beat dimmer, heavier, sleepier. Like a heart winding down into hibernation.

The chamber, lit only by dying Abyssal glow and Alice's fading circles, was almost peaceful.

If you squinted.

If you ignored the thing moving inside the crystal.

"Lucen," Alice said softly.

"Yeah?"

"You're shaking."

I was. Had been for a while. Full-body, fine-tremor shaking — part cold, part adrenaline crash, part the ongoing spiritual experience of being French-kissed by the concept of oblivion.

"I know."

"You're really brave."

"I'm really terrified."

She laughed.

A small sound. Watery. Cracked at the edges. Completely, unmistakably *hers* — no second voice, no harmonic, no eldritch undertone. Just a sixteen-year-old girl laughing because the alternative was screaming.

"Same thing," she said.

The silence that followed wasn't awkward. It was the earned kind. The kind of silence two people get after standing in front of the abyss together and discovering the abyss needed a tap on the shoulder and a firm *no*.

Then:

"Can I tell you a secret?" she said.

Every speedrunner instinct in my body fired at once. Flag. Event flag. Relationship-critical dialogue node.

"You probably shouldn't."

"There's something inside me."

The light pulsed once, deep and slow.

"Something old," she whispered. "And hungry. It's been there since I was born. My family— they knew. Of course they knew. The circles were never for training. They were always a cage." Her voice stayed very level, which was somehow worse than if she'd cried. "I've never told anyone. Not Mother. Not the professors. Anyone who found out would— the Empire has *protocols* for people like me, Lucen."

She turned her head.

In the dark, her eyes were fully blue again. Just blue. Just a girl.

"You're the only one who's ever touched it," she said, "and not pulled away."

'I know.'

'I know about the entity, Alice. I know about your family's Cold Chain Protocols. I know about the containment circles you've worn since you were four. I know about the night you froze the nursery and nobody ever talked about it again. I know your favorite food is honey cake and you draw hearts in textbook margins and in Bad End 41 you freeze the entire northern continent because nobody ever once said it was okay.'

'I've known since before I ever met you.'

"I know," I said.

She went completely still.

"...How?"

Careful. Careful. This was the single most carefully scripted dialogue node in her entire route. In the game, Lucas says some golden-boy protagonist line about trusting her. It takes him until mid-volume-two to earn this conversation.

I skipped the script.

"Because I can feel it," I said.

True. My void-signature had been reading her double-layered existence since the moment my hands touched the node — the girl-frequency and the thing-frequency, braided.

"And it's okay," I said. "You're okay. We're going to be okay."

I had zero basis for saying this.

Statistically, we were surrounded on all sides by Bad Ends. My remaining lifespan was a betting market and the house always wins.

But it was the right thing to say.

And sometimes — rarely, at the exact worst moments of my life — I manage to say the right thing.

Alice started crying.

Very quietly. No sobbing, no shaking. Just tears, tracking down her cheeks in absolute silence, and each one crystallized into a tiny crown of ice before it could fall, so that by the node's slow-breathing light she looked like she was wearing a mask of frozen diamonds.

The most dangerous girl in the world.

Crying because someone told her she was okay.

'This game, man.'

'This stupid, beautiful, murderous game.'

I kept my hands on the node. I couldn't hold hers — they were both occupied holding the apocalypse in a headlock — so I leaned my shoulder against hers and stayed there, and we watched the light pulse slower and slower together.

Twenty-two minutes, sitting in the dark, hands frozen to a pillar of ancient evil, shoulder to shoulder with a girl who wore the end of the world under her skin.

The node wasn't just going dormant.

It was *listening*.

The way a sleeper listens when someone quietly enters the room. The crystal's inner light bent — subtly, constantly — toward the exact point where my hands touched it. It couldn't see me. It couldn't drink me. It couldn't even understand what I was.

And it wanted to.

Very much.
