# Chapter 9: The Notebook Incident

There are three stages of grief when you transmigrate into a game.

Denial. Anger. And the library.

The library is where you go when denial fails and anger costs too many calories. It's quiet, it has corners that nobody patrols, and — most importantly — it has tables wide enough to hide a notebook recipe for your own survival.

That's where I was on the evening of day four.

Corner desk. Second floor. Wedged between two bookshelves so thick with dust they qualified as archaeological strata. On my left: *A Comparative History of Pre-Collapse Monetary Policy, Vol. VII*, which had clearly never been opened by any human being, ever.

On my right: the exit.

Always know your exits.

That's not game knowledge. That's just being a Korean office worker who's survived too many team dinners.

I flipped open my notebook and uncapped the pen.

The notebook was a small, leather-bound thing I'd bought from the academy supply shop for four copper. Cheap paper, mediocre binding, and pages that currently held the single most dangerous document in the empire.

Not because of what it was.

Because of who it was about.

*'Iris Aurelia — Route 1: The Tyrant of Obsidian Flame.* DO NOT ENGAGE. *Will literally burn everything. No, seriously. In 34 endings, she burns the capital in 12 of them, burns the academy in 9, and in Bad End No. 7 she burns the PLAYER—'*

I scratched that last line out, because reading it back made my hands sweat, and wrote below it in Korean:

*Day 4 update:*

*Combat assessment deviated from plot. Instructor Vance matched me against Erik Holt, but I had to use the Ashenveil Retreat to keep my ribs intact—and Rhea Valtor was watching from the bleachers. She recognized the footwork. I got caught using the Ashenveil Retreat — footwork from a disciplinary technique from a dead commander whose daughter now thinks I'm a clue to her father's treason case. Great. Fantastic. Ten out of ten transmigration experience.*

*Bruised ribs. Minor. Healing cleanly.*

*Total active heroine flags: 2.*

*Iris Aurelia (Route 1): proximity warm. She watched the whole match. Didn't blink.*

*Rhea Valtor (Route 3): proximity warm. She watched my feet. Everyone should have been watching my face. She watched my feet.*

*Conclusions: I'm going to die.*

*Deeper conclusions: I might die while also being late for the ethics lecture tomorrow, which would be embarrassing and on-brand.*

I chewed the end of the pen and thought about it.

*Do I put in the 'If you're reading this, please bury me with my notebook' clause?*

*That's dramatic. I'm not dramatic.*

*I'm pragmatic.*

*There's a difference.*

I'll admit it. There's a certain rhythm to writing in Hangul that calms you down. It's like building a sandcastle while holding the flood back with tea.

Then a voice, three feet away, said flatly:

"That's not Pre-Collapse Runic."

My heart didn't just stop.

My heart quit the position.

My heart left a resignation letter on HR's desk and moved out of state with its stuff.

Every survival instinct I had, honed across forty playthroughs of a yandere dating sim with lethal consequences, screamed at me in perfect unison:

*'WHY DID YOU NOT HEAR HER?!'*

I turned my head slowly.

Slowly, because if I turned too fast, she'd know I knew she was there.

Slowly, because the alternative was acknowledging that a five-foot-eight walking natural disaster — the First Princess of the Aurelian Empire, the Tyrant of the Obsidian Flame, the final boss of Route 1 — was standing inside my personal bubble like a rumor that had arrived three years early.

Iris Aurelia.

Evening light through the tall window behind her turned her hair into something that looked less like platinum and more like a sunset rendered by a vengeful god. Her posture was utterly straight, utterly idle, and utterly terrifying.

She was looking at my notebook.

She had been standing there.

'How long—'

'How long has she been reading over—'

My brain went into hard-reset mode.

*'No no no no no—'*

*'Breathe, you absolute potato! BREATHE!'*

I breathed.

She continued, voice coolly academic, the way a scholar dissects a frog:

"It's similar in brushstroke economy. The shapes are compact, the stroke order appears consistent. But the character set is entirely different from Runic. The syntax structure suggests a phonetic alphabet, not ideographic."

Her eyes flicked along the page.

"This is a complete, functional language. I've never encountered it."

She said 'never encountered it' the way a chess master says 'I've never seen this opening' — with the mild irritation of someone who'd just discovered a new continent and hadn't been consulted first.

'She's—'

'She's already analyzed it.'

'She analyzed my handwriting while I was writing a paragraph about how I'm going to die.'

'In the time it took me to write three sentences about my own funeral, she's already worked out the structure, syntax family, and alphabet class of a script she's never seen before.'

'WHO DOES THAT?'

'That is not a human. That is a post-graduate degree with a body count.'

I tried to close the notebook.

Calmly. Casually.

The way you'd close a door on a neighbor you don't like.

"It's just personal shorthand, Your Highness—"

Her hand came down on the notebook.

Not hard.

Not aggressive.

Just... firm.

The way you'd pin a butterfly to a board before labeling it.

Iris did not look at me as she spoke. She looked at the page like it was a puzzle and she was deciding which piece to eat first.

"Personal shorthand doesn't have consistent grammatical particles and a base-fourteen syllable block structure. That's a language. You either invented it — which would make you a linguistic genius — or you learned it somewhere. Either answer interests me."

My 0.009 mana pool sent up a single, symbolic spark in protest.

It did nothing.

She lifted the notebook.

'I could—'

*No. You cannot.*

She was mana-reinforced, combat-trained, and a princess of the most powerful military empire on the continent. I was a boy with bruised ribs and a mana pool so small that a candle could outcast me. If I'd tried to grab it back, I'd have been composted into the library's rare-book collection inside four seconds, and the *Pre-Collapse Monetary Policy, Vol. VII* would have finally seen action as the thing that fell on me.

So I sat there.

Very still.

Very calm.

Completely internally screaming.

She turned the pages.

One. Two. Three.

Her brow furrowed — a tiny, elegant line forming between her eyebrows like a crack appearing in a perfectly frozen lake.

I watched her eyes move. Not randomly. Tracking. Sweeping left to right in rows, then doubling back. She was doing frequency analysis. Searching for repeated character clusters. Comparing spacing patterns between adjacent pages. Looking for the kind of repetition that betrayed a hidden meaning.

'She's terrifying.'

'She's not even taking notes. She's doing this in real-time. Her RAM is insane—'

'She's trying to decode it. Right here. Right now. In my library. At my desk. With my notebook full of her psychological profile.'

One of her fingers tapped the page once.

"A repeated glyph cluster," she murmured. "Here. Here. And here."

"The spacing suggests compound nouns. There are particles appearing after verbs that look like case markers. And—" her eyes narrowed, "—there's marginalia. You're annotating your own writing."

I did not say anything.

I was a stone. A stupid stone. A stupid, doomed, planning-the-funeral stone.

Then, after what felt like the longest thirty seconds of my transmigrated life, she closed the notebook, lowered it to her side, and looked at me.

Directly.

Tyrant eyes.

The kind that had, in twelve different endings, watched cities burn from a throne room balcony like it was a fireworks show.

"I can't read this," she said.

And there was something in how she said it — clipped, precise, teeth-gritted — that made it very clear this admission physically cost her something. Iris Aurelia did not enjoy being unable to do things. Iris Aurelia considered inability a personal insult issued by reality.

"Not yet."

'Not yet.'

'Not yet, she said.'

'That means she's GOING to.'

I watched her hold my notebook — my survival manual, my psychological-profile compendium, my 'who to run from and why' index — in one manicured princess hand, and felt the cold weight of the situation settle fully across my shoulders.

'She has it.'

'She has my notebook.'

'She's going to figure it out. Eventually.'

'Or worse: she's going to give it to someone who can.'

Iris tilted her head a fraction. A studied, predatory motion.

"You have two options, Lucen Gray."

She said my name like she'd read it off a dossier. Which, given who her father was, she probably had.

"First: I give this notebook to the Imperial Intelligence Bureau's cipher division. They'll have a preliminary analysis within a week and a full decryption within a month. Given your... anomalous enrollment circumstances, this will likely result in an investigation."

She let the silence stretch for exactly one beat.

"You may or may not survive that."

*'May or may not.'*

*'She said 'may or may not' like a waitress asking if I want the check or the dessert menu.'*

I made a small, dry sound in my throat.

My mouth felt like someone had replaced my saliva with library dust, which, to be fair, was probably accurate given my surroundings, given the bookshelves older than many nations.

"Second option," she continued, with the air of someone moving an unbeatable piece across a chessboard.

"You meet with me. Privately. Twice a week. You translate this notebook for me, section by section. If I'm satisfied that it contains nothing treasonous, I return it when we're done."

She let that sink in.

"No Intelligence Bureau. No investigation."

'Oh no.'

'Oh no OH NO—'

'This is a forced weekly check-in with the Tyrant.'

'Regular, private, closed-door sessions with the final boss of Route 1.'

'The woman who in one ending disemboweled a conspirator with her bare hands, wiped them on silk, and said 'Next' without changing tone.'

'Twice a week.'

'Indefinitely.'

'Until she's satisfied my notebook isn't treasonous.'

'My notebook, which contains, among other things, a detailed breakdown of her own psychology, her trauma, her triggers, her hidden weaknesses, and at least one annotated flowchart labeled 'HOW IRIS AURELIA FALLS IN LOVE — DO NOT REPLICATE.'

'Twice a week.'

'Sitting across from her.'

'Telling her lies.'

'Lies I have to invent, on the spot, about a language she cannot read.'

'Lies to a person whose mana intuition pings silver when someone breathes wrong near her.'

'This is the worst deal in the history of deals. Historians will study this deal. There will be a chapter on this deal in textbooks about bad decisions. There will be a 'Before/After' comparison image of me in memorial pamphlets.'

'...And it's still better than the Intelligence Bureau.'

'Because if the Intelligence Bureau gets my notebook, they won't just decode it.'

'They'll decode *me*.'

'The notebook is the only proof I exist that no one else can cross-reference. If a professional cipher team gets it and cross-references it with my scholarship application, my enrollment records, my combat record—'

'—the idea that some scrawny first-year scholarship student 'invented' a complete, functional, non-existent language out of nowhere—'

'—they'll know.'

'Not what. But they'll know *something* is wrong.'

'And wrong, in the Empire, gets you disappeared.'

I exhaled slowly through my nose.

"...Option two," I said.

Something flickered across her face.

Something fast.

Something I couldn't read, and that worried me, because I had forty hours of saved Iris-route data burned into the back of my brain and I *still* couldn't read it.

Then it was gone, replaced by the calm, cool expression of a princess who had just acquired a new asset.

"Tuesdays and Fridays," she said. "My private study room. Seven o'clock."

She tucked my notebook under her arm like it was a library rental.

"I'll keep this safe in the meantime."

'Safe.'

'SAFE.'

*It's mine. Give it back. That's mine. I wrote that. That's personal. That has my marginalia—*

"You will come prepared," she added. "Prepared students are useful. Unprepared students are paperwork."

She turned.

She walked away with the same unbothered elegance of someone who had just requisitioned a supply wagon instead of hijacking a stranger's entire survival strategy.

The library doors clicked shut behind her.

Somewhere near the entrance, I heard the faint sound of a maid — Sera, had to be, Iris never traveled unguarded even in spaces considered 'safe' — murmuring something, and Iris responding in the clipped, assured tone of someone whose day had gone exactly as planned.

Then silence.

The real kind.

The kind that settles in the pit of your stomach like lead.

I sat at my desk, between the *Monetary Policy Vol. VII* and the exit, and stared at the space where my notebook had been.

Then the system windows started blooming.

Four days.

Four days to prepare to lie to a human lie detector twice a week, forever, about a notebook that contained, among other things, a detailed breakdown of my own contempt for her court etiquette, my list of her 'top 5 red flags,' and the line 'smiles like a cat that found the canary, acts like a cat that ate the canary,' written at 3 a.m. when I was very tired and very honest.

I put my face in my hands.

The next window slid open with the gentle, smug elegance of a toast at a wedding.

I stayed like that for a long time. Face in hands. Desk between me and the floor. Dusty financial textbook watching from the side like a disinterested funeral attendant.

Eventually, I lowered them.

Two flags. Two heroines. One stolen notebook. One scheduled lie-fest.

Fourteen was shaping up to be quite the day.

I picked up my pen.

I couldn't write my notes in the notebook anymore, because the notebook was now in hostile princess hands. So I did the next best thing.

I tore a blank page out of the *Pre-Collapse Monetary Policy, Vol. VII* — the first time anyone had touched that book in recorded history — and began, in careful, tiny Korean, a new list.

*Day 4. New rules.*

*Rule 1: The notebook is gone. Accept this. Adapt.*

*Rule 2: Twice a week, Tuesday and Friday, you will sit across from the Tyrant and lie to her face. Control your breathing. Control your pulse. Do not think about the route where she proposed to the player character in a burning council chamber. Do not think about— actually, just don't think about *anything* from Route 1.*

*Rule 3: She will try to provoke genuine reactions. Trained interrogators do. Genuine reactions are your enemy. Be boring. Be neutral. Be a rock.*

*Rule 4: If she ever actually decodes the notebook... there is no Rule 4.*

I looked at that last line for a long time.

Then I crumpled the page, and ate it.

Yes.

I ate it.

Because leaving written evidence had already gotten me into this mess, and at this point, paper was a co-conspirator.

The library candles burned lower.

Outside, evening bells rolled across the academy grounds, deep and slow, announcing the hour like a countdown.

Tuesday.

Friday.

Seven o'clock.

Her study.

'Four days.'

'Four days to build a mask thick enough to fool a Tyrant.'

'Four days to remember every lie I'm going to tell.'

'Four days to stop thinking about the fact that the first time she'll hold my notebook in her hand, looking at me across a private table, asking "what does this mean?" — how many seconds will it take for her to notice I flinch wrong?'

'Or—'

'—worse—'

'—that I don't.'

I stood up.

I walked out of the library.

Past the desk, past the shelf, past the posted rules about silence and candle use and food.

Rule five, I amended mentally, because apparently I was now keeping rules in a language no one could read, of the boy who had been trying his best to stay off the board since he'd woken up in this world, and who had just been made into a permanent piece.

*Dodge everything, except flags.*

*But when the flag catches you anyway—*

*Lie beautifully.*

Outside, the night air was cold.

Somewhere in the Imperial wing, in a private study, a princess who had never encountered an uncrackable code was probably already sitting at a desk with candles burning, my notebook open in front of her, reading my Korean handwriting with eyes the color of winter monarchs, believing with absolute certainty that she was a few weeks away from knowing all my secrets.

She was not.

But she thought she was.

And for reasons I refused to examine too closely, the gap between those two things scared me more than either of them.
