# Chapter 23: Alice's Interlude: The Girl Behind the Ice

The dormitory rooms on the first-year girls' floor were identical by design.

Alice's was identical to none of them.

The staff never asked why a fire-element affinity student — registered, on paper, with a perfectly respectable mana index of 31,847 — kept her room cold enough to see her breath.

They didn't ask because her family name was Rimehart.

And you simply did not ask a Rimehart questions about temperature.

Alice sat cross-legged on her bed in the dark, curtains drawn, frost creeping in delicate ferns up the inside of her window glass.

On her nightstand, in a small glass vial, a star-shaped ice crystal caught the moonlight.

It had bloomed across the back of Lucen Gray's hand only hours ago.

It should have melted before she even reached the dormitory.

It hadn't.

She had transferred it into the vial with her breath held, like a girl smuggling a firefly home in a jar.

When she pressed it against the glass, it *glimmered*.

Her crystals never held this long without her actively sustaining them.

This one didn't need her at all.

It was content on its own.

It was, she thought distantly, the first thing she'd ever made that didn't want anything from her.

---

*You're thinking about him again.*

The thought wasn't a thought.

It was the entity's version of a thought — a pressure behind her sternum, a taste like cold iron on the back of her tongue.

"And?" Alice said out loud.

Chief among the long list of things Alice Celestia Rimehart could not explain to her professors: she talked to the thing living under her ribs like a roommate who'd overstayed by six years.

*You held his arm for eleven minutes.*

"…You're counting now?"

Silence.

The entity did not answer questions that had obvious answers.

It only answered questions it could use.

Alice fell backward onto her pillow and stared at the frost patterns on her ceiling.

The Rimehart family kept her issued room at or below a fixed chill, because warm rooms made the thing restless, and restless was a euphemism for *asks to be let out*.

She had learned that when she was eleven.

The greenhouse incident.

She did not think about the greenhouse incident.

She thought about it every night.

---

Eleven years old, and the gardener's daughter had asked to hold her hand.

The girl had been kind to her.

Nobody was kind to her, not really — not the tutors who flinched when she laughed too hard, not the cousins who called her *Princess Popsicle* behind closed doors only they thought were soundproof.

So when Emilia — that had been her name, Emilia, and Alice had not been able to say it aloud since — when Emilia offered her small, warm hand, Alice took it.

And the thing inside her had *reached*.

It hadn't meant harm, she'd told herself afterward, in the hospital wing, listening through the door to doctors using words like *mana siphon* and *exposure trauma* and *miracle she's alive*.

It hadn't meant harm the way winter doesn't mean harm.

It had simply reached for warmth the way a drowning thing reaches for a hand.

And when it touched something it wanted, it never, ever let go in time.

Emilia survived.

Emilia did not visit.

Alice learned, that week, the precise mathematical difference between being feared and being loved.

She had been performing that difference ever since.

Smile always.

Joke always.

Touch *never*.

Become so bright, so relentlessly sunny, that no one ever thought to look behind the smile and notice the thing wearing the girl.

It was a flawless strategy.

It had worked for six years.

Until a boy with dead-fish eyes and a mana index lower than a training dummy had looked at her arm linked around his — visibly, unmistakably *terrified* —

— and stayed.

---

Alice rolled over and pulled the vial off the nightstand.

She held it up into the moonlight filtering through her frozen curtains.

The little star rotated gently inside, suspended, patient.

"Everyone's nice here, you know," she told the crystal, or the room, or the entity, or all three. "All of them. Lady Iris. Rhea. The class council girls who invited me to tea last week."

*Yes,* the entity implied, in the way it implied things — a ripple of cold amusement. *And when you laugh with them, where am I?*

"…Quiet."

*Contained,* the entity corrected. *Leashed. I am a wolf in the back of your throat, and you sing so no one hears me growling.*

Alice didn't answer that.

Because the wolf was right.

When she touched Iris — and Iris, despite herself, had allowed a congratulatory handshake exactly once — the thing had *stirred*. Leaned. Assessed the empress-to-be the way a glacier assesses a coastline.

When Rhea had clapped her on the shoulder after combat class, the entity had surged so sharply that frost had bloomed across Rhea's boot and the entire girls' locker room had spent a week theorizing about a mana leak.

Anyone with mana was a meal it noticed.

Anyone with warmth was a hand it wanted.

Everyone, everyone, everyone — except —

*Today,* the entity murmured. *Today I sat still.*

Alice's fingers closed around the vial.

"…Yeah."

When she'd linked arms with Lucen Gray — this afternoon, walking him back from the archives under the flimsiest 'class representative escort duty' excuse she had ever manufactured, and she had manufactured some world-class ones in her time —

The thing had gone quiet.

Not contained. Not leashed.

*Quiet.*

Like a winter lake finally freezing all the way to the bottom.

Like a cat that had found the one warm patch of sunlight in the entire house and simply ceased to be a problem.

She had walked eleven minutes beside a terrified boy, chattering about refractory angles and crystal lattices, and inside her, for the first time since she was eleven years old, there had been nothing reaching for him at all.

No pull.

No want.

Just… presence.

Peace.

It had felt like the silence after a storm you've lived inside your whole life, the first moment you realize the wind stopped and you can't remember when.

Safe.

She had felt *safe touching someone*, and the absurdity of that — that *she*, the thing's cage, could feel safe — had been so enormous she'd nearly tripped on perfectly flat pavement.

Alice blinked at the ceiling.

That wasn't her thought.

That was — what *was* that?

It left as quickly as it came, a cold little voice like a footnote someone whispered and then took back.

She stared at the frost on the ceiling for a long moment.

"…Am I going crazy now too?"

*More than before?* the entity asked, dry as a frozen lake.

"Rude."

She had gotten used to it, honestly.

You could get used to anything. That was the terrible, resilient horror of being human.

---

She replayed the walk.

She had a habit of doing that — replaying conversations after everyone had gone, checking them for cracks, measuring each of her own smiles for thickness. Quality control on the mask. Tonight, she was checking something else.

He'd been afraid.

There was no way around reading it any other way — his hand had trembled against her sleeve, his steps had matched hers half a beat late like a man walking a known minefield on a dare.

He knew something.

She didn't know *what* he knew. But boys did not sweat in seventy-degree courtyards over a friendly class representative offering escort duty unless they knew *something*.

And yet.

"You didn't pull away," Alice whispered to the ceiling.

That was the part she could not file away.

That was the part that kept replaying whether she asked it to or not.

Six years. Dozens of classmates, cousins, colleagues, teammates.

The ones who learned what lived under her skin — even the rumor-shadow of it, even just an instinct — they moved away. Carefully. Politely. Smiles in place and steps already retreating, always retreating, an entire world performing a folk dance with her at the center of an empty circle.

She'd long ago stopped blaming them.

She was afraid of the thing too.

*I* was afraid of me. How could I possibly ask someone not to be?

But Lucen Gray had known fear, worn it openly on his face like bad weather —

— and walked her all the way to the fork in the path anyway.

He'd even said *that is slander* with a completely straight face when she'd told him he was warm.

Alice laughed.

Alone, in her freezing room, over the vial in her hands, Alice laughed.

It wasn't the practiced laugh.

It wasn't the disarming, *please-don't-look-closer* laugh she deployed like caltrops across every conversation since age eleven.

It was small and cracked and wet around the edges and it startled her.

Inside her chest, the wolf lifted its head.

*Oh,* said some tiny voice beneath them both — the real Alice, the one buried under six years of performance, the one that hadn't spoken since a greenhouse when she was eleven —

*Oh.*

---

*I want that.*

The entity's voice was not a voice.

It was hunger, shaped into meaning.

*Closer.*

Alice's smile faded.

"…No."

*MORE.*

The frost on the window *surged* — ferns exploding into wild branching structures, racing across the glass, racing up the wall, a lattice of ice maps reaching toward the ceiling like fingers—

"NO."

Alice sat up.

Her fingers were white on the vial.

Her breath steamed hard in the air, once, twice — and the frost *stopped*, shuddered, hung there on the walls like a tide arrested mid-flood.

"Not him," Alice said softly.

And to her own astonishment, her voice did not shake.

"Everyone else, fine. Everyone else, I understand. You want what you want, and I keep you leashed, and that's the deal. That's the deal we've had since I was eleven, and it is the deal until the day I die."

Silence.

The entity waited.

It had infinite patience. A glacier doesn't hurry.

"But not him," Alice whispered. "He stayed."

*That is WHY.*

"No."

*He is the ONLY DOOR I HAVE EVER FOUND.*

"No!"

Alice stood.

The frost shattered off her as she rose — shattered like the first thing she'd ever made that shattered *away* from her instead of toward something, and she stood in the settling glitter of it with her heart slamming and her fists shaking and her eyes burning.

"He's *mine* to choose. Not yours to take. Not yours to *fill* or *empty* or eat. Mine to — to *know*. Like a *person*."

The word landed in the cold air with weight.

*Person.*

The thing inside her recoiled from it like an animal that has nosed at an electric fence.

And slowly — *slowly* — the pressure eased.

The frost receded from the ceiling, retreating down the walls in orderly ranks, returning to its usual faint maps at the window corners where it lived on quiet nights.

*For now,* said the hunger.

It did not argue further.

It never argued further, when it knew it could wait.

That was the worst thing about it, Alice thought, sinking back down onto the edge of her bed — it never lied, because it never needed to.

*For now, little cage. For now is fine.*

*You will let me near him again.*

*You will* bring *me near him again.*

*You will do it smiling.*

Alice didn't answer.

Because, God—

—because tomorrow she would absolutely, certainly find some reason to walk beside him again.

---

She lay down.

She pulled the blanket up, and then — after a long minute of staring at the vial on the nightstand — she reached out, took it, and tucked it under her pillow.

Ridiculous.

A girl with the power to crack a glacier, keeping a homemade ice star under her pillow like a child's luck charm.

Ridiculous was fine.

Ridiculous was hers.

Sleep came the way it always came in this frozen room — slowly, in layers, like sediment, like drift.

And into it, soft and certain and more terrifying than anything the entity had ever shown her, came the dream:

Gray eyes, warm in winter.

A hand that trembled — and stayed.

A greenhouse, unbroken.

And a girl in it, laughing, whose laugh nobody had to perform, whose frost spread outward across the glass in delicate, harmless ferns, beautiful and idle and *done* with hunger —

— while the thing under the world lay coiled in the corner, perfectly calm, perfectly patient, licking its teeth.

Waiting for morning.

Waiting for Volume Two.
