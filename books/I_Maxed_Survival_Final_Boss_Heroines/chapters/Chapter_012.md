# Chapter 12: The First Evaluation Exam (Announcement)

I did not sleep Tuesday night.

Not because of the tea. Not because the future tyrant of the continent had patted my head like I was a stray cat she'd decided to adopt.

Okay. Mostly because of that.

But also because my body had finally processed that I had stood within touching distance of a woman who, in the game, had a special combat animation where she removed heads with the casual elegance of someone picking fruit. And she'd patted me. On the *head*.

[Evil Goddess Comment: "You keep replaying it. It's embarrassing. She patted you. You're a grown man with the soul of a spreadsheet. Move on."]

'It was a head pat from Death, okay? Give me a night to process.'

Anyway.

Wednesday morning arrived the way Wednesday mornings always do — suddenly, rudely, and with complete indifference to my personal suffering.

I dragged myself to homeroom looking like a man who had spent the night calculating his own funeral costs, because I had, in fact, spent the night calculating my own funeral costs.

---

The classroom buzzed.

Thirty-eight students, first-years of Solhart Academy, most of them nobility, all of them gifted, all of them capable of killing me by accident during a stretching exercise.

I slid into my seat near the window. Finn was already there, scribbling in a notebook with the desperation of a man rewriting his notes for the fourth time.

"Morning, Lucen. You look like death."

"Thank you, Finn. Your support means everything."

"No, seriously. Are you sick? You've got — " he gestured vaguely at his own face, " — *that* thing. The darkness. Under the eyes."

"I stayed up doing research."

"On what?"

'On how long a severed head remains conscious, Finn. The answer was upsetting.'

"Tea," I said, and left it there.

---

Instructor Kaelin entered exactly at the bell.

He was a tall, scarred man with the build of someone who had retired from the army not because he got old, but because war ran out of interesting ways to challenge him. He set his ledger on the desk with a *thunk* that silenced the room instantly.

"Listen up."

The room listened. Even Derrick Holt stopped cracking his neck, which was, by my count, the first time he'd stopped doing that since Monday.

"As most of you already know — " a pause, the kind of pause that meant *the rumors were true*, " — the First Evaluation Exam has been scheduled. Five days from today. Monday, dawn."

The room went very still.

'Oh.'

'Oh, no.'

I knew what this was. Of course I knew what this was. It was in the game.

But there was a critical difference between reading "First Evaluation Exam — Tutorial Arc, Chapter 3" in a quest log, and being a person with 0.009 mana sitting in the audience while the exam ate people alive.

---

Kaelin began walking the rows, hands clasped behind his back.

"Format is simple. One-on-one sparring. Brackets determined by random draw." He let that sink in. "Each bout is assessed on three criteria. Technique. Mana application. Tactical adaptability."

He stopped beside the window, and sunlight cut across the scars on his jaw.

"Judges are standing instructors. The result is a class ranking. One through thirty-eight."

'One through thirty-eight.'

'There are thirty-eight of us. That means someone has to be number thirty-eight.'

'I have 0.009 mana.'

'I know who number thirty-eight is. I've been playing this game long enough to recognize a tutorial boss when I *am* one.'

"Rewards," Kaelin continued, "for the top performers. Scholarship stipends. Access to advanced resources. House crest privileges."

Murmurs rippled through the class. Eyes glittered. Funding politics, family honor, pride — the usual academy starter pack.

"For the bottom performers — " the room quieted again, the way it does when the good news ends, " — academic probation. Supplementary training. Mandated study halls."

A few students shifted. Nobody wanted that.

"And for the last-place ranking."

Kaelin let the silence breathe.

"Expulsion."

---

'There it is.'

'There it *is.*'

My brain, which was a speedrunner's brain, a completionist's brain, a calculator with anxiety — immediately started running numbers.

Class of thirty-eight. Bottom three get, in descending order of severity: probation, probation-with-humiliation, and — for the dead-last rank — expulsion.

Expulsion. Gone. Out of the academy.

Which sounds, on paper, like a survival win. No academy, no death flags, no heroines with head-removal animations, back to an honest life of poverty and considerably fewer attempts on my life.

Except.

'Except the story doesn't pause because I left the stadium.'

'The heroines still meet their fates. Iris still burns everything. Rhea still vanishes into the west. The bad ends still trigger. And the only person in this world who knows the flags, the triggers, the timing — is me. And I'd be a bakery assistant in the capital wondering why the sky is on fire.'

Leaving the academy wasn't escaping the bad endings.

Leaving the academy was *scheduling* them.

Kaelin's voice pulled me back. "Provisional enrollees." His eyes didn't find me. He didn't need to point. "The same standard applies to you. There are no exceptions."

'Provisional enrollees. Enrollment worth one provisional me.'

'Right. Great. Love that for me.'

---

Kaelin dismissed the details and let the class absorb it.

And that's when I got to watch the reaction shots — which, as any veteran of this game knows, is where the important information lives.

**Lucas Ashford**, the protagonist, the actual main character of this world, sitting two rows ahead — he just nodded. Calm. Composed. Already visualizing his bracket like a man planning a light afternoon workout. The boy radiated the quiet confidence of someone whose mana core was eventually going to be measured in *apocalypses*.

'Of course he's calm. Statistically speaking, he's not fighting thirty-seven classmates. He's fighting *destiny*, and destiny throws the match.'

**Derrick Holt**, three seats to my left, cracked his knuckles. One by one. All ten. Then he grinned at the ceiling like it had challenged him personally. Derrick was going to be fine. Derrick was built like a door and hit like the wall the door was in.

And **Rhea Valtor** —

Rhea Valtor didn't react at all.

Not a blink. Not a shift in posture. The cold-eyed swordsman with ash-gray hair just sat there, practicing sword calligraphy on the corner of her desk with one finger, like the announcement of a ranked combat examination had the same emotional weight as a weather report.

'And somehow that was the scariest response in the room.'

'Amateurs flinch. Professionals plan. *She* had already decided who she was going to beat and was deciding in what order.'

[Evil Goddess Comment: "Careful. You're doing the admiring thing again. She's second on the kill-you list."]

'SECOND worst. I know. There's a whole ranking. I'm aware of my own content.'

---

The rest of homeroom passed in a blur of logistics. Bracket drawing on Friday. Dueling hall assignments. The option to concede "with honor" once.

*Once.*

'Once. As if conceding gets you out of the ranking. It just means you're ranked by a judge's opinion of how gracefully you gave up.'

When the bell rang, students poured out in chattering clusters — bravado, strategy talk, families already being invoked like patron deities.

I packed slowly. Deliberately. A man who does not want to be the last one walking out the door at the same time as the instructor.

"Lucen."

'Ah.'

'Too slow.'

Kaelin stood by his desk, gesturing me over with two fingers. The classroom emptied around us.

"A word."

---

"Sir," I said, approaching with the upright posture of a man attending his own performance review in hell.

Kaelin studied me for a moment. There was something in his expression I didn't like. It was — warm. Warm was bad. Warm in a combat instructor after a public demonstration was very bad.

"About the evaluation," he said.

"Sir."

"I've spoken with the dean's office." He folded his arms. "Regarding your assessment."

'Oh god.'

"Given your... *unusual* technical foundation — " there it was, the word *unusual*, the word he'd been using about me since the Ashenveil Retreat demonstration, the word that was going to get me buried, " — the standard single-instructor evaluation would be inadequate."

"Sir, honestly, the standard assessment would be more than suffi— "

"I've arranged a panel."

I stopped breathing.

"A panel," I repeated.

"Three senior instructors. Possibly an observer from the knighthood." He said this the way someone says *I got you a birthday present.* "It means more thorough evaluation. More accurate placement. Your breadth of technique deserves proper eyes, not one overworked homeroom instructor grading thirty-eight students in a day."

There it was.

The kindness.

The absolute, genuine, devastating *kindness* of a man who believed he had found a hidden gem and was doing his professional duty by polishing it under brighter lights.

'He thinks he's helping me.'

'He is arranging a magnifying glass, a pedestal, and an audience of experts, because he believes there is a diamond here, and what is actually here is a guy who memorized a dodge move from a video game.'

[Evil Goddess Comment: "Say thank you, Lucen. The man is beaming."]

"Sir," I managed, smiling. Smiling with my entire soul while my inner self was already pricing coffins. "That's — deeply generous. Thank you. I'll do my best not to waste the opportunity."

"I expect great things," Kaelin said, clapping my shoulder hard enough to rattle my spine.

My smile held until I was in the corridor.

Then I leaned against the wall and stared at the ceiling for a full ten seconds.

'Five days.'

'I have five days, 0.009 mana, zero offensive capability, and a *judging panel.*'

'Three options. Fake competence — impossible, panels detect bluffs, that's their whole job. Forfeit — expulsion, scheduled apocalypse, bad option. Find a loophole — '

I stopped.

' — find a loophole.'

---

Which is how I spent the rest of that Wednesday the way any self-respecting gamer would: in the library, speedrunning the academy's archives for exam precedents.

The academy library was old. Pre-Empire old. Dusty in the places that mattered — the regulation codices, the archived examination records, the centuries of accumulated bureaucratic precedent that every institution generates and nobody ever reads.

'Nobody ever reads the precedent library. That's the first rule of every game ever made: the answer is always in the appendix nobody opens.'

Hour one: nothing.

Hour two: the exam rules had changed three times in two hundred years, none helpfully.

Hour three: my eyes were blurring.

And then — deep in a ledger from seventeen years back, in a margin note written in sharp, resentful handwriting — I found it.

> *"Cadet C. Vale — passed First Evaluation without a single offensive action. All six bouts won by evasion alone. Opponents collapsed from exhaustion or forfeited in frustration. Judgment: PASSES, by regulation. Note appended by reviewing board: legal, but* dishonorable*. Recommendation that such conduct be discouraged. — Registrar."*

I sat very still.

'Cassian Vale.'

'Six bouts.'

'Zero attacks.'

'*Passed.*'

The precedent glowed in my mind like a legendary drop.

Because the evaluation was scored on three things — technique, mana application, tactical adaptability. Nowhere — *nowhere* — in two hundred years of evolving regulation did any criterion require dealing damage. Winning was nice. But *assessment* was about capability, and capability could be demonstrated defensively.

The board hated it. The board had stamped it *dishonorable*.

'I don't care about honor. Honor is a stat for people whose heads stay on their shoulders by default.'

'I care about breathing.'

And I already had the tool. The Ashenveil Retreat. A defensive footwork art designed — in the game's lore, anyway — for exactly this: surviving superior opponents by being impossible to hit, exhausting them, outlasting them.

I had demonstrated it once at half-effort and Kaelin had nearly offered me tenure.

If I could refine it. Make it real. Use it for a full bout against a live, mana-enhanced opponent —

'…I could lose every match and still rank.'

'Not well. But not last. Not *expelled.*'

The plan was ugly, cynical, and entirely dependent on technicalities — which is to say, it was a *perfect* speedrun strategy and I loved it with my whole heart.

There was only one problem.

'A speedrun strat requires practice attempts.'

'The Ashenveil Retreat in my head is a game mechanic. The Ashenveil Retreat in my legs is three days old. If I go into a real bout against someone swinging mana-charged steel at full speed, I need to have tested this against a live opponent.'

'But I can't ask anyone. Asking anyone to spar reveals that I have literally zero offense — and "the commoner with the miracle footwork who can't actually throw a punch" is exactly the kind of story that ends up in front of people who dig. People like Iris.'

'So. I need a sparring partner who's skilled, discreet, motivated, and won't report weirdness.'

'A person like that doesn't exist.'

---

I left the library after dark, head full of marginal regulations and exiled cadets, walking the long corridor toward the dormitories.

The lamps were low. My footsteps echoed.

Someone was waiting at the corridor's end.

Ash-gray hair. Steel-cold eyes. A practice sword resting on one shoulder with the easy familiarity of a limb.

Rhea Valtor.

'No.'

'No, no, the person who spars with me is supposed to be a hypothetical. I checked. Hypotheticals are supposed to stay in the planning phase.'

She wasn't blocking the corridor. She didn't need to. I stopped walking all on my own, out of the ancient prey instinct that governs the relationships between small mammals and apex predators.

"Cadet Valtor," I said pleasantly, in the tone of a man greeting the edge of a cliff.

"You use the Ashenveil Retreat," she said.

Not a question.

Not even close to a question.

My brain spun up every exit strategy it had and selected the dumbest one, because the dumbest one was the only one not already burned.

"I — recognized the footwork from a book," I tried. "An old manual. In the — the archive."

Rhea Valtor looked at me for a long moment.

"There are no books."

The corridor was very quiet.

"My father destroyed the manuals before his execution," she said evenly. "Every copy. Every plate. Every annotated sketch. He had his officers burn them in the yard while he watched, because the last thing he did was make sure the technique died with the legion."

She took one step closer. She was taller than me. I had somehow never been close enough to notice that before, and now it was the only fact in the world.

"That technique lives in muscle memory or not at all," she said. "You don't learn the Ashenveil Retreat from a page. The footwork punishes hesitation the page can't describe. Someone *taught* you. Someone who survived."

Her eyes didn't leave mine.

"I want to know who."

---

'Video game.'

'I learned it from a video game, Rhea. From a combo list on a screen, in a game in which you are a romance option with twenty-seven illustrated scenes and a bad ending where you disappear into the western wastes. Your father's legion was lore text. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.'

I said nothing.

Silence, as strategies go, has one great advantage: it can't be fact-checked.

Rhea studied me the way her father must have once studied maps. Weighing. Deciding what I was — liar, spy, ghost, mistake.

And then, to my eternal horror and immediate salvation, she made her decision.

"Spar with me."

'Oh no.'

"Tomorrow. Dawn. Eastern training grounds." She settled the practice sword across her shoulder. "You show me the full form. Not the half-pattern you used in assessment — the *form*, the way the legion drilled it. If it's real, I'll know. If you know it, you survived someone — or someone survived and taught you — and either way I get closer to the truth."

"And if I refuse?" I asked.

"Then I'll wonder," she said simply, "why the only person in the Empire who carries my father's art is afraid to show it to his daughter. And I'll keep wondering. For a long time. Loudly."

'That's not a threat delivered. That's a threat described, which is worse.'

She watched me do the arithmetic in real time. She could see me doing it. She was letting me.

"And," she added — quieter, and this was the part that actually landed — "I'll help you survive the evaluation."

I blinked.

"I watched the announcement land on your face, commoner. You've got no offense. You've got paper mana. You're planning something desperate — I can smell desperate, I grew up in it." A pause; something old moved behind the steel of her eyes. "A legion deserter's daughter knows what it looks like when a man's plan is 'don't die.' Show me the form. I'll make sure the exam doesn't kill you in the meantime. Deal."

A devil's bargain, offered in a quiet corridor, by the second most dangerous heroine in the entire cast.

A private tutor who was also an executioner's daughter. A sparring partner whose curiosity I absolutely could not satisfy. A rescue plan that would collapse the moment she learned the truth: that there was no survivor, no legacy, no answer for her — just a boy and a video game.

And five days until a panel of experts graded my corpse.

'I have played this game a hundred times.'

'I know what it means when two heroine flags run active simultaneously without resolution.'

'I know *exactly* what I'm agreeing to.'

"...Okay," I said.

Rhea nodded once — the way soldiers seal contracts — and walked past me into the dark, ash-gray hair catching the lamplight, leaving me alone in a corridor that suddenly felt like the closing scene of a season.
