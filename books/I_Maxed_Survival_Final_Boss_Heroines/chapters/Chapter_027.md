# Chapter 27: The Library Incident — Part 3: The Aftermath

Thirty minutes is a long time to sit in the dark with your hands glued to an eldritch doomsday machine.

Long enough to get bored.

Long enough for the adrenaline to drain out of your system and leave behind the shaky, hollowed-out exhaustion that comes afterward, like the day after an all-nighter where you swore you'd never do it again and then did it again two weeks later.

Long enough for my brain, freed from the immediate threat of death, to start producing genuinely useless thoughts.

*I wonder if the dining hall is still open.*

*It closes at nine on Saturdays.*

*Damn.*

"*Probably.*"

"Mm?" Alice lifted her head from wherever it had drifted. "What?"

"The system says I can probably let go. Probably."

"Ah." She considered this with the gravity of someone evaluating a restaurant menu. "Probably is better than definitely not."

"That's the spirit of someone who's never played a gacha game in her life."

"What's a—"

"Nothing. On three."

We counted together. One. Two. Three.

I pulled.

My hands came free with a low, wet *peel*, like tape off skin, and for one horrible second I thought I'd left the skin behind.

I hadn't.

What I had left behind, apparently, was blood circulation.

I held my hands up in the faint blue glow of the dying node.

They were black.

Not pink. Not angry red. Not even the pale waxy white of proper frostbite.

Black from fingertip to halfway down the palm, with thin branching lines of darkness crawling up toward my wrists like a child's drawing of lightning. They didn't hurt, which was the part my hindbrain correctly identified as the *worst possible sign*, because pain is your body's way of saying something is alive, and silence is its way of reporting casualties.

"Your hands," Alice whispered. "Lucen, your hands—"

"It's fine."

"Your hands are *black*—"

"They're very committed to the aesthetic. I'll give them that. It'll heal."

It would not, I felt, heal. But there are certain statements you make not because they're true but because the person across from you has finally stopped crying and you'd like to keep it that way.

Alice reached out and took my wrists. Her fingers were warm — *actually* warm, warmer than they had any right to be after an hour in a magical meat freezer — and she stared at the blackened skin with an expression I couldn't parse.

Guilt, mostly. Guilt wearing a mask of anger.

"You did this because of the node," she said. "Because of my—"

"Because of a structural engineering failure in an ancient seal that neither of us designed, built, or maintained," I said. "I did this because somebody left a live doomsday device under a school. Which, frankly, I intend to complain about. In writing. There's going to be a strongly worded form."

"...You're unbelievable."

"I get that a lot."

"You get that *never*. Nobody says that to you."

"That's also fair."

I stood up on wobbling legs and looked up at the long stairway we'd stumbled down what felt like a lifetime ago.

---

The climb up was agony in the way that only stairs can be when your whole body has decided to file a formal complaint.

Not sharp pain. Worse. The deep, systemic exhaustion of a body that had spent an hour running on terror and spite and had just realized both accounts were overdrawn.

Alice half-carried me for the last flight. She didn't ask permission. She just wedged her shoulder under my arm and took my weight, and I was too tired to mount a protest and too proud not to try anyway, and the resulting compromise was me complaining the entire way up about how this was chivalrically backwards.

"I'm supposed to carry *you*," I wheezed. "That's how the script goes. I maxed Survival specifically so I could look cool doing this kind of thing."

"You look very cool," Alice said gravely, dragging me up another step. "Like a dying grandfather. Extremely heroic."

"Your comfort is a blade, Princess."

"It runs in the family."

We reached the top of the stairs and found the library thawing.

It was the strangest thing I'd seen all day, and my day included an ancient evil smiling at me in its sleep, so that's saying something.

The frost was retreating. Not melting — *receding*, visibly crawling back down shelves and walls like a tide going out, seeping into the floorboards, withdrawing in long silver veins toward the sealed door of the sub-levels. Where it withdrew, pools of water steamed gently. Books dripped. A frozen chair broke with a sound like a knuckle crack and its occupant — a third-year whose name I didn't know — collapsed forward into a coughing, shivering heap.

Alive. They were all alive. Every frozen student I'd counted on the way in, thawing, gasping, trembling, but *alive*.

Somewhere to my left, a girl started crying with relief.

Somewhere to my right, someone threw up.

Clean-up on aisle everyone.

Then, with a groan that shook dust from the rafters, the great front doors of the library — sealed shut for hours by ice and ward and malevolent intent — swung open.

The cavalry arrived.

"INCOMING!" someone bellowed, and the night filled with light, shouting, and the heavy clatter of faculty robes and healer's satchels, and for the first time all evening I felt something unknot in my chest.

We'd made it out.

*Beat the level.*

Now came the part I hated most.

The debrief.

---

They found Professor Maren propped against the circulation desk, silver hair fanned out, staff still clutched in one loosely curled hand.

A faculty healer ran a diagnostic and announced, with audible relief, that she was unconscious but stable — mana exhaustion, minor hypothermia, no structural damage.

I felt something inside me relax about four percent.

She'd gone down buying time for students she didn't have to buy time for. Faculty like that deserved better storylines.

"Over here! Two more! A boy and a girl — they're on the stairs—"

"I'm fine—" I started.

"THE BOY'S HANDS ARE BLACK—" someone shrieked.

"Everyone remain calm," announced a resonant, unimpressed voice, and Instructor Vance pushed his way through the crowd like a freighter through driftwood, took one look at me, then at my hands, then at the frost still weeping from every surface of the library, and pinched the bridge of his nose with an expression of profound, cosmic fatigue.

"Gray," he said. "Of *course* it was you."

"I was just browsing."

"Get him to the infirmary. Get them ALL to the infirmary." He turned, barking orders. "Full sweep of every level. No one — *no one* — goes into the sub-levels. I want ward teams on the sealed door—"

"Already headed there, Instructor."

"—and I want a count of every student, living, dead, or unaccounted for, in the next *ten minutes*."

The academy, to its credit, mobilized like it had rehearsed this.

Which, given this school's track record of barely-contained catastrophe, it probably had.

A pair of healers descended on Alice with blankets and diagnostics. She endured approximately ninety seconds of it before dismissing them with a frost-queen glare that belonged in a museum.

"I'm fine," she said, as a healer tried to press a warming stone into her hands. "Check the ones who were frozen."

"Miss, your core temperature—"

"Is naturally low."

"Your *mana signature*—"

"Is also naturally low. Everything about me is naturally low. Check. The. Frozen ones."

I knew, in the way you know someone's lying when you've watched them nearly crack open and spill for an hour, that she wasn't fine. But her exterior temperature checks out fine, her pulse checks out fine, and the entity coiled under her skin had gone down with the node like a dragon clocking out after a shift.

Which meant the healers found nothing, and after a few minutes of increasingly bewildered diagnostics, they left her alone.

I was less lucky.

Because my hands were very visibly *not* nothing.

---

The infirmary smelled like antiseptic herbs and institutional despair.

I was deposited on a cot, curtains half-drawn, and surrounded by people who kept saying variations of *hold still* while my hands were subjected to an escalating series of glowing diagnostic spells.

The head infirmary mage — a stern woman with spectacles and the bedside manner of a tax auditor — turned my hands over for the sixth time and frowned so hard I worried her face might break.

"This is wrong," she announced, to no one in particular.

"I thought that too when I first saw them."

"This tissue damage pattern doesn't match cold exposure." She conjured another pale diagnostic light, ran it down my fingers, and watched the readout ripple and distort. "Cold burns from the outside in. This goes from the channels *outward*. It's almost like... mana drain burns. But inverted."

"Inverted."

"Mana drain burns happen when too much mana is pulled out of a person's channels too quickly. But you—" She shook her head slowly. "According to these readings, you have almost no mana to begin with. You're barely registering above a training dummy. If something drained *you*, it would find nothing there worth draining."

"It sounds like you figured it out before you even finished the sentence," I said.

She adjusted her spectacles. "Then whatever did this wasn't draining you. It was *scraping* you. Trying to pull mana from a vessel that has almost none, and taking the channel walls instead."

She said it clinically, like she was describing weather. I appreciated that. I needed her to keep it clinical, because my mental image of "scrape the inside of his soul like a pumpkin" was doing me no favors whatsoever.

"Will he keep his hands?" someone asked.

"Oh, he'll keep them. Healing magic can rebuild the channels — they're shallow ones to begin with, we'll regrow the damage. Two weeks of treatment, minimal scarring." A pause. "Full function uncertain until the channels settle. He'll drop things for a while."

"Two weeks of treatment," I repeated.

"Longer if you aggravate them."

"What counts as aggravating them?"

"Everything you're currently doing."

*Fantasy workplace hazard pay, when?*

*Yeah. Unlikely.*

The healer wrapped my hands in bandages soaked in something that smelled like mint and regret, gave me a draught that tasted like licking a copper pot, and moved on to the next cot.

I lay back, staring at the infirmary ceiling — stone, vaulted, disappointingly free of fast travel points — and let my eyes drift to the window.

Through it, across the courtyard, I could see the library's grand façade. Faculty going in and out. Lights moving in the windows. A cordon of ward-staves already going up around the entrance, orange light building in the dark like a perimeter being drawn around a crime scene.

Which, in a sense, it was.

I watched Vance direct traffic for a while.

And then I watched something new arrive.

---

The Imperial guards hit the courtyard like a tide of polished steel.

Six of them. Full ceremonial kit, Imperial sunburst on their cloaks, gaits so synchronized they could've been one organism wearing six bodies.

And in the center of the formation, walking with the precise, unhurried stride of someone who owns the ground she's standing on and the ground for three miles in every direction—

Iris Aurelia.

She didn't come to the infirmary in a rush. Perish the thought. Princesses do not rush; the world rearranges itself around their walking pace.

She entered through the main door — not bursting, not storming — just *arriving*, the way weather arrives. Heads turned. Conversations died. The curtain-parted chaos of a post-incident infirmary went suddenly, almost superstitiously quiet.

She stopped at the threshold.

Her eyes swept the room once — fast, efficient, categorizing. Triage-mind. The kind of look a commander gives a field hospital.

Then her eyes found my cot.

And for exactly one second — one frame, unnoticeable unless you were me and had, over the course of an extremely unfortunate amount of playthroughs, catalogued the microexpressions of the Princess of the Solar Throne like rare item drops —

—her composure cracked.

The tightening around her eyes. A fractional narrowing, like a flinch aimed inward.

One step. A micro-step. Her weight shifted forward onto the ball of her lead foot, the beginning of a stride she didn't complete, toward me.

Then she stopped.

Caught it. Smoothed it over. Turned the aborted step into a pivot toward the head infirmary mage, and by the time she completed the motion, she was so perfectly composed that I could have convinced myself I'd imagined it.

I hadn't imagined it.

I'd spent 347 hours learning to read these people.

I did not imagine it.

"Report," Iris said to the infirmary mage.

Not *how is he.*

Not *is he all right.*

*Report* — clipped, cool, the word of a princess who understands that concern, publicly shown, is a wound people can reach into and pull things out of.

The mage straightened and delivered it casualty-report style. "Fourteen students admitted, all stable. Minor hypothermia throughout. One case of severe mana-channel damage — first year, Lucen Gray. Treatable. Two weeks of recovery. No fatalities."

Something eased around Iris's shoulders.

Half a millimeter. You'd miss it if you blinked.

"I see." Her gaze traveled back to me on the cot, bandaged hands resting on my chest like a very sarcastic mummy. "The boy found near the sub-level access."

The sub-levels. She already knew where we'd been found. Of course she did. An Imperial intelligence network doesn't send six guards to a library unless someone briefed them first, and that brief almost certainly included *exactly* where the anomaly originated, and it almost certainly included my name.

"Yes, Your Highness."

"Walk me through what happened."

"He won't have to walk far," I said from my cot. "It's about thirty feet. I can crawl."

The head mage shot me a look of pure, undiluted murder.

Iris turned to me slowly, and her expression — I swear this — had the ghost of something caught between exasperation and relief.

"You're walking around?"

"Crawling, technically. Bandaged hands. Can't grip a cane."

"You fell into a ward collapse, you sub-zero mana anomaly, and you are *cracking jokes*."

"Some of us cope through humor, Your Highness. Others cope through Imperial formations. We each have our process."

"I'm told you went *down*. Into the sub-levels. With one other person."

The infirmary went quiet around us.

The thing about Iris is that every question she asks is a loaded one. She doesn't fish for information — she places hooks and waits to see which fish bite.

"That is what happened," I said.

"Why?"

"Door was open."

"The door was *sealed*."

"Then it was a welcoming door."

"If you're going to be glib—"

"I'm always glib. It's a chronic condition. No known cure."

Her lip twitched. *Twitched.* Twice. I watched her fight the corner of her mouth like it was a duel.

"We will speak about this at length," she said, with the tone of a woman issuing a subpoena. "When your hands are less... mummy."

*She made a joke. She actually made a joke.*

The guards exchanged glances that suggested they, too, had noticed.

Iris Aurelia turned and glided back out through the curtain wall of Imperial steel, leaving behind only the faint scent of sun-warmed metal and a room full of staring faces.

I closed my eyes.

*Two problems down. Princess wants answers. I'll give her a version that ends with me being boring and unremarkable, and hopefully she'll believe it.*

*She won't believe it.*

*She never believes it.*

The last arrival came an hour later, after most of the injured had been discharged or moved to overnight cots, after Vance had finished his preliminary incident report, after the library cordon had tripled in size.

She came alone.

No retinue. No guards. Just a woman in scholarly robes with silver hair swept back and a smile so kind, so immediate, so full of genuine warmth that every tired face in the infirmary unconsciously relaxed when she walked through the door.

Vice-Headmistress Elara Voss.

I knew that face.

From the game? No — not as a character. Not in the main cast. She existed at the edges of certain routes as background faculty, an administrator figure, the kind of NPC you talk to exactly twice for plot permissions and then forget.

But I'd had 347 hours and a completionist's disease and a stubborn refusal to leave any stone unturned, and I remembered — I *remembered* — that in one hidden data mine, one buried chain of cut content, there were unused dialogue files labeled with her name next to words like *seal integrity* and *resonance monitoring* and *the slumbering debt*.

Most players never saw it. Most didn't know.

I knew.

She moved through the infirmary like sunrise, stopping at each cot to ask a soft question, to offer a warm word, to place a light hand on a healer's shoulder and thank them. The faculty visibly adored her. The students visibly relaxed around her.

She was *good* at this.

Which was exactly the problem.

*Oh, she's good. She's very, very good.*

*The bad ones always are.*

She finished her rounds of the infirmary, spoke privately with Vance in low tones for several minutes — I watched her nod, watch her hand drift to his forearm in sympathy, watch his shoulders come down — and then, politely excusing herself, she walked out of the infirmary.

Out the door. Through the courtyard. Toward the cordoned library.

Toward the sub-levels.

*Alone.*

I sat up.

"Easy—" The duty healer caught my shoulder. "Where do you think you're going?"

"Nowhere."

"Lie back down."

"I'm lying. I'm extremely lying down. Champion of lying down."

I couldn't get to the window properly from my cot, but I could see enough.

I watched Elara Voss — kind-eyed, silver-haired, beloved by everyone — walk past the ward line without breaking stride, the orange barrier-light parting around her like it recognized her.

Like she'd ordered it to.

She descended into the library. I lost sight of her.

My chest went cold in a way that had nothing to do with Alice's frost.

*She knows what that node is.*

*Not "she's figured it out." Not "she suspects." She has always known. She's been *monitoring* it. The sub-levels aren't lost knowledge — they're*a managed asset.*

*That library didn't malfunction tonight by accident. And she — she's going to go down there and find that the seal isn't just weakened.*

*She's going to find it* dampened*.*

*Suppressed by a mana void.*

*By something that shouldn't exist.*

*By me.*

Behind the thin curtain partition — in the cot directly across from his, separated by a half-drawn screen — Alice lay awake.

She'd told the healers she was fine.

She'd lied, in the manner of someone who has a great deal of practice being not-fine while wearing calm like a cloak.

The entity inside her was quiet.

Truly quiet. Not suppressed-by-fear quiet. Not silenced-by-willpower quiet. *Quiet*, the way a house gets after a terrible noise finally stops, when you keep expecting the noise to come back and it keeps not coming back.

The node was dormant. The screaming had stopped. And the whisper in her blood — the thing that had coiled under her skin her entire conscious life, the thing that made her cold in every sense of the word — had curled up somewhere behind her sternum and gone very, very still.

Like it was *resting*.

She'd never felt it rest before.

She turned her head toward the partition.

Through the gap in the curtain, she could see him.

Lucen Gray, flat on his back on the infirmary cot, hands wrapped in white bandages and resting on his chest, eyes open, staring straight up at the ceiling with the hollow, exhausted stillness of a man who has survived something he has not yet decided how to feel about.

He looked terrible.

He looked like he'd been through a war.

He'd gone down into the sub-levels and put his bare hands on the end of the world, because she was there and it was her problem and he'd decided, somewhere along the line, that her problems were also his.

No one had ever done that before.

No one, in seventeen years of her life, had ever *done that*.

Even when she'd tried to push them away. Even when she'd worn coldness like armor and answered kindness with distance and made herself into the glacier that everyone learned to navigate *around* rather than *towards* — he had gone *towards*.

He had grabbed her hands and put them on the machine that wanted to eat both of them and said, in so many words — *we'll handle it together or not at all*.

She watched his chest rise and fall.

Watched the way he chewed the inside of his cheek in the particular unconscious way that meant he was thinking about something he'd rather not be thinking about.

Watched his bandaged hands.

His hands.

*My hands.*

For the first time since they'd stumbled out of the sub-levels, Alice became fully aware that her own hands — her *actually cold hands*, the ones that had spent her entire life pushing the world away — were curled toward the partition. Toward him.

She didn't move them.

She thought:

*I am going to keep him.*

It wasn't a conscious decision. It was a recognition, like the moment you notice the ground under you has already become solid. Like stepping out onto a frozen lake and finding the ice is a mile thick and you were never in any danger of falling.

*I'm going to keep this one.*

And deep inside, in the warm, honey-dark place where the entity lay curled and quiet and still half-dreaming of the strange gentle void that had soothed it to sleep—

Something hummed.

*Yes,* the entity rumbled, very softly, in a voice that for once did not sound like a thousand screaming minds. *That one. Keep that one.*

Alice went still.

In seventeen years, she had never once agreed with the thing inside her.

Not once. Not on anything. They were a body at war with itself, a cold girl and her colder passenger, locked in a quiet lifelong stalemate.

And tonight, for the first time —

—they wanted the same thing.

She should have been alarmed.

Instead, under the thin infirmary blanket, Alice slowly smiled.

A real one. Small, private, no audience.

*Dangerous,* she thought.

She didn't mean him.

She meant herself.

Outside the infirmary, beyond the courtyard, down in the thawed and empty sub-levels of the great library of Stellaris Royal Academy, Vice-Headmistress Elara Voss stood before the dormant node and placed one long, cool finger against the crystal.

It did not respond to her.

It had *never* failed to respond to her.

Her kind, warm smile faded by very slow degrees, like a candle being methodically snuffed.

In the dark, all alone, she turned her hand palm-up and watched the faint residue of void-scraped channel burn clinging to the crystal's surface — the fingerprints of something with no mana, nothing to take, nothing to drain, the exact antithesis of everything this node had ever known.

"Now *that*," murmured Elara Voss, to no one at all, "is interesting."

And in the dark and quiet of the sealed sub-levels, beneath a sleeping school, she began, very gently, to smile.
