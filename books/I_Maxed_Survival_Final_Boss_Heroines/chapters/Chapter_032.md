# Chapter 32: Strategy Session with a Tyrant

---

Wednesday evening. Iris's private study room.

I arrived exactly on time, because the decalibration incident had taught me that clocks are gods in this room, and blasphemy is punished.

I knocked.

"Enter."

I entered, bracing myself.

Because here's the thing about our Wednesday sessions: there was a pattern. Cryptography drills. Then food. Then food-related near-death experiences. Caramel-melon kisses. Chocolate cake ambushes. Emotional damage served with tea.

So when I walked in and saw the table, my brain short-circuited.

'Where's the food?'

There was no food.

No melon. No cake. No ambush platter of suspicious pastries arranged with military precision.

Instead — papers.

A wall of papers. Stacked. Sorted. Some bound with string, some with wax seals, some held down by a brass paperweight shaped like a lion crushing something smaller under its paw.

'That's not decor. That's a warning label.'

Iris sat at the head of the table. Her notebook — THE notebook, the one that recorded my life like a naturalist observing an endangered shrew — was closed. Set aside. Off to the left.

'The notebook is closed.'

'The notebook is never closed.'

'Something is very wrong, or something is very right, and I can't tell which is worse.'

"Sit," she said. "I've prepared a tactical brief."

'A tactical brief.'

You know, most girls her age might prepare snacks. Maybe a study guide. Iris Aurelia, Third Princess of the Empire, had prepared a *tactical brief* for a mock duel between two seventeen-year-olds.

I sat.

She slid the top document across the table.

Seven pages. Bound. Titled in her immaculate handwriting:

"Page three," Iris continued, merciless as tide tables. "The supplements."

> *Alchemical enhancement: confirmed. Dosage pattern suggests a standard catalyzer cycle — surge phase followed by metabolic depletion. In sustained combat, subject's mana output will peak between minutes one and three, then crash. Estimated vulnerability window: minutes 3 to 4. Output degradation of up to 40%.*

She tapped the page with one elegant finger.

"Here is your war, Lucen."

My name in her voice. Not *Lucen Gray.* Not *Gray.* Just *Lucen,* the way she'd started saying it this week, like it belonged to her vocabulary now.

"You do not win in the first three minutes. You *survive* the first three minutes. Then, when his mana crashes, the fight is between a boy who is exhausted and a boy who has been running for his life his entire time at this academy." Her eyes narrowed slightly. "Which of those two has better stamina?"

"...Me," I admitted. "Regrettably, me."

"Regrettably?"

"I've developed the cardiovascular system of a hunted deer. It's not something I'm proud of."

"It is something Derrick Holt will learn to fear." She said it flatly, like a weather report. *Rain tomorrow. Derrick will fear your cardio.*

'Why is that the most badass thing anyone's ever said about me.'

"Page four covers his dueling psychology. He has never lost. Not once. Not to a peer, not to a tutor — his father dismissed two instructors who scored touches on him in sparring."

I blinked. "He fired tutors for *winning against him?*"

"The Holts paid for applause and received instruction. They corrected the error." Her voice went cold in a specific way — the way it did when she talked about people who bought what should be earned. "So Derrick has a decade of victories that mean nothing, which he believes mean everything. When the duel does not go according to his expectation — and it will not — he will not adapt. He will *escalate.* He will throw more mana, more force, more rage, because rage is the only tool he has that was never gifted to him."

"And a rage-blind opponent..."

"...is a predictable opponent." She closed the folder. "Angry men swing wide. Wide swings overcommit. Overcommitment leaves the twelve-to-fifteen-centimeter gap. Everything connects, Lucen. Everything is a lever. You only have to find where it pivots."

Silence.

I looked down at the seven pages. The angles. The timing windows. The tutor's knee. The supply chain of a weakness, traced from a mercenary's old spear wound all the way down to where Derrick Holt's guard would hang loose on a festival afternoon.

"You did this in one afternoon," I said.

"I have resources."

"You have a spy network."

She looked at me steadily. Not blinking. Gold eyes like coin-flips — always landing on the side she chose.

"I have resources," she repeated.

"Iris."

"It is *page five,*" she said, ignoring me entirely, "that you should worry about."

---

Page five was different.

It wasn't about Derrick.

***GRAY, Lucen — Current Capability Assessment & Recommendations***

'She made a dossier on me too.'

> *Defensive capability: ADEQUATE against this opponent. The Ashenveil Retreat's evasion patterns are a direct counter to Carovian attack strings — angled retreat defeats committed linear assault. HOWEVER—*

I looked up. "There's a 'however' in my own review?"

"There is always a however." She leaned forward. "The however is this: you have no *answer.* The disarm trick — the technique you showed at the smithy assessment — will not work twice. He knows about it now. He will brace for it, compensate, maybe even bait it. You cannot win a duel on evasion alone. You can only *not lose.*"

She was right.

God, she was right, and I'd been chewing on the exact same thought all afternoon, like a stone in my shoe. Five days of Rhea beating survival into me meant nothing if survival was all I had. In Eschaton Hearts, every boss had a phase where dodging stopped being enough. Every single one.

"I know," I said quietly. "I've been thinking the same thing."

Something flickered across her face.

It was fast. Not a smile — Iris didn't waste smiles on *being proven right.* It was smaller than that. The tiniest easing at the corner of her eyes, like a knot loosening one thread.

'Wait.'

'Was she... relieved? That I agreed with her?'

"Your problem, Lucen," she said, rising from her chair, "is philosophical."

"Those too. But this one is philosophical." She walked around the table, heels clicking on the stone, and pulled a book off the shelf — old, leather-bound, no title on the spine, the kind of book that has killed people just by existing near them. She set it down in front of me. Open. A marked page.

"The Void Counter."

I read the heading under the diagram. A swordsman. Standing. Just — standing. And an opponent's blade mid-swing, and little ink arrows showing force flowing *around* the defender like water around a stone.

"It's a redirection technique," she said. "High-skill. Low mana requirement — which makes it one of perhaps a dozen advanced techniques in this empire that your condition does not disqualify you from." Her finger traced the diagram. "The catch?"

Her gold eyes lifted to mine.

"The catch," she said, "is stillness."

"Stillness."

"Stillness."

"As in..."

"At the moment of impact, you must be perfectly, absolutely still. No flinch. No shuffle. No pre-lean. A single muscle twitch telegraphs the rotation and turns the counter into a funeral." She closed the book with one finger over the page. "You must let the attack *arrive.* Fully. Almost. And then move — at the last possible instant — from a state of total calm."

'...Oh no.'

'Oh no, that's bad.'

'That's anti-me. That's the exact opposite of every setting I'm configured with.'

Because let me tell you about Lucen Gray. Let me tell you about 347 hours of Eschaton Hearts and a dozen games before it. My entire skillset — muscle memory across two lifetimes — was one commandment, carved deep:

*Don't be there when it hits.*

Dodge. Roll. Reposition. Distance. Read the wind-up, memorize the tells, iframe through the hitbox. Survival wasn't stillness. Survival was *motion.* Kang Min-jun once beat the Ashen Regent with 4 HP remaining by rolling for eleven straight minutes of gameplay. My hands still remembered that fight. My hands still got tense thinking about it.

"Your entire combat philosophy is avoidance," Iris said, watching my face like a scholar reading a scroll. "I have seen it in every drill. Every assessment. You are, so far as I have ever observed, impossible to touch. But this—" she tapped the book, "—requires the opposite. You must be *willing* to let an attack reach you."

"That sounds like getting hit with extra steps."

And there.

*There.*

The corners of her mouth moved. Not a smile — let the record show, Your Honor, that the defendant did NOT smile — but a near-collision event. A smile that had to swerve.

"It is getting hit *strategically,*" she said. "There is a difference."

"The difference is marketing."

"The difference is who walks away afterward." She shut the book for real this time and stood. "Stand up."

"What."

"Stand up. I am going to show you the stance."

---

I stood. Because when a princess who has personally executed traitors tells you to stand up, you stand up, and you file your questions in a folder labeled *Later, When My Heart Isn't in My Throat.*

She moved a chair. Cleared the floor space with the efficiency of someone who'd used this room for exactly this before.

"You know this technique?" I asked. "Personally?"

She took her position in front of me. Feet placed. Spine a straight line from crown to heel. And then —

She stilled.

I don't know how else to describe it. She didn't get *rigid.* She didn't brace or lock up like Derrick would before swinging. It was the opposite. Every muscle seemed to... agree to stop. Her breathing slowed visibly. And suddenly the air around her felt like the surface of a lake at dawn — the kind of stillness that isn't empty, but *loaded.* Like nature holding its breath.

'She's trained in it.'

'Of course she's trained in it.'

'How much else does she actually know how to DO?'

"The Void Counter is taught to the imperial line," she said, and her voice was calm in a way that made the hair on my arms rise. "We are born with enemies. Not all of them announce themselves across a dueling ground. Some arrive as a friendly hand, a poisoned cup, a blade in a corridor. We cannot dodge forever, Lucen. Even I cannot." Her eyes, half-lidded in the stance, found mine. "So we learn to stand still, and let the blow come, and return it to its owner with interest."

Session ended an hour later. Numbers. Ciphers. Two more drills where I reviewed Derrick's combo strings out loud while she corrected my terminology with the vicious patience of a tutor who had decided, probably against her own better sense, that I was worth the ink.

At the door, I paused. The folder was under my arm. Seven pages that nobody else on this continent would have received. Seven pages of labor that, as far as the Empire of Aurelia was concerned, had been spent on precisely nothing of value.

"Hey, Iris."

"Hm."

"Thanks. For the brief. This is—" I lifted the folder slightly. Words failed in a way they rarely failed me. "Nobody's ever done anything like this for me."

True in both lives, as it happened. Kang Min-jun had guides and walkthroughs and forum threads written by strangers. Nobody had ever made the strategy *for him.*

Iris didn't look up from her notebook. But her pen had stopped moving.

"You are a useful variable," she said. "Your survival has strategic value to the academy. To the account you're helping me build. To—" a fractional pause, a ripple in the pond — "*me.* It is not charity."

"Right," I said. "Strategy."

'I hate that I can't read her cipher fast enough yet to know what she just wrote about me.'

Walking home through the academy grounds, evening cooling off into early night, the fountain district quiet and lamplit and smelling of cut grass and the mana-lamps humming like a low chord —

I stopped in the middle of the empty path.

I set the dossier down on a bench.

And I took the stance.

Feet apart. Weight in the ground, not the neck — thanks, memory-ears. Shoulders down. I pictured it: the overhead cut, the full theatrical fury of it, Derrick's silhouette coming down at me like a falling tree.

*Don't move.*

Every muscle in my body disagreed. Loudly. With citations.

This is the thing about survival instinct — real survival instinct, the kind you build dodge-rolling through hell modes, the kind that kept a powerless commoner alive through two weeks of an academy designed to erase him.

It isn't a skill. It's a *reflex.* Reflexes don't negotiate. My body had one answer to *a sword is coming at your head,* and it was forty kilometers per hour of *leave.*

'Move.'

'MOVE. The hitbox is coming. ROLL. You've rolled through worse. You beat the Ashen Regent on 4 HP and eleven minutes of panic. MOVE—'

*This point stays fixed.*

Her finger. My sternum.

*Everything else rotates around it.*

I held still.

One second.

Two.

A breeze went through the hedges. Somewhere across the grounds a door shut. My heart banged like it wanted a transfer to a braver student.

I held the stance as the lamplight buzzed and the night settled and my body screamed its old one commandment, and over the top of that scream I could hear her voice, steady as stone.

Somewhere in the high west tower of the academy, a light was still on, and a notebook was open.
