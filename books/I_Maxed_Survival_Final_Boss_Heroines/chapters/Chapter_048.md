# Chapter 48: After the Duel: Four Conversations

The Shadow Banquet resumed around me like a movie theater unpausing after the fire alarm turns out to be a prank.

Nobles drifted back to their gossip. Orchestra strings crept back to life. Waiters materialized from whatever pocket dimension waiters retreat to during high-ranked duels, carrying fresh trays of champagne.

And me?

I was leaking.

Not literally — Rhea had excellent control, I'll give the Senior Council that — but spiritually. Everything from my left shoulder down to my right hip felt like it had been tenderized by a particularly judgmental mallet. Seven bruises. Maybe eight if I counted my dignity.

'Run status check.'

'Legs: functional-ish.'

'Ribs: filing formal complaints.'

'Social battery: negative four thousand percent.'

I limped toward the edge of the hall with the single-minded focus of a speedrunner beelining past every skippable cutscene. Corner. Quiet corner. Any corner. I needed approximately forty-five seconds of being furniture, and then I could go back to being a person.

I got eleven steps.

'Of course I got eleven steps.'

---

**Conversation 1 — Balcony (East)**

The cool night air hit my face before I'd fully registered that someone had opened the balcony doors for me.

Or rather — positioned themselves at the balcony so precisely among my escape routes that "intercept" was the only accurate verb.

Iris Eredine stood at the railing, silver hair stirring in the breeze, backlit by the twin moons of this world like the universe had personally handled her lighting.

'Right. The framing. The moonlight. This is a fully authored scene. I'm in a cutscene whether I skipped or not.'

"You wore my cravat," she said.

She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the moons. In Iris-speak, that was her standing with both arms wide open, emotionally.

I glanced down. The systematic layering I'd done this morning to keep them happy. The cravat sat exactly where she'd placed it at the fitting — crisp, immaculate, tight against my throat.

"I wore everyone's gifts. I was very diplomatic. A walking treaty."

"Yes." A pause, precisely one heartbeat long. "But you wore mine closest to your throat."

'...'

I had no available response. Not because she was wrong — she was technically, geometrically, insultingly correct — but because there is no gamer skill tree that prepares you for a duke's daughter claiming romantic territory via proximity-to-necklace measurements.

'She literally has me by the throat. Symbolically. She knows it. She's stating it out loud like a land survey.'

"I'll take that as a... compliment?"

"Take it as fact." She stepped closer, until her shoulder almost — almost — touched mine. The gap between us was maybe two centimeters. In Iris units, that was roughly a marriage proposal.

We stood there, looking at the moons.

"You fight like you're already dead," she said quietly. "You said that once. About yourself. I didn't believe it then."

"And now?"

"Now I've watched it. Standing still. Taking a councilwoman's full assault like rain." Her fingers curled once against the railing — the only crack in the marble composure. "Is that a compliment you're fishing for? It isn't. It's the saddest thing I've ever seen."

"Ouch."

"And the most beautiful."

'Oh.'

'Oh no, the double-tap. The standard 'insult followed by devastating sincerity' combo. That's an unblockable string. There's no frame data for defending against that.'

I opened my mouth. Nothing came out. My brain — the same brain that had routed fourteen Bad Ends and talked a seven-hundred-year-old dragon into a cookie break — offered me absolutely nothing.

She turned and walked away.

She didn't look back.

She never looked back. That was the terrifying thing about Iris Eredine — every exit was permanent, every word was load-bearing, and she'd just left a structural beam confused on a balcony.

'...She's also limping very slightly, which means she's been standing here waiting for me since before the duel even ended.'

'Don't think about that, Lucen.'

'"Closest to your throat," she said.'

'DON'T think about it.'

---

**Conversation 2 — Dessert Table**

My escape vector took me past the dessert table, which was fine, because my actual plan now involved consuming roughly my body weight in sugar as medical treatment.

"There you are!"

A blonde missile hit me at approximately forty kilometers per hour.

Alice wrapped her arms around my ribs — my BRUISED ribs, my seven-times-over tenderized ribs — and I made a sound that I will be denying until my grave.

'She aimed for the bruises. I know she can't see them through the formalwear. She STILL aimed for the bruises. This is a precision strike.'

"You're crying," I said, once I could breathe again. "Are those—"

"Happy tears!" She pulled back just enough to glare up at me, cheeks wet, eyes shining. "They're happy tears, shut up."

She was freezing.

That hit me a second late. Her hands on my arms, her face against my chest a moment ago — cold. Not room-temperature cold. Cold like a window in winter. And me, the human zero-mana space heater, warm enough that standing next to me must have felt like leaning on a stove.

'The temperature differential. She's been maintaining it all night. Through the whole duel. Through everything.'

"You were amazing," she said into my chest.

"I lost."

"You were AMAZING."

"I lost by a standing margin. I lost so hard the System gave me a title about it. 'The Man Who Stood Still.' That's not a compliment, that's a coroner's report—"

"Lucen."

She pulled back and looked up at me, and her eyes had gone serious in that way that always rearranged the gravity in a room.

"After tonight... can I ask you something?"

"You can always ask. That's literally your perk. Unlocked it ages ago."

The joke bounced off. She took a breath.

"The thing inside me." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "The cold thing. You said you could feel it. In the library. You said—"

"I remember."

"Does it scare you?"

And there it was. The question under all her questions.

I thought about lying. The speedrunner answer was *'no, of course not, next line of dialogue please.'* The Min-jun answer — the answer of a shut-in who played Alice's route with the lights on and still flinched at the scene where the cold thing in her looked back — was *'yes, obviously, your inner passenger is an existential horror wrapped in a snowstorm.'*

But Min-jun wasn't the one standing here.

The person standing here had held her hands in a frozen library while ice climbed the bookshelves and frost wrote her name across the windows. Had felt that thing turn its attention toward him, evaluate him, and — impossibly — settle back down.

"No," I said.

It was the truth. Which was alarming. Honesty was becoming a habit, and habits were how runs died.

Alice's eyes filled with tears again.

"Those better also be happy tears."

"They're happy tears, you IDIOT—"

She hugged me again. Bruises: recategorized from 'agony' to 'acceptable loss.'

'Somewhere, a route chart just rearranged itself. I can hear the flags shuffling like a card dealer.'

'Fine. Fine. Whatever.'

---

**Conversation 3 — Main Hall**

I made it exactly one table further before a hand came down on my shoulder with enough friendly force to shift my stance.

"Lucen."

Golden hair. Dawn-colored eyes. A smile that could legally qualify as a light source.

Lucas Ashford. Seat 4-C, Homeroom 1-A. The protagonist. The main character. The boy with the holy sword in his future and the sun in his purpose, currently looking at me like *I* was the impressive one in the room.

'Please stop doing that. It breaks my immersion. I'm the mob character. You're supposed to walk past me on your way to the plot.'

"You fought like a man with nothing to lose," he said.

"I *am* a man with nothing to lose. That's my whole build. Zero mana, zero stats, zero faction. I'm a survival mode save file."

He didn't laugh. Worse — he shook his head slowly, like I'd said something wrong in a way that mattered.

"That's not true anymore."

He gestured around the hall. One broad, easy motion that took in — oh no — Iris on the balcony, a silver silhouette against the moons. Alice by the dessert table, pretending very hard not to watch us. And across the hall, Rhea Valtor, standing alone at last, looking somehow lighter, like someone had finally put down a weight she'd been carrying through decades.

"You have people now, Lucen." Lucas looked back at me, and his eyes were unbearably, heroically sincere. "Whether you wanted them or not."

'Stop it.'

'Stop being the protagonist so CORRECTLY.'

'Do you know how tiring it is, having the game's intended hero look at the disposable extra and say things like that? This man's earnestness does area-of-effect damage. I'm taking emotional splash damage just standing in his radius. My nothing-to-lose armor has zero resistance to genuine kindness. It's a type disadvantage.'

"...You're right," I heard myself say. "I do."

Saying it out loud felt like stepping off a ledge. Because it was true, and true things in this world had *mass*. They pulled things toward them.

Fourteen Bad Ends didn't form around nobodies.

Lucas grinned — bright, devastating, exactly the smile the game made you wait thirty hours to see.

"Then fight like it. From now on."

He clapped my shoulder again — the unbruised one, somehow, as if even his hands had protagonistic luck — and walked back into the light of the hall, where people instinctively turned toward him like plants.

'Fight like I have something to lose.'

'Lucas, you golden idiot. Do you have any idea what you're asking? Fighting to protect things is how *you* lose everything. I've seen the route charts. I've seen YOUR bad endings.'

'...'

'But man. Coming from him, I almost believed it was good advice.'

---

**Conversation 4 — Stairwell (Observers' Gallery)**

I needed air. Real air. Air that hadn't been circulated through four hundred nobles, a magical duel, and three consecutive emotional boss fights.

I found a stairwell on the hall's north side — narrow, spiraling, unlit except for moonlight leaking through a high window. Unmapped territory. Off-route. The kind of space that in a horror game would get you killed and in a romance game got you—

—nope, she was already sitting there.

Lyra occupied the windowsill like a cat who'd been there before the building was constructed. Small legs swinging. Cheeks puffed out with candy. The eternal nine-year-old, peeling the wrapper off another piece of butterscotch with the solemn focus of a bomb disposal expert.

She didn't look down at me.

"You showed her nothing," Lyra said.

I climbed the last few steps and leaned against the wall beside the window. Everything hurt. It was becoming ambient.

"I showed her everything," I said. "There was just nothing to show."

"Exactly."

She grinned.

It was too wide. It had too many teeth for that face. It was magnificent.

"That's why it was brave."

'Right. She gets it.'

'Of COURSE the dragon gets it. Dragons don't have techniques. They don't have stances or forms or mana indexes. A dragon doesn't dodge a mountain — the mountain moves or it doesn't. Lyra doesn't have an Empty Stance. Lyra IS the empty stance, wearing a child and eating caramel.'

'Everyone else analyzed what I did. She just... recognized it.'

She held out another candy without looking, the wrapper already loosened. An offering. A treaty. A dragon's version of applause.

I took it.

"You're going to have a very interesting life, Lucen Gray," she said, swinging her legs. "I intend to watch."

"From a safe distance, I hope."

"Dragons don't do safe distances." She winked — and for one instant, in the moonlight, her eyes flashed molten gold, all the way through, no whites, no pupils, just ancient fire wearing a little girl's face. "We do proximity."

'Proximity. From a DRAGON. The creature whose idea of "proximity" is occupying a ballroom ledge during a council duel while snacking.'

'You know what it costs to have dragon-class attention on your save file? EVERYTHING. There is no insurance for this. There is no difficulty setting for this.'

I sighed. I unwrapped the candy. I put it in my mouth.

It was really, really good.

Like, suspiciously good. Butter and caramelized sugar and something warmer underneath, something that sat in the chest rather than the stomach. Either this was enchanted, or seven-hundred-year-old confectionery standards just hit different.

"...You made this yourself, didn't you."

"I did not confirm that."

"You made it."

"I did not CONFIRM it."

'The dragon bakes. The ancient apocalypse-in-a-child-form personally manufactures butterscotch and hands it out at banquets. This information is going to live in my head rent-free forever.'

---

**Alone, Finally**

I stayed in the stairwell after that, because Lyra's presence was, paradoxically, the closest thing to solitude this building offered. She required nothing. She asked nothing. She just sat on her windowsill and consumed sugar and observed existence like the world's longest-running spectator stream.

I leaned against the cold stone and looked out at the twin moons.

Four conversations. Four threads.

Iris, claiming my throat with a cravat and calling my survival the saddest beautiful thing she'd ever seen.

Alice, asking if her monster scared me, and crying when it didn't.

Lucas, handing me the hero's burden with a smile and telling me to fight for what I had.

Lyra, recognizing my nothing as something, and promising to watch all of it burn or bloom.

I came to this world expecting to die. Then I decided to survive by hiding. Then I hid so catastrophically badly that I ended up standing motionless in the center of a duel everyone would talk about for years.

'Let's review the strategy, shall we? Original plan: be invisible. Stay at the edges. Let the protagonist do protagonist things. Clear the game by being furniture.'

'Current status: title acquired — "The Man Who Stood Still." Four heroines orbiting. A goddess subscribing to my suffering. A Senior Council deputy carrying my secrets. A dragon stockpiling candy with my name on it.'

'Mission. Failed. So successfully it looped around to a different genre.'

And that was the thing I finally understood, leaning there with melted butterscotch on my tongue and bruises cataloguing themselves in the dark:

I was never invisible.

I'd spent 347 hours learning this world's rules, and I'd missed the most basic one. I wasn't a crack in the system. I wasn't nothing.

I was a void.

And voids don't get ignored. Voids pull. Everything near them — moons, dragons, saintesses, serpents, golden-hearted heroes — drifts toward them, quietly, inevitably, the way light bends around a black hole and calls it orbit.

Being empty wasn't my camouflage.

It was my gravity.

'...That's horrifying.'

'That's the most beautiful and horrifying mechanical property a character has ever had, and it's MINE.'

'Oh good. The System is mocking my insomnia now. Wonderful. Add it to the pile.'

Below me, the banquet's light guttered like a candle at the end of its patience. Somewhere in the dorms, beds existed. Somewhere, tomorrow was loading — the academy, the classes, the routes, the sequel I'd been promised with the slow menace of a goddess clearing her throat.

I pushed off the wall. Winced. Winced again.

'Eight bruises. Definitely eight.'

I walked down the stairs one careful step at a time, a maxed-out survival build piloting a body made of regret, and the twin moons watched me go the way they watched everything —

— like I was already part of the story.

'...Because I am,' I admitted, at last, to the empty stairwell.

'Damn it.'

'I am.'
