# Chapter 34: Training Montage: Four Days of Hell

## Day 24 — Thursday

The ground met my face at dawn.

Not metaphorically. Literally. The frozen dirt of the eastern training grounds, 6:01 AM, introduced itself to my cheekbone via Rhea Valtor's practice sword.

"Again," she said.

I got up. I swung. I fell.

"Again."

I got up. I swung. I fell.

"Again."

This continued for exactly one hour. I counted, because counting was the only thing keeping me sane. Twenty-three falls. Twenty-three separate occasions where the ground filed a restraining order against my spine and won.

"Your offensive capability," Rhea said finally, standing over me like a war goddess reviewing a disappointing casserole, "is below what I would consider functional."

"I know."

"You're never going to overpower Derrick Holt."

"I know."

She crouched down to my eye level. Which, given I was sprawled on the ground, was fairly low of her.

"So we build a one-move strategy," she said. "Everything we do for four days flows toward a single decisive action. Not ten techniques. Not a combination. One move, sharpened until the whole duel bends around it."

'...Huh.'

Because that was — verbatim — Iris's Void Counter philosophy. One pivot. One rotation. One moment where the enemy's own force becomes your weapon.

Two of the most dangerous women on this campus had independently designed me the same strategy. I was either a genius for picking them or the world's most obvious idiot.

"I know a move that fits," I said.

"I know you do." Her eyes narrowed a fraction. "Where did you learn it?"

"A book."

She stared at me.

The wind blew. A decorative academy bird screamed somewhere, probably mocking me.

"...The library has no books on Void techniques," Rhea said quietly. "I checked. There was one. It was checked out two weeks ago by someone using a faculty override."

'Iris, you snake. You checked out the ONLY copy so nobody could cross-reference me.'

"It was... a foreign edition," I said.

"Mm."

She didn't believe me. She absolutely did not believe me. But she picked me up by the collar, set me on my feet, and handed back my practice sword.

"Show me the stance," she said. "We'll start there."

**Fall count by end of morning session: 61.**

I called that progress. Rhea called it 'a geological event.'

## Day 24 — Evening

"You look terrible," Iris said.

The strategy room smelled like expensive tea and conspiracy. She sat behind her desk, posture perfect, examining me the way a jeweler examines a stone that might be fake.

"I fell down sixty-one times today," I said. "It's a new personal record. I've been speedrunning downward."

"Vice-Captain Valtor's methods are... blunt."

"They work."

Something flickered across her face. Very small. If I hadn't spent 347 hours reading facial micro-expressions in this game's dialogue portraits, I'd have missed it.

'Oh no. She knows.'

Of course she knew. Iris Aurelia had 'resources.' Which meant Sera. Which meant a student-council shadow sitting in a tree at 6 AM with a notepad, filing reports titled *Subject fell down again (14th time), morale unclear.*

"Speaking of methods," Iris said sweetly, pouring tea, "your sword grip has changed this week. Thumb placement, wrist angle, pressure distribution. It's a house-knight correction. Valtor-style."

"...It's from a book."

There it was again. The flicker.

'She's heard this answer before. From Rhea's side, somehow, or she's already guessed. These two women are going to triangulate my entire training schedule like a pair of angry GPS satellites.'

"Of course," Iris said. "A book."

She let it go. Iris Aurelia always let things go — the way a spider lets a fly go. Temporarily. Strategically.

"Now," she said, setting down her cup. "Let's discuss your opponent's actual weakness. It's not his sword arm. It's the space between his ears."

"Derrick's pride."

"Derrick's *entire self-image*," she corrected. "He fights from ego, Lucen. Every technique he uses is a performance. He's not trying to win the duel — he's trying to be *seen winning* it. That's a fundamentally different objective, and it has a fatal flaw."

She stood, walked around the desk, and began adjusting my shoulders with two fingers.

"If you make him feel foolish in front of the audience — even once, even briefly — his technique degrades. He'll overcommit trying to erase the embarrassment. He'll swing bigger than he should. He'll *tell* you where the fatal strike is coming from, because he needs it to be spectacular."

"...That's evil."

"That's politics." A small smile. "Same thing, in my experience."

Then she made me practice standing still for forty minutes.

Not joking. She circled me like a critic at an art gallery while I stood in Void stance, and critiqued my face.

"You look like you're dying. Don't look like you're dying. You're calm. You're *bored.* Derrick Holt is beneath your concern."

"My face doesn't do bored. My face does 'mild food poisoning.'"

"Then tonight, we fix your face."

'Iris Aurelia is *sculpting* me. She's going to be angry when she finds out another sculptor is working the same marble.'

## Day 25 — Friday

Timing.

That's all Rhea taught on day two. One concept, drilled into my skull with the subtlety of a pickaxe.

"Every attack has dead space," she said, tapping the midpoint of her practice blade. "The moment where a committed strike can't redirect. The attacker's body has voted. The motion is law. Until the strike lands, they are *locked in.*"

"And in that window—"

"You are free." A rare thing appeared on her face. Almost a smile. Almost. "A voter can't change their ballot after it's cast. Be the polling station, Gray."

'...that's the worst metaphor I've ever heard and I completely understand it.'

So we drilled reads. She attacked. I called the moment of commitment. Wrong answer — I got hit. Right answer — I got hit slightly less.

Swing. "Now." *Thunk.* "Too late."

Swing. "Now." *Thunk.* "Too early. He could still redirect. Dead."

Swing. "Now—" *I stepped, blade passed my ribs by an inch.*

"...Acceptable."

By noon, something weird was happening.

My brain — the part soaked in 347 hours of *Eschaton Hearts* combat routing — was starting to layer Rhea's 'dead space' concept over attack animations I already knew by heart. Derrick Holt's moveset wasn't real to me. It was *data.* It had startup frames. Recovery frames. A tell on the right shoulder before his signature overhead.

Rhea was teaching me to feel the timing. The game had already taught me to *know* it.

The two things clicked together like a key entering a lock.

Swing. I stepped. Blade missed.

Swing. I stepped. Blade missed.

Swing. I stepped. Blade missed, and Rhea stopped.

She looked at me for three long seconds.

"You're reading me," she said slowly. "That's not beginner timing. That's trained-eye timing. Where did you—"

"A bo—"

"If you say 'a book,' Gray, I swear on my captain's rank—"

"A *series* of books."

She hit me. Fair, honestly.

**Fall count by end of day two: 38.** Down from 61. Math was on my side.

## Day 25 — Evening

"Faster," Lucas grinned, wiping sweat off his forehead. His wooden sword rested on his shoulder like he was posing for a recruitment poster. "Seriously, man, you're *faster* this week. Your reactions are... sharper. Like something clicked."

Because something had clicked. Rhea's timing drills plus my game knowledge plus ten days of Lucas's hero-speed sparring were stacking into something ugly and beautiful — a prediction model that was approaching precognition for standard attack patterns.

'I can't win a fight. But I can dodge one.'

"Good night's sleep," I said. "Lots of vegetables."

"Liar." He grinned wider. "But a fast liar. Come on — one more round. I wanna see if you can slip the thrust this time."

I could not slip the thrust.

I slipped the *first* thrust, though, and the second, and Lucas Ashford — the actual protagonist of this world, the man with the plot-armor-pass — laughed out loud like I'd just done a magic trick.

"Who ARE you these days, Lucen Gray?"

'A speedrunner on ironman mode with four days left and two apocalypses on the schedule.'

"Your sparring partner," I said. "That's all."

He liked that answer. Too much. His duel against Derrick was the whole reason I was in this minefield, and his eyes still went warm every time I landed a footwork sequence.

'Please stop looking proud of me. I am a walking patch note of stolen techniques.'

## Day 26 — Saturday

Every day, sometime between sessions, I visited Alice.

Not for long. Ten minutes. Sometimes five.

I brought her tea.

Real tea. Plain. Unglamorous, non-magical, kettle-boiled leaf water — which, in this academy, was practically an act of rebellion. Everything Alice was given came laced with mana reinforcement, stabilizing arrays, 'health elixirs.' Everything she consumed was *treated* like she was already a patient.

Maybe that was part of it. The engagement ring drama, the Calamity variant, her entity stirring — behind every one of those events, there was a girl who hadn't been handed one ordinary thing in months.

"Is it... enchanted?" she asked today, cupping the mug. Her dormitory room was cold. Always slightly cold. The frost on her window had spread another four centimeters overnight.

"It's aggressively normal," I said. "The most average tea on this continent. Historians will write nothing about it."

She laughed. Small, but real.

I smiled back and monitored the ice crystal formation rate on her fingertips while she wasn't looking.

'Day 24: crystals at the second knuckle. Day 25: past it. Day 26: mid-finger, and the crystals are branching — structured growth, not random frost.'

'Containment is decomposing on schedule. Day 28, 9 PM, her entity hits the trigger condition. The game script doesn't care that two hundred kids are asleep in that building.'

"You're staring at my hands," Alice said.

"You have nice hands," I said, the way you'd defuse a bomb.

She blinked. Color rose in her pale cheeks. I saw her open her mouth and close it again, and the frost on her window *pulsed —* once — like something under the ice had flinched.

'...Emotional spikes accelerate it. Great. Wonderful. Affection is also a trigger condition. This game was designed by a sadist.'

"I should go," I said. "Training."

"You've been training a lot lately." Her eyes — blue, still blue, not the wrong blue yet — searched my face. "Lucen. Is something happening?"

'Yes. You, specifically. Your heart contains a sleeping natural disaster and my entire four-day plan exists to make sure you never find out.'

"Just a duel," I said lightly. "The whole school's talking about it. Guy with the body's mana of a breadbox versus the heir of Holt. Very funny content. Top quality entertainment."

She smiled faintly.

Then, as I reached the door, quietly:

"...Win."

The frost pulsed again.

I closed the door behind me and leaned against the hallway wall for exactly three seconds.

'Don't get attached to the success state, Min-jun. Attachment is how you make routing errors. This is a resource-management problem...'

'It's Alice.'

'...No one wins twice in the same day. Move.'

## Day 26 — Evening

Iris's lesson tonight: the walk.

"The duel begins before the duel," she said. "You enter the arena. Fifteen hundred people watch you cross the sand. In those twelve seconds, Derrick Holt decides whether you're a threat or a joke. Right now, you walk like you owe the ground money."

"I walk efficiently. Speedrunners don't waste frames."

"Speedrunners—? Never mind." She exhaled through her nose. "Chin up. Not arrogant — *settled.* Shoulders down. Your gait should say: 'I have already calculated this evening and I find it acceptable.'"

"What if I haven't calculated it and I find it unacceptable?"

"That," Iris said, "is what acting is *for.*"

So I walked up and down her strategy room for thirty minutes while the student council president refined my stride like she was training a show horse. At minute twenty-one, she stopped me with two fingers on my chest.

"Wait." Her eyes had gone sharp. "That's not the gait I gave you. You changed the weight distribution — your back foot pivots earlier now. Where did—"

*Don't say a book. Don't say a book.*

"...I adapted it," I said. "From your pivot theory. Made it mine."

'Technically true. Rhea drilled the dead-space footwork into me this morning and my body merged it with your posture like a cursed software update.'

She studied me for a long time.

"You absorb things faster than anyone I've ever taught," she said at last. "It's unsettling. It's as if you're not learning from scratch, but... remembering. Fitting new pieces onto a frame that already exists."

'She's too smart. She is genuinely, dangerously too smart. Dex Check.'

"I've had an unusual education," I said.

"I know," Iris said softly. "I've read your file. It's seven pages long and four of them are redacted *by order of the faculty.*"

'WHAT. SINCE WHEN DO I HAVE A FILE?! WHO WROTE MY FILE?!'

She smiled at my expression and returned to her desk.

"Tomorrow is your last full day," she said. "Rest in the morning. You'll need everything you have, *Lucen.*" My name in her mouth sounded like a chess piece being set down. "Do try not to break your own spine before sunset. The dramatic structure wouldn't survive it."

## Day 27 — Sunday

The last day.

Rhea's final session was short. She corrected my pivot angle once, watched me slip four of six strikes, then lowered her sword.

"Done," she said.

"That's it?"

"The cup is full. Pouring more in now would just spill on the floor." She slid her practice sword back into its rack, then paused. "Gray. One more thing."

"Yeah?"

"The footwork in the pivot is Valtor-taught. Fine. I trained you." Her voice stayed level, but her eyes were ice-blue and reading mine like a court document. "But the *base* — the stance that stance grew out of — isn't mine, and it isn't standard. Someone else has been teaching you, Gray."

'...

 '...a book taught—'

"I will dock your meal privileges," she said.

"—a very knowledgeable friend taught me the theory," I finished smoothly. "The rest, I built. What works to keep me alive stays in. What doesn't gets deleted. You're looking at... assembled knowledge."

She looked at me for four full seconds.

Then, incredibly, the corner of her mouth twitched.

"Frankenstein fighting," she said.

"I'm learning from the best."

"You're learning from the two most incompatible—" she caught herself. She knew. She knew exactly who the other teacher was. The twitch died and something colder set in. "...Choose your teachers carefully, Gray. Not every hand that sharpens you intends to keep the blade."

"Huh."

"What?"

"That's just — funny. Someone else said almost the exact same thing about you. Worded differently."

Rhea Valtor's expression did something I had genuinely never seen in 347 hours of gameplay. It was — annoyed? Embarrassed? *Competitive?*

"Lunch," she said flatly. "Then rest. Dismissed."

She walked off with the rigid posture of a woman who had just realized she was one half of a rivalry she'd never agreed to enter.

'Central misunderstanding confirmed and operational. Fantastic. My two mentors now hate each other's fingerprints on me.'

'...This has *flag* written all over it someday. Today is not that day. Lock it in a box.'

## Day 27 — Midnight

Eastern training grounds. Snow beginning to fall, fine as salt.

I stood alone in the center of the yard where, four days ago, the ground had eaten my face sixty-one times.

Tonight wasn't about Rhea's dead-space. Wasn't about Iris's psychology. Wasn't about Lucas's tempo.

Tonight was the Void Counter itself.

Iris's one move. The pivot. The whole plan hinged on it working — not in theory, but on the sand, at 8:30 PM tomorrow, with Derrick Holt's real blade coming down at hero-adjacent speed.

'Theoretically: the Void Counter requires minimal mana. The technique uses the caster's own mana signature as an anchor point — a void — and redirects incoming force around it. It's the only S-rank defensive technique in the entire game rated as learnable by low-mana users.'

'In practice: every guide I read assumed a mana index of at least 0.5. The user's mana forms the anchor.'

'Mine is 0.009.'

'Less than a wooden training dummy.'

'The Void Counter is a reverse filter. It's airflow around a space where nothing is.'

'...So let's test the stupidest hypothesis in my speedrunning career.'

I set the stance.

Imaginary Derrick. Hero-perfect form, ego-powered overhead, the exact attack pattern that had one-shotted players in Bad End No. 22's prelude. Startup frames: twenty-four. Commitment point: frame sixteen.

Let it come. Stand still. Hold the hinge.

At the last instant — *rotate.*

Failure. I moved too early. Phantom blade cleaved through the idea of me.

Reset.

Failure. Too late. Phantom Lucen, bisected. Again.

Failure. Foot placement shifted off the hinge. The whole pivot collapsed like bad code.

Failure. Failure. Failure.

Forty minutes. My breath clouded in the cold. Somewhere in the dormitory, lights were going out. Tomorrow's festival banners hung limp over the main road, glowing faintly with enchanted light — happy, festive invitations to the end of my current save file.

Failure.

'Why does it still feel wrong? The timing is right now. Rhea burned that in. The footwork is right. Iris polished it. The pivot angle is clean...'

I stopped.

'...Because I've been doing this backward.'

'The Void Counter doesn't need mana as an *anchor.* That's the misread every guide writer made. It needs a space where force registers *nothing.* The less mana at the pivot point, the *cleaner* the void. The technique was never designed for strong mages.'

'It was designed to be impossible for everyone **except** —'

'Everyone has more mana than me. Every mage. Every child. Every *wooden training dummy.*'

I stood up straight in the falling snow.

'I am sitting in the middle of the only 0.009 on this continent.'

'I have been the physical walking embodiment of this technique's requirement for eleven years without knowing it.'

One more try.

Imaginary Derrick. The overhead. Startup — my body recognized it without thought now, Rhea's drills humming right in my muscles, game knowledge calling the frames like a metronome.

Frame sixteen. Committed. Locked in.

I didn't step. Didn't brace. Didn't resist the idea of the strike at all. I let every muscle answer zero. Let the 0.009 spread out through me — not powering anything, not reinforcing anything, just *being* what it was: almost nothing.

At the last instant: rotate.

...And for one moment — one half second of clear, crystalline certainty — my mana pulsed.

Not strong. Never strong. But it *recognized* the motion. My body aligned with the technique the way a door aligns with its one specific key, and every concept the manuals explained in six chapters of dense theory suddenly wasn't theory at all.

The blade's phantom force came down, met the space where I had been, found *nothing* — and slid away, redirected, harmless, my body rotating out of the kill line like water splitting around a stone that isn't there.

The pivot completed.

Snow fell through the space where my head had been.

I stood there, frozen in the follow-through, heart hammering so hard Rhea could probably hear it from the knight's hall.

'...The technique didn't fail.'

'The Void Counter WORKS at zero mana.'

'It's the ONLY S-rank technique in the entire game that gets *easier* the more of a failure you are.'

I laughed. Quietly at first, then for real, one of those exhausted midnight laughs where you're not sure if it's relief or hysteria, snow collecting in my hair, alone in the dark training yard.

'Zero mana. Not a disability. Not a death sentence. Not a curse.'

'My entire existence — the breadbox mana, the deduction-worthy stats, the body that can't cast a light spell — has been the prerequisite for this one move.'

'I have been training for this since the day I woke up in this world.'

'I am the void.'

I stood there for a long time, letting the snow land on my shoulders, some small, exhausted part of Kang Min-jun from another world grinning like an idiot.

'Files patched. Route confirmed. Deadline stands.'

'Day 28, 8:30 PM: the duel. Required intervene window: under fifteen minutes.'

'9:00 PM: two hundred students, one awakening calamity, nine hundred meters of festival crowd between me and the worst phone call of my life.'

'Two boss fights. One night. Cooldown sharing not permitted.'

'...God, I am so underprepared for this.'

'But I've been underprepared for worse and rolled credits.'

## Day 28 Eve — Dormitory

I lay on my back and stared at the ceiling panels of my dormitory room until the frost patterns on the window became abstract art.

Somewhere below, the festival staff were still setting lanterns along the main street. Tomorrow this school would smile. Tomorrow fifteen hundred people would eat skewered meat and watch the candidate games and cheer for the duel between Derrick Holt and the academy joke.

'By 9:30 one way or the other, the joke ends.'

The System bloomed across my vision. Quiet this time. No sirens.

```
[SYSTEM STATUS — DAY 27, 11:58 PM]

[Mock Duel Festival — TOMORROW, 10:00 AM.]
[Scheduled Duel: vs. Derrick Holt — TOMORROW, 8:30 PM.]
[Calamity Event: Alice von Wiesenthal Containment Failure — TOMORROW, approx. 9:00 PM.]

[Void Counter: REHEARSED. Verified compatible with host mana index.]
[Dead-space reading: INSTALLED. (Instructor: Rhea Valtor)]
[Psychological destabilization routine: INSTALLED. (Instructor: Iris Aurelia)]
[Sparring acclimation to hero-tier speed: INSTALLED. (Partner: Lucas Ashford)]

[Assessment: Combat readiness... elevated from 'laughable' to 'statistically interesting.']
```

'...From laughable to statistically interesting. In four days. I'll take it.'

Then, below that, in the slower text — the purple-threaded text I had come to recognize as Her:

```
[Are you ready?]
```

'No.'

```
[...]
```

'Asked the Goddess who wrote my epitaph three days early.'

The System didn't respond for a long moment. The snow ticked against the window. Down the hallway, someone's floorboard creaked. Ordinary sounds. The last ordinary night.

```
[The Evil Goddess pauses.]
[She doesn't have a joke this time.]
```

'...Huh.'

'She always has a joke. She's had a joke for every near-death experience for eleven straight years. She drafted jokes about my ACTUAL death—'

```
[...]
```

'...You're worried.'

```
[Be careful, Min-jun.]
```

The words sat there on the translucent screen, glowing faintly against the dark.

I didn't move.

'Kang Min-jun.'

'...My real name.'

'Eleven years in this body. Hundreds of system alerts, thousands of sarcastic asides, eleven thousand years of evaluated plans, and she has never once — not once in all this time — called me anything but "host" or "player" or some elaborately insulting nickname.'

'She used my name.'

'The one nobody here knows. The one from before.'

I stared at the ceiling until the text faded.

'...Yeah.'

Something tight and unwelcome moved in my chest. I labeled it, filed it, boxed it — consequences later, execution now. The calamity timer didn't care about the emotional vulnerability of ironic goddesses.

But as I closed my eyes, one thought stayed, stubborn, un-deletable:

'She's watched me for eleven years.'

'And for the first time, she's not laughing.'

'Be careful, she said.'

'Okay.'

'Tomorrow, then.'

'One duel. One collapse. Nine hundred meters in fourteen minutes or two hundred kids don't see Monday.'

'Aight.'

'Speedrun.'

The snow kept falling over Stellaris, over the arena, over the dormitory where Alice slept with winter blooming quietly in her fingertips — and in the dark, a boy with the mana of a breadbox ran the route one last time in his head, and slept.
