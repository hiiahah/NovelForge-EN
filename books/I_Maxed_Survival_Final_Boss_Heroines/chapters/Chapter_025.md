# Chapter 25: The Library Incident — Part 1: Something Wakes Up

Saturday afternoon at Stellaris Royal Academy had exactly one thing going for it.

No classes.

Which meant no Professor Mera's withering stare, no combat drills designed by a man who clearly hated children, and no surprise encounters with Student Council presidents who had started saying my name like it was a chess opening.

Just me, the library, and a mission.

A *crisis* mission.

'Okay. Operation Golden Flower Bullshit. Begin.'

I sat at a corner table on the second floor of the Main Library, surrounded by every botanical reference tome I could physically carry, which — given my pathetic Strength stat — was four.

*Flora of the Northern Principalities. Rare Blooms: A Catalog. Medicinal and Ornamental Petals, Volume IX. The Gilded Herbarium.*

I was looking for one thing: a golden flower named "Lukaseu."

Because I, a genius, a survival tactician, a man with three hundred and forty-seven hours of speedrun experience, had told Iris Aurelia — the continent's most dangerous sixteen-year-old — that she reminded me of a flower.

A flower I had *made up on the spot*.

And Iris Aurelia was a dragon. A collector. A person who, upon receiving a pretty compliment, would absolutely one hundred percent go check the botanical archives to see the flower.

'She's going to check. She has the personality profile of someone who keeps receipts. She probably has a clipboard dedicated to me by now.'

So the plan was simple: find a real golden flower, any golden flower, learn everything about it, and retroactively claim THAT was the one I meant.

Damage control. Speedrunner style. Route correction mid-run.

"Golden... golden..." I muttered, flipping pages. "Sunspike. No, too spiky. Emperor's Lantern. Too lantern-y. Goldenthorn — that's a weed that kills livestock, nope, definitely not comparing the sun princess to a livestock-killer—"

"Hi!"

A stack of books landed on my table with a *thunk* that shook my inkwell.

I looked up.

Silver-blonde hair. Bright eyes. A smile like the first day of spring decided to become a person.

Alice.

'Oh no.'

"Are you busy?" she asked, already pulling out the chair beside me. "I'm studying ice-crystal taxonomy and it's SO boring. Can I study near you? You make things more interesting."

'I make things more interesting. Me. The human equivalent of a gray loading screen. The 0.009 wonder. HOW. HOW AM I INTERESTING.'

"I—" I started.

"Great!" she said, sitting down with the satisfied finality of a nation that has just annexed a small neighboring country.

She opened her book. Her shoulder was approximately nine centimeters from mine. I had measured. Not because I cared. Because proximity to a final boss was survival-relevant data.

'Okay. Okay, this is manageable. Alice during the daytime, in public, with witnesses — she's docile in this state. Statistically her worst outcomes all happen at—'

"Lucen!"

I closed my eyes.

'Nope. No. I heard nothing. I'm a rock. I'm a decorative gourd—'

"There you are!" Finn dropped into the chair on my other side with the explosive energy of a golden retriever that had never once been told "no" in its entire life. "Want to quiz each other on the Mana Theory exam? I've got flash cards! I made FLASH CARDS, Lucen!"

"You made flash cards for an exam that's three weeks away."

"Front side has the question, back side has the answer," Finn announced, apparently to the concept of the conversation itself.

I sat there. Trapped. Sandwiched.

On my left, a cinnamon-roll commoner boy with the plot armor of wet tissue paper.

On my right, an eldritch horror wearing the skin of a beautiful teenage girl, who was currently — I glanced over — *drawing tiny hearts in the margin of a textbook about ice lattice structures.*

'How. How is this my Saturday.'

This was the youth I never had in my previous life, delivered at gunpoint.

"Man," Finn sighed happily, entirely oblivious to the cosmic terror seated one meter away, "Saturdays are the best. No classes. Just libraries. And friends."

"Yeah," said the cosmic terror brightly, drawing another heart. "Friends!"

'I'm going to die with flash cards in my lap.'

Then the floor shuddered.

---

It was subtle at first.

A vibration. The kind you feel in your teeth more than your feet. My inkwell rippled, once, and went still.

Finn didn't notice. Alice did.

Her pen stopped moving mid-heart.

'...Hm.'

Then the temperature began to drop.

Not gradually. The library went from "comfortable study hall" to "why can I see my breath" in the space of ninety seconds. My breath fogged in front of my book. The pages of *The Gilded Herbarium* grew stiff under my fingers.

"Uwah, did someone open a window?" Finn rubbed his arms. "It's freezing."

No one had opened a window.

Because across the reading room, on the tall arched windows of the library, frost was *crawling*.

Up. From the bottom of the glass. In long, branching fingers, like the window was developing nerves.

'...No.'

'No no no.'

I knew that frost pattern.

Three hundred and forty-seven hours. Hundreds of runs. Datamined archives. I had seen that exact crystalline branching structure rendered in-game at sixteen times this resolution.

That wasn't natural frost. Natural frost feathers outward in hexagons.

That frost grew *inward*. Spiraling. Deliberate.

Abyssal ice.

'It's April. It's Year 1, Month 2. The library event is a YEAR AWAY. It's a YEAR TWO EVENT—'

Down on the lower shelves, I heard the soft *tick-tick-tick* of ice forming on book spines. A first-year girl near the periodicals gasped and pulled her hand back from a shelf, ice crystals sparkling on her fingertips.

And then Professor Maren stood up from the circulation desk.

Old. Severe. Gray bun tight enough to qualify as a martial art. She surveyed the frost on her windows with the expression of a woman who had worked in this building for forty years and had *seen some things*, but was choosing, professionally, to lie about which ones.

"Everyone, please remain calm." Her voice carried with practiced librarian projection. "Please move up from the lower shelves and gather here on the second level."

Students began shuffling over. Finn scrambled up immediately. Alice didn't move. Her eyes were fixed on the floor.

"The archive wards below the building are cycling," Professor Maren continued smoothly. "It's a known phenomenon. The old containment enchantments trigger a cold surge every few decades. It's harmless, if inconvenient. Facilities will have it corrected by evening."

The three background students — a pair of second-years and one small first-year with round glasses — nodded with the trusting relief of people who have just been told the monster under the bed is a plumbing issue.

Finn nodded too. "See? Happens every few decades. It's like a... a mana geyser. Kind of cool, actually!"

'Sure. A mana geyser. That paints spirals. That grows upward against gravity. That my very soul is currently humming along to like a tuning fork.'

Professor Maren was wrong.

I couldn't blame her. She had forty years of institutional knowledge, a groundskeeper's report, and every rational reason to believe this was the wards.

I had a speedrunner's save-scarred paranoia and the complete script of *Eschaton Hearts*.

And the script said: *Sub-Level 3. Sealed resonance node. Abyssal anchor point, one of nine buried beneath the academy's foundation by the founding archmages.*

In the game, it doesn't wake up until Year 2.

In the game, it wakes up because Alice — Celestia Rimehart, dormant vessel of the thing wearing her name — has a late-night study session three floors directly above it, and her dormant entity stretches in its sleep, and the node *answers*.

A resonance. A hand reaching up to meet a hand reaching down.

'But it can't be activating now. Alice hasn't had any incidents. She's contained. She's—'

I stopped mid-thought.

A terrible, cold, marble-heavy thought rolled into place in my skull.

'My mana is 0.009.'

'Weaker than a training dummy. Weaker than a tea kettle with ambitions. A void. A hole in the world wearing a boy's uniform.'

'And I have been coming to this library. Every. Single. Week. Sitting for hours on the second floor. Directly above Sub-Level 3.'

An Abyssal seal doesn't respond to power. Power flows *around* seals.

You know what a seal *can't* account for?

A gap. A nothing. An absence-shaped absence, parked on top of it for four hours every Saturday, radiating zero-point-zero-zero-nine worth of *nothing* into it like a slow leak in reality.

'I didn't trip this event.'

'I FED it.'

"Lucen? You look pale," Finn said. "Paler. Paler than the standard amount of pale."

"I'm fine," I said, in the tone of voice that has never, in the entire history of the phrase, meant *fine*.

Then a *groan* rolled up through the floorboards. Deep. Structural. The whole east wall of shelves swayed, books rattling like teeth.

And the library doors — both sets, the grand main entrance and the east reading room doors — slammed shut on their own.

*CRACK.*

Ice exploded across them from the frames inward. Thick, white-blue, spreading like something alive slapping its hands over a mouth. The enchanted locks flashed once — defensive wards firing — and then the light *died*, smothered under ice that didn't care about four hundred years of archmage craftsmanship.

The small first-year with round glasses whimpered.

"Oh," said Professor Maren quietly, looking at the frozen doors.

And for the first time in her forty-year career, her voice lost the librarian steadiness.

"...That's new."

"Not now," I muttered under my breath.

"The wards have never sealed the study halls," Professor Maren was saying, striding toward the doors with her sleeves pushed up. "Stand back, children."

She pressed her palm to the ice. Mana flared — a proper, strong, professional output, the kind my 0.009 looked at the way a candle looks at the sun.

The ice pulsed once.

And her ice-blue spell-light *vanished into it*, swallowed, gone, like a coin dropped into a frozen lake.

Professor Maren stared at her hand.

"...Everyone," she said very carefully, in the voice adults use when they have decided the children must not know there is a fire, "please move to the center of the hall. Away from the walls. Now."

Students screamed softly and clustered. Finn grabbed my sleeve. "Lucen, she's scared. Why is she scared — Professor Maren is NEVER scared—"

I wasn't listening.

Because Alice hadn't moved.

She was still sitting at the table. Her pen lay where it had fallen, mid-heart. And she was looking at the floor with an expression I had never, ever seen on her face before.

Not in 347 hours. Not in a single frame of a single route.

She looked *frightened*.

"Alice?"

She grabbed my arm.

Her hand was cold. Not cold like skin — cold like the *concept* of cold had grabbed me. And her face, when she turned it up to me, was pale in a way that had nothing to do with the falling temperature.

"Lucen," she whispered. "Something's wrong."

'I KNOW.'

"I can feel—" Her breath hitched. "Something's waking up. Down there."

She pointed at the floor.

At Sub-Level 3.

At the sealed node that in sixteen months of canon time should have been sleeping, and that was now — I could feel it now, we all could, a pressure in the ears, a wrongness like standing too close to a very large bell after it's struck — *stretching*.

And Alice's hands were glittering.

Ice crystals. Forming out of nothing on her skin, blooming up from her fingertips, crawling past her wrists. Except they weren't frost. They were *structured*.

Geometric. Ring within ring within ring.

Circles.

Abyssal containment circles — I recognized them from the Year 3 boss fight CGs, the ones that showed up on-screen right before the player character died in ways requiring an age verification checkbox.

Her sleeves hid most of it. The patterns were spreading up her forearms, under the fabric of her academy uniform, glowing faintly through the cloth.

Her entity was casting containment wards.

On *itself*.

Reflexively. Involuntarily. The caged eldritch thing was grabbing the bars of its own cage and *holding the door shut* — through her body, on the second floor of a library, surrounded by six innocent students and one increasingly suspicious librarian.

"Alice." I kept my voice low. "Your arms."

She looked down at herself and her eyes went huge.

"No," she breathed. "Not here. Not here, please—"

The circles pulsed. **thmm.**

I felt it in my teeth.

"Can you feel it?" she whispered suddenly, grabbing my wrist with both hands now, crystal-laced fingers and all. Her voice was strained like a rope at maximum load. "The cold? Not — not the air. The *deep* cold. The one underneath. Can you?"

"Luceeeen," Finn called nervously from the student huddle ten meters away. "Why are you guys still over there—"

The truth is, I should have said no.

Every survival instinct, every speedrunner instinct, every scrap of the playbook screamed it. *Say no. Be a normal boy with normal mana-less senses. Insignificance is armor. Blend in. The abyss has never once eaten a background character.* That's the whole meta. That's the whole *build*.

But Alice was looking at me with ice forming in her eyelashes and terror in her face and containment circles burning up her arms, fighting something the size of a buried god with nothing but her own white-knuckled grip on being a girl named Alice who drew hearts in textbooks.

And underneath my ribs, my pathetic, worthless, 0.009-of-a-soul could feel it too.

The deep cold. The bass note in the bones. The thing below, breathing in its sleep, dreaming louder.

Because of course I could feel it.

My void and its abyss. Nothing recognizing nothing.

"...Yes," I said. "I can feel it."

Her eyes went wide.

"You can?" A broken, disbelieving laugh shook out of her. "You can *feel* it? No one else can. Look at them—" her voice cracked without rising, desperate, hushed— "they all think it's the wards. Even that professor. Even she can't hear it, and I've sat in her library for two years being so careful, being so— Lucen, it's been singing at me since spring and I've been telling it to *sleep*—"

'It's been singing since spring.'

'Oh, you have got to be kidding me. I've been coming to this library since spring.'

'I haven't just been weakening the seal. IT'S BEEN CALLING TO HER THE WHOLE TIME. THROUGH THE FLOOR. AND I'M THE ONE WHO TURNED UP THE VOLUME—'

All of them. Every crystal lamp in the Stellaris Royal Academy Main Library, dead in the same half-second.

Screams. Finn yelling my name. Professor Maren shouting for calm over the sound of frost now *running* up the walls, audibly, like an army sprinting up the architecture in the dark.

The library plunged into black.

Almost.

Because Alice was glowing.

Her circles — spiraling up her arms, her neck, curling under the line of her jaw — shone with a faint, steady, eldritch blue. The color of light that has had to travel a very long way through a very deep place to get here. Every student in the hall went silent, staring at the one lit shape in the darkness.

The girl glowing like a lantern made of winter.

And from below — from beneath Sub-Level 3, beneath the stone, beneath the seal that my worthless nothing-mana had spent a season quietly unbuckling — came a sound.

Not a sound.

A sound isn't the word. It didn't enter through the ears. It bloomed in the marrow, in the teeth, in the soft jelly behind the eyes. A vast, slow, contented note, like a leviathan turning over in the dark after a very long sleep.

Something was waking up.

And it was pleased.

Alice looked at me.

Her eyes were changing. Ice-blue — her pretty, ordinary, human ice-blue — deepening on the exhale, sinking past blue into something older. Something from the bottom of the ocean of the sky. Something vast. Her pupils widened, and for one unbearable half-second they weren't round.

Containment circles shatter-cracked off her left wrist and dissolved into glittering dust. Then another. Off her right forearm. Failing. One by one. The deep blue light eating the lantern-glass of a sixteen-year-old girl from the inside.

She raised her hand toward me. Trembling. Crystalline. Beautiful and ending.

"Lucen," she whispered.

Her voice had a harmonic underneath it. Two tones, braided. One belonged to a girl who drew hearts in textbooks.

The other one didn't belong to anything with a name in any human tongue.

"Help me."
