# Chapter 39: Voss Moves a Piece

The office of the Vice-Headmistress of Stellaris Royal Academy smelled like chamomile and old paper.

It was a warm room. Sunlight came through tall arched windows and pooled on a handwoven rug the color of wheat. Shelves lined the walls, and on the shelves were books, and among the books were plants — ferns and climbing ivy and one cheerful little succulent that Elara Voss had named 'Roderick' because students found that funny.

There were things on her desk.

Three things.

The first was an incident report: *Dormitory 3, East Wing. Night of Day 27. Manifestation event. Partial containment breach. Casualties: none. Injuries: two students, minor. One professor, moderate. Property damage: significant.*

The second was a personnel file, thin and sad and barely a month old, stamped with the Academy's silver seal: **RIMEHART, CELESTIA. Homeroom 1-A. Seat designation: Heir of the North.**

The third was a file so thin it was almost embarrassing. A handful of pages. An entrance examination noted with a single incredulous word in the examiner's margin — *"repeat??"* — and a photograph of a thin, gray-eyed boy who looked like he'd been briefly misplaced by the universe and no one had come to claim the error.

**GRAY, LUCEN. Homeroom 1-A. Seat 7-F.**

**Mana Index: 0.009 / 100,000.**

Elara Voss read all three documents the way a doctor reads patient charts.

Slowly. Carefully. With the tender, focused attention of someone who genuinely, achingly cared.

"Poor children," she murmured, setting the incident report down and touching the corner of Celestia's photograph with two gentle fingers. "Carrying burdens they never asked for."

She meant it.

She meant it completely.

That was the problem.

Elara Voss rose from her desk and went to water the plants.

She watered Roderick first — one doesn't let one's friends wilt — then the ferns, trailing the spout of her little brass can, humming something. An old hymn. The kind sung in chapels to a god of light, in a major key, warm and grateful.

She had sung it in those chapels too, once. Decades ago. Before she understood.

'The world is a garden,' she thought, pausing to wipe a speck of dust from a leaf with her thumb. 'And the faithful keep telling me that suffering is simply part of its design.'

'What arrogant, lazy gardeners we have been.'

She had read the old texts. Not the sanitized cathedral editions — the *real* ones, the ones the Church of Ending kept in their vaults, translated off stone tablets that predated the current calendar by eleven thousand years.

The world had died before.

Not once. Not twice. Iteration after iteration — the texts described them the way a farmer might describe failed harvests. Planted with hope. Grown with labor. Ruined by rot that grew from within, patiently, every single time, because the flaw was in the seed.

And somewhere in that long, wearying litany of failed worlds, a promise was written:

*When the light knows what to do with what it makes, then mind will meet mind and flesh know flesh, and sorrow is a stranger, and the shadow gone.*

A new iteration. A *correct* one.

Not destruction. Germination.

You do not hate the old tree when you clear it to plant a better orchard.

You thank it, and you weep a little, and you clear it anyway — because leaving it standing means the rot wins forever.

Elara finished watering, set the brass can down, and smiled at her plants the way grandmothers smile at sleeping grandchildren.

"Soon," she told them softly, the way one reassures beloved things that are temporary.

She had loved this world for sixty-two years. That was precisely why she had accepted the necessity of ending it.

The mirror on the far wall was a mirror until it wasn't.

At precisely four in the afternoon, the lamplight along its frame deepened from gold to gray, and the reflection stopped showing the office and started showing a man in a dark room, wearing a dark hood, in the manner of conspirators everywhere who have never once been told they look theatrical.

"Vice-Headmistress." The shadow's voice came warped through the enchantment, flattened and breathy. "You requested an account."

"I did." Elara took her seat again, folded her hands. "Tell me about the node."

A pause. The kind that precedes confessions.

"Compromised. The seal lattice beneath the eastern dormitory was disturbed three nights ago — substantially. We reviewed the resonance logs. The puncture event matches no registered technique in our records."

"Continue."

"It was destabilized." The shadow's warped voice did something delicate — a man choosing his words as if they were tripwires. "And then it was *re-sealed.* By the same agent. Within minutes. The node is quiet, Vice-Headmistress. Quieter than it was before the disturbance. The entity's host is—" another pause, "—*calm.* We lost two years of graduated weakening in a single night. The schedule was a controlled release in the third year. Now the lattice is *healthier* than our baseline."

"Mm." Elara turned a page in the thin little file. "A boy named Lucen Gray was present at the incident. Dormitory 3 resident. Survivor."

"We're aware of him. There's— there is something wrong with the readings around him, Vice-Headmistress. The instruments don't fail. They *decline.* They measure him and report... nothing. Less than nothing. A hole in the data where a person should be."

"Yes," Elara said warmly. "I know. I've verified it personally. Mana Index: zero-point-zero-zero-nine. Practically negative space."

She folded her hands atop his photograph.

"An anti-node."

The shadow in the mirror went very still.

"That's — that's theoretical, Vice-Headmistress. The old texts speak of the concept in four lines, total. A balancing mechanism. The field producing an absence to counter a surplus. There's never been a *confirmed*—"

"There is one now," Elara said. "He is sitting in Homeroom 1-A as we speak, seat 7-F, by the window. I watched him at luncheon. He dropped his spoon twice and glared at a raven."

"The world's immune response," the shadow breathed. "The texts say—"

"—that when the world generates an anti-node, it is *fighting* the Ending. Yes. Which makes him a threat." Elara opened her fingers over the photograph and looked down at the unremarkable gray-eyed boy gazing blankly up at her, and her voice went soft in that way it had, the way that made students bring her their failures and grandmothers bring her their grief. "Or a catalyst."

"Vice-Headmistress?"

"Think, now." Her tone was patient. The tone she used on first-years struggling with channeling basics. "What does a void *do,* in proximity to a seal?"

"It... suppresses. Dampens. It starves the lattice of resonance."

"On instinct. Yes. Unconsciously. A child's grip." She tapped the page once, gently, right on Lucen Gray's unremarkable forehead. "But inversion is written in the same four lines of text you're quoting at me, my friend. A void, *turned,* doesn't starve a seal. It inverts it. The seal's whole structure depends on polarity. Introduce a guided anti-node and you can turn the lock backward. What two years of ritual weakening would accomplish..." Her eyes half-lidded in something that looked, from outside, like tenderness and was actually arithmetic. "...he could accomplish in an afternoon. Holding the entity's hand, unaware, humming a song."

The shadow was very quiet. Conspirators go quiet when the plan gets beautiful, the same way prey goes quiet at the scent of the tiger.

"Then we should recruit him," the shadow finally said.

"We should *guide* him." The correction came mild as milk. "Recruitment implies a choice, and choices can be refused, and refusal would be a tragedy — for him most of all. No. He'll make the right choice naturally, once he understands what the world truly is."

She turned another page in the file. There was barely anything. An orphan's sparse biography. A boy who arrived at Stellaris with nothing, knew nothing, was told nothing.

"He doesn't know what he is," she said softly. "Can you see it from the records? No one has ever told him. The world's immune response, walking around in shoes that were handed down, sneaking onto rooftops, flinching from the Heir of the North—" a warm, sad little laugh escaped her, "—*flinching,* my friend. The only creature in this iteration built to silence the Abyss, and he spends his days terrified of making eye contact with his classmates."

"It sounds like he wants to be ordinary."

"The ordinary always want to be ordinary. That's what makes the role a burden." Elara closed the file. "That is precisely why he needs someone kind. Someone who will tell him the truth gently. Someone he can trust."

She smiled at the mirror.

"He should have a mentor."

The sub-levels of Stellaris Royal Academy were older than the Academy.

Older than the capital, older than the kingdom — if the stone's whisper was to be trusted, older than the *calendar.* Elara walked it alone, unhurried, her heels making soft, regular sounds on the ancient seal-etched floor.

She knew the way in the dark.

She'd walked it hundreds of times, lecturing and humming to the thing below the same way she hummed to her plants — and why not? The node was a garden bed, in its way. It held a seed.

The corridor opened into the chamber.

And there, in the center of all that carved and consecrated stone, the node hunched — a great dark crystal the color of a wound at night, faintly luminous from within, wrapped in seal-geometry that pulsed with slow, exhausted light.

Elara stopped at the perimeter and looked at it the way one looks at a sleeping parent's face.

Then her eyes found the mark.

A handprint.

Burned into the crystal's face — not mana-scorch, not ritual etching. An *absence.* A negative. Five fingers and a palm, rendered in total void, like a hole punched through the concept of the node itself. Where the hand had touched, the crystal's inner light was simply... gone. Not dimmed. *Absent.*

The world's immune response had leaned on the Abyss — probably frightened, probably bleeding, probably doing it purely by instinct while trying to rescue some idiot classmate — and the Abyss had closed its eye in submission, and then the same hand had helped put it *back* to sleep.

"Oh, Lucen," she breathed, and there was genuine maternal ache in it. "You poor, extraordinary child. Nobody even told you what you *did.*"

She stepped over the perimeter line — the seals didn't register her; they hadn't in years — and approached the node.

Then she raised her hand and placed her palm over the void-print.

The node resonated.

Not with her mana. Her mana was irrelevant here, and gratefully so. It resonated with a frequency she'd spent two decades cultivating — the ritual cadence of the Church, the little tuning-fork wound in the world's side that she'd been taught to keep vibrating. The node's dark surface rippled beneath her hand like deep water acknowledging gravity.

"Not yet," she whispered.

Her voice broke a little on it, because sincerity is a terrible instrument.

"But soon."

She looked down at the handprint beneath her palm — hers, superimposed over his, the believer over the void — and felt something close to courtship. A plan settling into its final shape the way a key settles into a lock it was always forged for.

"And you, Lucen Gray, will help me."

She smiled at the node, warm and certain.

"Whether you know it or not."

---

The climb back up took eleven minutes.

Elara spent them thinking about the boy's mentorship curriculum with the thoroughness of a gardener planning a trellis. He'd need context first — gentled context. The truth about his nature, delivered with sympathy. Then the truth about the world, delivered in portions. Then, when he was ready — and only when he was ready — an introduction to the node, framed as *rescue.* Of course a kind boy would want to rescue the world.

They always did.

She sat down at her desk while the afternoon light went honey-gold, and she drew a memorandum form toward her, and she wrote in a hand that had graded ten thousand essays:

> **MEMORANDUM — TO THE OFFICE OF THE HEADMASTER**
>
> *Re: Special Mentorship Program, Second Semester.*
>
> *In light of the recent dormitory incident and observed anomalous developments in First-Year class standing, I respectfully recommend the following student be assigned to a supervised individual development program under my personal tutelage, commencing next semester:*
>
> **GRAY, LUCEN — HOMEROOM 1-A — SEAT 7-F.**
>
> *The student exhibits exceptional, undocumented irregularities in his mana constitution. Without structured guidance, there is meaningful risk of harm — to himself, and to the Academy. I volunteer my office for this charge personally.*
>
> *— Elara Voss, Vice-Headmistress*

She read it over once.

"He will be safe with me," she murmured, signing it. "I will be so good to him."

She meant that too.

She sealed it, rang the courier bell, and watched a secretary's aide carry it down the hall toward the Headmaster's tower.

It came back in fifty-three minutes. The Headmaster — a tired man who trusted Voss the way the whole Academy trusted Voss, because Voss had been kind at him for thirty years and kindness is the longest con — had scrawled his approval at the bottom in hasty blue ink.

**Approved. Begin preparations.**

Elara set the memorandum in Lucen Gray's file, on top of his photograph, and closed the folder gently, the way one closes a book they plan to enjoy very much.

"Poor boy," she said to the quiet office, to Roderick the succulent, to the sleeping sun.

"He's been alone so long."
