She wasn't there anymore.

One step — *one step* — inside his arc, a turn of the wrist, and the flat of her blade touched his throat while his sword was still committing to a future that was no longer going to happen.

No one applauded. No one made a sound. You don't applaud a landslide.

'Stay away from her,' I told myself, adding it to the mental notebook, entry 4-2. 'Stay away from her forever. She is final-boss content wearing school clothes.'

'Okay. Inventory check, Min-jun style. Realistic analysis.'

The objective was: lose *acceptably*. Don't get injured. Don't get humiliated badly enough to trend. Don't do anything interesting. Be the beige wallpaper of first-year combat class, get slapped once on the ribcage, bow, go home, sew notebook.

A clean, controlled, forgettable failure.

"Begin."

Here's the thing about *Eschaton Hearts* that I never appreciated until this exact moment.

The combat was *animation-locked*. That was the game design philosophy. Every enemy attack had wind-up frames, active frames, recovery frames — and the dodge system wasn't a roll or a teleport. It was *positioning*. Step back at forty-five degrees. Pivot the back foot. Let the blow pass your shoulder. Reset center.

I had done it ten thousand times. Controller in hand, chips on chest, spamming the advanced footwork the game taught you in the level-40 tutorial because anything less got you killed in the Ashen Legion end-game dungeons.

You don't forget that. Not really. It's muscle memory that lives somewhere deeper than muscle.

So when Erik's sword came in, my body — this underfed fifteen-year-old noodle of a body — stepped back at forty-five degrees, pivoted the back foot, and let the blade whisper past my shoulder with maybe four centimeters of daylight.

The swing hit air.

My center was already reset. Weight balanced. Facing him.

Erik blinked.

I blinked harder.

'...What did my legs just do.'
