# Chapter 28: Decryption Session #5: The Princess Comes to the Infirmary

Day 20. Sunday.

The infirmary smelled like medicinal herbs and regret.

Mostly regret.

'I have officially become an infirmary regular.'

I stared up at the enchanted ceiling, which displayed a soothing illusion of drifting clouds designed to calm patients.

It wasn't working.

Twenty days. That's all it took. Twenty days at Stellaris Royal Academy and I'd already earned myself an overnight stay in a hospital bed with both hands wrapped up like a mummy who's lost a fight with a very angry stove.

```
[System: Hand Damage Status Report]

[Right Hand — Channel Burns: Healing. 62% recovered.]
[Left Hand — Fractures: Healing. 58% recovered.]
[Treatment: Accelerated Restoration Magic (Applied ×3)]
[Estimated Full Recovery: 4-6 Days]

[Note: The attending healer described your injuries as 'fascinatingly impossible' and went to write a paper.]
```

Fantastic. I was going to be a case study.

'Channel burns with no detectable mana channel.'

Yeah, good luck publishing that one without getting laughed out of the Alchemy Society.

My Mana Index was 0.009 out of 100,000. An enchanted wooden training dummy had 0.01. A *plank of wood* was more magical than me, and yet my hands looked like I'd spent the night arm-wrestling a lightning elemental.

'Because I stuck my hand into a void-sealed containment array and got my lifeforce scraped across the inside of an ancient void-node like butter on toast.'

But sure. Call it 'fascinatingly impossible.'

...

The thing that had me actually sweating wasn't my hands, though.

It was Voss.

Vice-Headmistress Elara Voss. The kind, gentle, warm-smiling administrator who was absolutely, one hundred percent connected to Eclipse Veil, and who now knew that something with *no mana signature* had interfered with her node.

I'd dealt with her before. I'd cauterized our conversation with a loaded book-throwing incident like a man cauterizing a wound with gunpowder.

It had bought me time.

But time was running out, and now she'd come down to that sub-level and put her fingers on that crystal and found—

'My fingerprints.'

Metaphorically speaking. The fingerprints of nothing. The distinctive residue of something that shouldn't exist poking around where the Church's secrets slept.

I was a cockroach that had crawled across the dinner table at exactly the wrong moment.

'Why do I not believe her.'

The door opened.

Not the side door the attendants used. The *main* door. The one with the polished brass handle and the quiet hinge.

I turned my head.

And forgot how to breathe properly for a second.

...

Iris Aurelia stood in the doorway.

Grey eyes that had stopped wars. Silver-blonde hair so perfectly ordered it looked unconstitutional on a Sunday. The Stellaris uniform, because of course she was in uniform even on a rest day — that coat stayed buttoned to the top like it was sworn to national defense.

She was carrying the notebook.

*My* notebook.

And a bag.

A large, insulated, kitchen-staff-emblazoned bag that smelled, unmistakably, of food.

She looked at me.

I looked at her.

Neither of us said anything for approximately three full seconds, which in Iris Aurelia interaction time was roughly equivalent to a thirty-minute silence with a normal person.

"Your session is Tuesday," I said.

"I rescheduled."

She said it the way she reported river levels to the Imperial government. Like *I rescheduled* was a meteorological fact that had simply occurred, requiring no further explanation.

She crossed the infirmary, pulled the bedside chair up with her foot, and sat.

The notebook went on her lap. The bag went on my bedside table.

She began unpacking.

A container of soup — still steaming. Bread, soft, the kind that tore instead of crunched. A small dish of sliced fruit, gold apples by the look of them, cut into little precise wedges that were clearly the work of someone who believed fruit should obey geometric principles. And tea, in a sealed flask.

"There's no need—" I started.

"You haven't eaten since last night."

"How do you know that?"

"The attendant. I dismissed her." Iris glanced around the empty ward as if to confirm her own handiwork. No one else there. It was just us. "I told her I'd take responsibility for the morning."

'She dismissed the medical staff.'

'She just... dismissed them.'

'Because she's a princess and they listened.'

"I can't eat all this," I said.

"You'll eat what I brought." She unstopped the flask and poured tea with military precision. "Open your mouth."

I stared at her.

"Are you going to *feed* me?"

"Your hands are bandaged."

Yes. Yes, they were. She was not incorrect. She was technically, factually, demonstrably correct.

But there was a difference between being correct and — this.

She loaded up a spoonful of soup, lifted it, and held it out with the expression of a general executing a flanking maneuver. Focused. Deliberate. Coldly committed.

Her cheeks were slightly pink.

Her hand was absolutely steady.

'This is the insanely absurd one, right?'

'This is the absurd survival situation the universe keeps giving me?'

'Iris Aurelia. First Princess of the Aureline Empire. Future Dawn Tyrant. The girl who, in the game, condemned entire bloodlines by age twenty-two and smiled about it. This woman. Is about to feed me soup.'

"In the game," I said before I could stop myself, "I have never cooked for anyone."

She raised an eyebrow. "In your village?"

'Smooth, Lucen. Your village. The one that conveniently explains everything you know.'

I opened my mouth.

She put the spoon in.

The soup was good. Shockingly good. Rich broth, something earthy like root vegetables, a hint of herbs I couldn't name.

She fed me a second spoon. A third. Fourth. She had a rhythm now. Load, raise, deliver, pause while I swallow.

Iris Aurelia, first princess of the Empire, future Dawn Tyrant, fed a provincial student soup in an academy infirmary on a Sunday afternoon.

Neither of us commented on the absurdity.

Because there is simply nothing to be said about certain things.

'I am the most confusing thing that has ever happened to her political career,' I thought with total clarity. 'She is sitting in an infirmary. Spoon-feeding a boy with 0.009 mana index. On a Sunday morning. After rescheduling his session.'

She handed me a piece of bread. I took it in my bandaged fingers — which could hold soft things, thanks to the healing magic — and chewed.

'Careful,' I told myself. 'You fell for a dragon. You know how stories like this go. [Bad End No. 11. Bad End No. 19. Bad End No. 28. Bad End No. 44.] Feeling things about the heroine is how the game gets you killed.'

I chewed my bread.

It was very good bread.

...

After the meal — and it was a meal, I'd eaten everything, including the little geometric apples — the notebook came up.

My stomach tightened.

Not because of the notebook. Because of what always came with the notebook.

*The contents.*

The seven years of private Korean writing. My analysis. My notes. Every theory and observation and snide remark I'd put down in Hangul because Hangul was the safest language in the world — nobody in this world could read it.

Except her.

She sat back, crossed one leg over the other, opened the notebook on her knee.

"The fifth session," she said. "We'll work through your recent additions."

"Recent additions?"

"You wrote in it two nights ago."

'Oh, right. Before the library incident. My notes about Alice.'

Iris found the page. Turned it to me. Her finger found a line of characters — the characters Kang Min-jun had written at his desk in a dorm room on the other side of two universes and one reincarnation.

She began to read them aloud.

Her tongue processed Hangul like she was disarming a bomb. Slow. Steady. Every syllable placed, not dropped.

Her accent wasn't Korean. It was Imperial. Every vowel came out slightly sharper, slightly more formal.

But it was recognizably, unmistakably —

Korean.

My Korean.

Spoken out loud in the quiet infirmary by the future heir to the Aureline throne.

It was the strangest sound I'd ever heard in either of my lives.

'I think I've felt weirder before in my life. But less *fondly* weird, I think.'

"This," she said, tapping the line. "The one you wrote two days ago. You wrote something different. The characters at the end are different. They look — colder."

'Colder.'

She was looking at the section of my notes about Alice.

'Don't translate this one,' my survival instincts screamed. 'This is classified. This is Church-redacted. This is how people get disappeared.'

"Translate them for me," Iris said. Not an order. Just the flat tone that meant she already had my decision made before I made it. "As you always do."

"It's a — folk tale."

She waited.

"A folk tale from my village," I said, picking the words carefully. "About a girl."

"What about her."

"She has a winter spirit inside her."

The words landed and then just... lay there.

Iris didn't blink.

She looked at me the way a hawk looks at movement in the grass. Calm. Patient. Total.

"A girl with a spirit inside her," she repeated.

"It's a common folk tale. In my village."

'Why did I say village? I've said village so many times she probably thinks it's a geographical fact and not a placeholder for [REDACTED].'

"Celestia Rimehart was admitted to this infirmary last night," Iris said.

She delivered it like reading out the weather report.

'Oh shit.'

"Same time as you," she continued. "She arrived just after you did. She collapsed. Something about — overexertion."

'Ice. She iced my hands. I remember the cold spreading through my fractures.'

"She was in the library incident. With you."

"She was my lab partner. She was there when it happened."

"I've read her file," Iris said. "She has an unusual magical signature."

'Don't say anything.'

"It's heavily redacted by the Church."

'Don't. Say. Anything.'

I didn't say anything.

Silence. Quality silence. The kind that costs money.

Iris looked at the notebook on her lap. Then up at me.

"Your folk tale," she said. Her voice had dropped a notch lower. "About a girl with a winter spirit inside her."

Wind moved through the infirmary curtains. The enchanted clouds drifted overhead, calm and indifferent.

"Is it about *her*?"

'Well.'

'Guess what, cheeks.'

'I can't answer this.'

If I said yes — if I confirmed that my "village folklore" contained intelligence about a classified student, a student the Church had manually redacted — I confirmed that my whole village story was compromised. That I *knew things*. That I was an intelligence asset with access to sealed information.

If I said no, Iris would hear the lie. She was built for hearing lies. It was her primary skill tree. Swordsmanship, governance, lie detection — her stats were embarrassing.

"It's a folk tale," I said.

Iris looked at me for a very long moment.

She did not believe me.

I knew she didn't believe me.

She knew I knew she didn't believe me.

But she didn't push.

Instead — the thing she did that made my stomach drop into my shoes — she nodded slowly.

The most dangerous thing Iris Aurelia could do was *not* press a question when she suspected the answer.

Because it meant she'd already accepted the answer, and was now filing it away.

She marked the page.

Little quarter-inch mark at the top corner, made with her thumbnail.

"I'll revisit it," she said.

'She's not dropping it. She's postponing it.'

'That's worse.'

...

The session went on. Two hours total. Iris worked through the notebook with military discipline. Words, phrases, structures. She asked questions about grammar, about syntax, about the little particles I'd sprinkled throughout my writing like seasoning.

Nine times out of ten, I translated normal stuff. Recipe notes. Observations about weather. The analysis of mana crystal degradation rates.

But every so often, the notebook drifted back toward that page.

The page about Alice. The page about the girl with the winter spirit inside her.

Iris kept *not* asking about it.

And every time, I noticed her not asking.

'She's the type of person who's never once failed to get what she wanted. She is Athena wrapped in Armani. She outmaneuvered half the Imperial court before she turned seventeen. And she is voluntarily NOT asking about the thing she most wants to know.'

'Because of me.'

'Because asking risks my safety.'

'That is—'

"Your eyes are doing the thing," Iris said.

"What thing."

"The thing they do when you're calculating a threat assessment." She was looking at me over the notebook. "Stop it. I'm not attacking."

"You are literally the most dangerous person in this school."

"And yet, here I am." She put the notebook down. Crossed her arms. "Doing nothing threatening."

"...you have my notebook."

"And I'm *not* asking about the girl in the story," she said, cool as mountain air. "You can stop doing the calculations."

I stared at her.

She met my stare.

Outside, the light was changing. The afternoon was bleeding away. The infirmary windows, which faced east, caught the last direct sun and let the rest fall into something dim and golden.

'I've never seen her sit this still,' I thought. 'I've never seen her *choose* to not know something.'

'It's costing her something.'

'That's the part I don't know what to do with.'

...

Two hours, and the light in the room told the truth.

She gathered the notebook. Set it in her bag with the empty food containers. Stood.

I waited for her to say "Until Tuesday" or "Rest, Lucen" or any of a dozen normal Iris things.

Instead she looked at me for a long, still moment.

"Lucen."

"Yes?"

"In the sub-levels. When you touched whatever stopped the incident." Her voice was perfectly controlled. "Your hands."

'I know what she wants to ask. How did a boy with no mana stop a void-node event that the Church has been watching for years? I know EXACTLY what she wants to ask.'

'But that's not what she says.'

"I see them when you walk into a room," Iris said slowly. "Your hands. Everyone's hands, really. I'm trained to read hands. Yours are always steady."

'Oh.'

"Until they weren't."

She walked around the foot of the bed. Around to my right side. Where my wrapped hand lay on top of the blanket, pale and still.

She reached down.

And lifted my hand.

It wasn't clinical. It wasn't a healer's touch or a general's assessment of a soldier's wound.

It was just her fingers, holding my bandaged hand, turning it very gently, looking at the wrappings like something she was cataloguing.

Not intelligence. Not evidence.

*Damage.*

Damage to something she —

'Don't finish that thought. Don't. DO NOT — '

"These hands are your most valuable tool," Iris said, and her voice was quiet. Almost — not quite, but almost — soft. "For the translations. For your studies. For everything you'll do at this academy."

She set the hand down.

Back on the blanket. Arranged like it was something that deserved care.

Her fingers lingered for roughly 1.5 seconds longer than they needed to.

Then she stepped back.

"Don't break them again."

She didn't wait for an answer. She picked up her bag. Walked to the door. Paused with her hand on the brass handle.

She didn't turn around.

"Tuesday," she said.

"Tuesday," I agreed, to the door.

The door closed.

...

I sat there in the dimming infirmary, alone, and held my own hand.

The one she'd held.

My heart was doing something.

It was not doing anything useful, or productive, or conducive to my survival. My heart was doing something profoundly stupid.

'I'm the one with the psychological profile,' I thought. 'The full route map. Every flag. Every weakness. Her traumatic history with her mother's assassination. Her fear of emotional vulnerability. The six death flags attached to her route. The two harem-route flags. The specific dialogue choice tree that leads to the tyrant ending— I know ALL of this. I have completed her route nine times.'

'I was supposed to be immune.'

'I was supposed to be the one guy on earth who could look at Iris Aurelia and not — '

'Not—'

'I want to die.'

'Again. For the third time. In three weeks.'

I sat in the dim infirmary, holding my own hand, feeling the ghost of her fingers on the bandages she'd been so careful with.

'Distant early warning,' the System had said.

Which meant — it was far away. Not yet. Not now.

Still.

I knew how this worked. I'd seen it a hundred times in the game. Distant warnings had a way of becoming close ones, and close ones had a way of becoming permanent ones.

The good news was, I was aware of it.

The bad news was — being aware of the trap had never once saved anyone who'd already fallen into it.

'Sunrise Eternal,' I thought, testing the name.

I didn't know what it meant yet.

But I knew, with the bone-deep certainty of a man who'd played nine full routes, that it meant Iris Aurelia, a sunrise, and something that someone didn't come back from.

'Don't break them again,' she'd said.

'Don't.'

The light in the infirmary had almost completely gone.
