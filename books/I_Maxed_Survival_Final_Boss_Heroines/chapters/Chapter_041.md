# Chapter 41: Rhea's Ultimatum

Sleep is a beautiful fiction.

I know this because I did not experience any last night.

I lay in my dorm bed from midnight to four in the morning, staring at the ceiling, replaying the System's closing message on a loop like a [SEVERELY DAMAGED] save file.

*She has kept the recipe exactly the same ever since.*

Four hundred years of butterscotch. Offered to a boy with less mana than a doorstop.

"Yeah," I whispered to the dark. "Totally normal Tuesday."

Then I remembered it was Wednesday.

Then I remembered what Wednesdays meant.

Dawn training. Eastern training grounds. Rhea Valtor, heiress to the disgraced Ashenveil legacy, professional human alarm clock, and — per the System's deeply unhelpful tracking — the holder of a steadily degrading [Bad End No. 34: The Severed Crown].

'Great. Fantastic. Love that for me.'

I got up. I put on my training clothes. I walked out into the 5 AM dark of Stellaris Royal Academy's eastern grounds, where the frost was still holding the grass like a grudge, and where a five-foot-nothing disaster (0.009 mana, 100,000 needed, do the math and then laugh) was expected to swing a wooden sword until sunrise.

And Rhea was already there.

Of course she was.

She was always already there. I had never once beaten her to the training grounds. I was starting to suspect she slept standing up with her eyes open, like a shark.

But today was different.

I knew it in the first three seconds.

Rhea Valtor was a creature of ritual precision. Thirty stretches. Forty practice cuts. Every session identical, every motion the same, a routine polished smoother than river stone. Even her *breathing* had a schedule.

Today, she wasn't stretching.

She was standing at the center of the sparring circle, practice sword resting against her shoulder, eyes fixed on the eastern horizon where the sun was still deciding whether it wanted to exist.

She didn't turn when I arrived.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm three minutes early."

"You're late," she repeated, flatly, "for what I wanted today."

'Okay. Okay, no. That's a boss fight line. That's the line the NPC says before the arena music starts and the health bar unfurls across the bottom of the screen.'

"Nothing. Let's train."

I picked up my practice sword from the rack.

That was my first mistake of the day.

The spar began the way it always did.

Ashenveil Opening Form — *The Candle in the Field* — a guard so humble it looked like surrender. Rhea had drilled it into my bones for weeks. Step. Pivot. Deflect. Her sword tapped against mine with that familiar, almost gentle rhythm—

And then she hit me so hard I saw my ancestors.

"AAAGHK—"

I went down. Not gracefully. Not like a swordsman. I went down the way a folding chair goes down — all angles and no dignity.

I stared up at the pre-dawn sky from the frost.

"...We tap in practice," I said to the clouds. "That is the rule. That was the rule for four weeks. We. Tap."

"Get up."

"Rhea—"

"Get *up*, Lucen."

I got up.

She didn't wait for me to find my stance.

The second exchange, she came in fast — *The Falling Leaf*, the disarming strike, her father's favorite — and I knew this one, I *knew* it, my feet were already starting the fox-step she'd beaten into my muscle memory—

She changed tempo midway.

Changed. Mid-strike.

'She never changes mid-strike. The whole Ashenveil philosophy is commitment. She lectured me for twenty minutes about commitment. I have notes. I HAVE ACTUAL NOTES.'

Her blunt wooden blade stopped one centimeter from my throat.

"Dead," she said.

I stared at the blade.

"You hesitated," she said.

"You switched tempo mid—"

"A battlefield doesn't care about my form drills, Lucen."

She stepped back. Lowered the sword.

And something in her jaw was doing a thing. A tight thing. The kind of thing a jaw does when the person attached to it is holding a door shut against something very large.

"Again," she said.

Third exchange. I lasted nine seconds. Fourth, eleven. Fifth time she caught my wrist, torqued it exactly the way she'd taught me to avoid, and put me in the frost again.

"Dead."

"Observ— I said observed, ow."

"Get up."

The sun came up while I practiced the most sophisticated technique in my arsenal: getting knocked down at increasing velocities.

By the seventh fall, the horizon was molten gold, my ribs were composing a formal complaint, and somewhere in the back of my skull, my old-world gamer brain — the part that had 347 hours of Eschaton Hearts burned into it like a brand — started screaming a diagnosis.

'This isn't training.'

'This is a cutscene.'

'This is the part of the route where the combat heroine stops sanitizing her emotions and the player is presented with a dialogue tree where every option is labeled [DO NOT SELECT].'

Eighth exchange.

She came in high, I slipped low — the fox-step, perfect, executed purely on terror-powered muscle memory — and for half a second I actually gained the inside line.

Her eyes widened.

Just slightly.

Then my own foot betrayed me on the frost, because I am a 0.019 BEP statistical error with shoes, and I sat down hard on the frozen earth.

Silence.

Rhea stood over me, breathing hard, sword loose in her grip.

And then she did something she had never done in four weeks of dawn training.

She stopped.

She slid her practice blade through her belt loop. Real slow. Deliberate. Like a person ending not just a spar but an era.

The frost creaked under me.

The sun cleared the treeline and hit her face, and I could finally see her eyes, and—

'Oh no.'

'Oh, *that's* worse than the sword.'

Her eyes were steady the way a drawn bowstring is steady.

"Who taught you the Ashenveil Retreat?" she asked.

The question.

The question with four weeks of rust on it.

"The Ashenveil Retreat, Lucen. The Crossing of Embers. The Candle in the Field." Her voice was very, very controlled. A person handling live explosives with bare hands. "My father wrote those. My father's soldiers carried them. Every man and woman who walked them into fire died in the Purge. That is the *history.* That is the official record, carved into a memorial I have stood in front of every year since I was nine."

She took one step closer.

"You don't do them like someone who read about them."

"...Rhea—"

"Stop."

I stopped.

Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrifying part. It stayed level, and level meant she was past shouting, past anger, past all the temperature ranges where things could still be fixed.

"You've used my father's techniques. You've learned my family's legacy — *faster* than me, in places, which I should hate you for, and I don't, which is its own problem." A breath. In, out. Measured. "You've trained with me at dawn for weeks. You've eaten my terrible ration bread. You let me knock you into the dirt seventy times without once making me feel cruel for it."

My mouth opened.

Nothing came out.

Because what was the line here? What was the dialogue option? *[(1) I learned it from a video game where I romanced you thirty times to unlock the bad endings.]*

Yeah.

Game over. Instant. Skip the cutscene.

"And you won't tell me the one thing that matters," she said.

The wind moved through the training grounds.

Somewhere far away, an early bell rang across Stellaris, bright and innocent and completely oblivious.

"I need you to hear what I've figured out," Rhea said. "I need to say it out loud, just once, so that you understand I am not stupid, Lucen. I have never been stupid. I've just been *hoping.*"

"Your footwork," Rhea said, "was perfect on the first day we met. Perfect. Not talented — *perfect.* Textbook. The way my father described his veterans moving after a decade of war."

She started pacing. One line in the frost, back and forth, not looking at me now, looking at the sunrise like it owed her money.

"But your body was untrained. Weak. No calluses. No muscle. No instinct for pain. That's impossible, Lucen — footwork at that level takes years of repetition. Unless."

She stopped.

"Unless someone showed you. Not trained you — *showed* you. Demonstrated it. Over and over, while you watched and memorized like a man dying of thirst memorizing the shape of water."

Oh no.

Oh, she's building the argument.

She's building it and it's *beautiful* and it's completely wrong.

"A survivor," Rhea said. Her voice cracked, just a thread of a crack, on the second word. "Someone from the Seventh Legion who lived through the Purge. Injured. Hiding. My father's men were hunted for months after his execution — it's *documented* that two squads were never accounted for. Their bodies were never found. Everyone assumes they died in the passes."

She turned to face me.

Her eyes were wet.

Rhea Valtor's eyes were wet, standing in a frost-covered training ground at dawn, and the sight of it hit me harder than her practice sword ever had.

"But what if one of them didn't die, Lucen? What if one crawled out? A soldier who knew the whole Ashenveil Form, broken and burned and hiding in some village, and a strange, impossible boy crossed his path, and the old soldier thought — *I can't let it die with me. I'll show the boy. One time. A hundred times. Until the form lives somewhere outside my corpse.*"

"Rhea—"

"And a survivor would be paranoid. A survivor would swear the boy to secrecy." Her voice was climbing now, the control fraying thread by thread. "Wouldn't he? *Especially* if the boy was coming here. To this academy. Where the people who ordered the Purge still have their banners hanging in the great hall."

I stood very still.

Because she had built it.

She had built the one explanation — the *only* explanation — that didn't just excuse my silence. It *sanctified* it. A promise to a dying soldier. Loyalty to the hunted. The single most sacred virtue in the entire Valtor worldview, handcrafted and gift-wrapped, and all I had to do was let her keep believing it.

One nod.

That was all. One nod, and she would forgive everything, hold me closer, even. Probably cry. Possibly hug me. Definitely explode the route flags in ways that would make my survival probability do interesting interpretive dance moves.

But there was no soldier.

There was a seventeen-year-old with a save-addiction, hunched over a screen in Seoul at 3 AM, grinding a movement puzzle in a dungeon called the Ashen Catacombs because the wiki said it unlocked a hidden epilogue.

That was the sacred transmission.

Me, three energy drinks deep, yelling "I SWEAR IF I FAIL THIS JUMP ONE MORE TIME—" at my monitor.

"You don't have to give me a name," Rhea whispered.

"Just tell me they existed. Tell me someone lived, Lucen. Tell me my father's soldiers didn't all die forgotten in the snow." Tell me *anything* that is true, and I swear on the Ashenveil name I will never ask the rest."

Silence.

Total silence. Even the birds had the decency to shut up.

I opened my mouth.

And nothing came out.

Because there was no soldier, and I couldn't invent him — inventing him was worse than silence, a lie like that would eventually metastasize — and the truth was *a video game, Rhea, your entire family's legacy is a skill tree and I've seen you die eleven different ways and I cried at two of them.*

Two.

And it's not mine to—" I heard the sentence die as it left my mouth, because it was the exact sentence a man keeping a soldier's secret would say.
