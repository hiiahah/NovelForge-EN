# Chapter 14: Decryption Session #2: Controlled Detonation

I spent three hours rehearsing lies in my dormitory.

Three hours.

That's longer than most studio games take to beat on Any%.

I stood in front of my cracked mirror, holding my cursed diary, and recited fake translations to my own reflection like a man preparing for a dissertation defense in front of a committee that executes failures.

"The herbal remedy passage references mountain ginseng harvested in the third moon," I told Mirror-Me. "Not the Empress's sleep schedule. Mountain. Ginseng."

Mirror-Me looked terrified.

"She's not going to believe you," Mirror-Me seemed to say.

"Shut up," I told him. "You have bed hair and your left cheek still has bruising from where a war criminal in a school uniform hit you with a wooden sword six times at dawn."

It had actually been seventeen strikes, but I was choosing not to think about the exact tally.

Friday, 4:47 in the afternoon. Competency Exam: four days away. Rhea Valtor: scheduled to beat me senseless every morning until then. Iris Aurelia: scheduled to interrogate me over tea in thirteen minutes.

Last time, I'd improvised.

This time, I had come prepared.

This time, I was the one controlling the narrative.

This time, nothing could go wrong.

I want it on record that I recognized, in the moment, that thinking "nothing could go wrong" was the single most dangerous thought a living creature could produce.

I thought it anyway.

Because I'm an idiot.

The Imperial Wing corridor smelled like old wax and money.

I knocked twice on the door of Private Study 3, exactly as I'd been instructed by a servant this morning, along with detailed directions and a warning not to touch anything gold.

*Everything in this wing is gold.*

"Enter."

I pushed the door open.

The study was the same as last time — shelves of old books, the heavy desk, the lamps burning low. And Iris Aurelia sat behind the desk in imperial blue, haloed by lamplight, with her black hair loose over one shoulder and my notebook already open in front of her.

She'd gotten here early.

To a session she'd scheduled.

To interrogate me.

*She prepared too. This is a boss that pre-buffs before you enter the room.*

"Lucen Gray." Molten gold eyes lifted. "You're on time."

"Your Highness. I live in constant fear of disappointing you, so punctuality was the only option."

One brow rose — the faintest ghost of something that, in a person capable of expressions, might've been amusement.

"Sit."

I sat.

And then I saw the tea.

Two cups. Steam curling. One in front of her, one placed precisely at my seat, the porcelain warm before I'd even touched it, which meant it had been poured in anticipation of me arriving on time.

It was a pale gold-amber color, with two small white blossoms floating like tiny shipwrecked moons.

I knew this tea.

God help me, I *knew* this tea.

Marigold and chrysanthemum, brewed light, with exactly one spoon of honey.

"Something wrong?" Iris asked.

"...No," I said. "It just. Smells familiar."

"I see."

She said nothing else. She lifted her cup and drank, watching me over the rim with the unhurried patience of an apex predator that had already decided when dinner was.

I drank.

It was exactly right.

Of course it was exactly right. Because last session, when she'd pressed me about the "old script," I'd mentioned — *casually, defensively, like an idiot* — that one passage described a tea. A tea a mother used to make for her daughter, marigold and chrysanthemum with honey, brewed on the first cold night of autumn.

I'd described it because I knew it.

I knew it because it was Iris's route backstory. Her dead mother's tea. The detail appeared in Chapter 2 of her route, in the grief scene, the one I'd watched six times across six playthroughs.

And now the Dawn Tyrant had brewed her dead mother's tea from my description.

And she was drinking it.

While looking at me.

'Abort. ABORT. This session has already escaped containment and I haven't even sat down properly.'

For the record: the first hour went *perfectly*.

I want that acknowledged. Whatever happens later in this chapter, whatever catastrophe unfolds, I want the annals of history to record that for sixty full minutes, Lucen Gray executed a flawless operation.

She'd point at a passage. I'd consult my sleeve notes with an expression of scholarly effort. I'd deliver a fake translation. She'd cross-check it against last session's translations for internal consistency.

Everything matched.

Because last session's translations were also fake, and I'd spent Tuesday night building a concordance of my own lies.

I had invented a grammar for my own panic.

"The second passage," I said, tracing a block of Korean text that was actually my notes on exam venue security rotations, "describes an herbal poultice. Dried wolfsbane root, warm water, applied to swelling. My grandmother—" I caught myself, redirected, "—the, ah, the women in my village used it for bruising."

Iris wrote it down.

*The future Empress of the continent is taking notes on imaginary folk medicine from a Korean gamer's exam-security annotations. If there's an afterlife, I hope someone shows me the highlight reel.*

"Your village," she said. "You haven't described it."

Careful.

*This isn't in the script. She's off-script. Why is she off-script—*

"Not much to describe, Your Highness."

"Indulge me."

There it was. That tone. Not a request — a door being closed behind me, gently, by someone holding all the keys.

I had prepared for this. Not this exact question, but the category. I'd built the cover story during Tuesday's sleepless night, cross-referenced against everything I knew about Imperial intelligence records from the game's lore codex.

"Small fishing village," I said. "Northern coast. A week past the Ironwood Marches by cart. Maybe two hundred people. No lord's manor, no garrison, nothing worth taxing twice. We didn't even have a proper name — maps just called it 'the settlement.'"

All true details of a place that didn't exist. The beauty of that region was that it was real — sparsely mapped, dotted with coastal hamlets that appeared and vanished with storms and bad harvest years. Imperial records of the northern coast were notoriously incomplete.

I'd checked.

In the game. During a lore deep-dive. At 3 AM. With energy drink number four.

'God, my past life skills are so weirdly specific and I keep surviving because of them.'

"Two hundred people," Iris repeated. Not writing this down. Just... holding it. "And you left."

"Fishing wasn't in my future," I said. "Too much water. Too many fish."

"...Neither of those is a flaw."

"They are if you're bad at fishing."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. It lived and died in a quarter second, and if I'd blinked I'd have missed it, but I didn't blink, because I was watching her the way you watch a sleeping dragon for signs of REM.

The Dawn Tyrant almost smiled.

At my joke.

*Danger levels rising. This is not in any guide. None of the routes cover "Empress lowkey has a dry sense of humor."*

"Do you miss it?" she asked.

And there it was.

The question sounded simple. Casual, even. But Iris Aurelia did not do casual. Every word she spoke was placed, and this one had been placed directly on top of a landmine.

Because the honest answer was *I miss everything.*

I missed my apartment in Daejeon with the broken radiator and the wallpaper peeling in the corner. I missed my convenience store — the one where the owner knew I wanted banana milk and the same two triangle kimbap every single shift rotation. I missed my PS5, and my twelve-thousand-hour Steam library, and my headset with the tape on the left earcup. I missed the sound of buses at night and the tteokbokki place that stayed open until four and the guy who sold hotteok outside the station in winter and asked me how my runs went.

I missed a life that, as far as this world was concerned, had never existed.

There was no one alive I could tell.

"Sometimes," I said.

"What do you miss most?"

I thought about it. Don't say *electricity*. Don't say *the internet*. Don't say *my mother, who definitely noticed I was gone by now, who I cannot think about in a locked room with the Empress of the known world or I will Completely Lose It*.

"The sounds," I said, and it came out honest before I could vet it.

Iris's eyes narrowed a fraction. "Sounds."

"It was never quiet. Even at night. In the city—"

*FUCK.*

"—in the village," I corrected, smooth as gravel, "by the coast. You could always hear the waves. Wind in the nets. Gulls. Somebody's grandmother yelling about something at all hours." I let out a breath that I disguised as nostalgia and not as a near-fatal cardiovascular event. "Here, at night, the dormitory's so quiet I can hear my own heart. It's unsettling. Hearts are loud."

Iris was quiet for a moment.

The lamp flame bent. Outside the window, the academy grounds were going dark, lamps coming on in the distance one by one, little strikes of gold across the dusk.

"My mother's quarters overlooked the eastern gardens," she said.

I froze.

"You could hear the fountains from her window. All of them. She slept badly without them — the water, she said, covered the noise of the palace. After she died, my father had the fountains shut off." A pause. Each word placed with the delicacy of someone handling an explosive. "All of them. He walks the eastern gardens every morning now, in silence, and forbids anyone to follow."

She turned her cup a quarter rotation on its saucer.

"It's been very quiet since," she said.

'No. No, no, no, don't do this.'

'Don't tell me real things. You are the FINAL BOSS. You are the Dawn Tyrant who purges ministries before breakfast. I have a spreadsheet of the people you execute in Act Three. You cannot sit there in lamplight drinking your dead mother's tea and tell me you miss the sound of fountains—'

Because then I'd have to feel something.

And I had a very strict policy about feeling things for final bosses.

The policy was: *don't*.

"My grandmother used to say quiet is just the world listening," I heard myself say. "I never liked the saying. But... she meant it kindly."

Iris looked at me.

For a moment — one real, unguarded moment — the study wasn't a war room, and she wasn't an empress, and I wasn't a fraud sweating through his shirt over a notebook of Korean cheat codes.

We were just two people drinking tea, talking about the sounds we missed.

Two teenagers.

That's all.

Her hand rested beside mine on the desk's edge. Not touching. Just... near. A hand's width of silence between us, warm from the lamps.

My heart did something complicated and deeply unauthorized.

'Heart. I swear to god. She is the game's hardest boss and I am a mob with anxiety. Recalculate.'

Over tea.

Her dead mother's tea.
