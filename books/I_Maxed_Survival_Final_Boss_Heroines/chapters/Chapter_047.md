# Chapter 47: Lucen Gray vs. Rhea Valtor: The Empty Stance

The dueling hall of Stellaris Royal Academy was smaller than the Grand Arena.

That was the problem.

In the Grand Arena, the audience was far away. Tiny faces. Ambient noise. You could pretend they were cardboard cutouts with a sound effects track.

Here, they were *close*.

Close enough that I could see Alice's knuckles white around her own fingers. Close enough that I could see Iris leaning forward with an expression of clinical fascination, like I was a frog mid-dissection. Close enough that Lucas Ashford — golden boy, Seat 4-C, protagonist of this whole stupid world — stood with his arms crossed, watching me with open curiosity.

Lyra stood beside him, her blind eyes tilted toward the ring with that unsettling precision, like she was reading the fight before it happened.

And Voss lurked at the back like an expensive funeral.

'Great. Full house. Standing room only for my execution.'

The chandeliers overhead burned warm and low. The safety barriers shimmered around the ring like heat haze — the academy's way of saying *go ahead, try to kill each other, the insurance covers it.*

I rolled my shoulders.

0.009 mana. A practice sword that weighed like a guilty conscience. And across from me, the final boss of Chapter One of my personal nightmare.

Rhea Valtor stood at the opposite mark.

No armor. Just her training blacks and a practice blade held like it was part of her skeleton. Her hair was tied back tight. Her expression was —

Nothing.

That was the scary part. Not anger. Not cold fury. *Nothing.* The carved-away calm of someone who had already decided what tonight was for.

Instructor Vance stood between us, one scarred hand raised, radiating the energy of a man who had refereed ten thousand student duels and had not once been surprised.

"Then I'll say it once," Vance rumbled. "Safety barriers are live. First clean incapacitating hit ends it, or submission. No mana reinforcement —" he glanced at me, then visibly remembered my mana index and chose mercy, "— where applicable."

A small laugh from the audience. The kindest cruelty.

Rhea's eyes didn't leave mine.

"Last chance, Gray," she said. Quiet. For me alone. "Tell me the truth, and we don't have to do this."

'The truth.'

The truth was a 23-year-old unemployed Korean man in a gaming chair at 3 a.m., chugging energy drinks, memorizing her father's sword forms frame-by-frame because the Ashenveil Retreat skip saved forty seconds on the Volume 1 any% route.

The truth would break her worse than any lie.

"I can't give you what you want, Rhea," I said.

Something moved behind her eyes. Disappointment, maybe. Or the opposite — a door closing on the last chance she'd offered herself to walk away.

"Then show me what you have."

Vance's hand dropped.

"Begin."

---

She didn't charge.

That would have been merciful. A charge, I could read. A charge was a line — direction, speed, intent, all in one honest vector.

Instead, she *flowed*.

Three steps and she was in my guard like she'd always been there, and her blade came up in the second form of Ashenveil — Kestrel Rising — a low upward cut designed to catch a retreating opponent's chin.

I knew it.

God help me, I knew it like I knew my own birthday, because I had died to it in-game eleven times while trying to no-hit the sparring segment for an achievement called *Undefeated Nobody*.

My body moved before my brain filed the paperwork.

Weight back. Heel pivot. Blade angled — not blocking, *presenting*, letting her edge ride along mine and bleed its force into the empty air.

The Ashenveil Retreat. First movement: *Yielding Ash.*

Her blade whispered past my nose.

A hot thread of air kissed my cheek.

The audience made a sound — a low, collective *ohhh* — but I was already dealing with form three, because Rhea Valtor did not attack in single strikes.

She attacked in *sentences*.

Kestrel Rising folded into Ashen Descent, a falling overhead cut, and my body answered with the Retreat's second movement — socks screaming against the lacquered floor, a pivot so tight my knee filed a complaint — and her blade carved the space I'd just vacated.

Third strike. Horizontal. "Ember Sweep."

Fourth. A feint-high thrust that became a waist-level slash.

Fifth. Sixth.

I dodged them.

I dodged them all, and the whole time, my inner monologue was screaming with the specific pitch of a man watching his own funeral go well.

'Oh no.'

'Oh no, it's working.'

'Why is it working, this is WORSE—'

Because I could see her face.

Every strike I evaded in her family's language made her eyes go harder and brighter and more *starving*. I wasn't calming her down. I was waving the red flag. I was a man drowning, and every gasp sounded exactly like her father's name.

"That's it," she breathed, mid-combination, almost tender. "That's *it*. Where did you— who—"

"I don't—"

Her blade nearly took my ear off. Conversation over.

---

In the game, the Ashenveil sparring duel had a simple pattern.

Rhea — well, "Ashenveil Swordswoman V (Enraged)" — had a moveset of fourteen attacks, chained from a decision tree weighted by distance and player HP. Memorize the tree, learn the dodge windows, and you could evade forever. The fight was mathematically unwinnable by damage, so the devs made it winnable by *duration*. Survive ninety seconds, cutscene, done.

I had survived those ninety seconds over two hundred times across my 347 hours.

I knew this fight the way you know a song you've heard until you hate it.

But here was the thing about fighting a person instead of a decision tree.

*A person learns.*

Somewhere around the fortieth exchange, I felt it.

A half-beat delay where there shouldn't have been one. A cut that arrived from an angle the game had never used. I slipped it by pure flinch, and while I was still windmilling my balance back, I saw her mouth curve.

Not a smile. The *opposite* of a smile.

She'd found the seams.

Of course she had. The Ashenveil Retreat was a *defensive* form — the doctrine of a rearguard, built to let one soldier hold a corridor while civilians fled. It was never meant to win. It was meant to *not lose*.

And Rhea Valtor had spent her entire life studying her own family's style.

She'd spent lonely years in empty training halls asking the question no one else on the continent was qualified to ask:

*How do I beat my father's sword?*

She'd built counters to every protective form. She'd designed her offense to murder her own defense. She had been preparing for a mirror match against a ghost since she was a child.

'I brought a game guide to a blood feud.'

'Patch notes: the boss adapted.'

'Actually, wait—'

She came in again, and this time her rhythm was *wrong*. Deliberately wrong — syncopated, broken, strung with false openings that my muscle memory reached for like a speedrunner hitting a skip—

—and her blade was already there, waiting at the destination.

The practice sword slammed into my shoulder.

The barrier flashed blue. Pain exploded anyway — dulled by the enchantment, maybe, but "dulled" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. It felt like being hit by a car that whispered *sorry*.

I went down to one knee. The audience gasped. Somewhere, I registered Alice making a small, strangled sound.

'One hit logged. Thirteen loss conditions remaining. Great. Doing great.'

"Get up," Rhea said.

Not cruelly. That was the worst part. If she'd been cruel, I could've hated this. She said it like a training partner. Like someone who genuinely wanted to see how much more of her father's ghost was left inside me to dig out.

I got up.

'Not now.'

The second phase was worse.

She stopped testing and started *dismantling*. I'd slip right — she'd already be cutting right. I'd drop low — her pommel would be descending toward the crown of my head. Her footwork erased mine. Every retreat I knew, she'd already mapped, barricaded, and salted the earth behind.

The tags came in like punctual bad news.

Ribs. *Hit.* Left thigh. *Hit.* Forearm, when I raised it to save my skull. *Hit.*

Each one cost me a layer of barrier and a thin, bright stripe of pain, and each one asked a question I couldn't answer.

The audience had gone completely silent.

Even the murmuring nobles had shut up, because what they were watching had stopped looking like a student duel around the second minute. It looked like something else. Something older. A heresy performed in a dead dialect.

I heard Vance's breath catch — actually *catch* — when I slipped form seven by dropping straight down instead of sideways, a trick that only worked in the game because of jump-cancel frame data and had no right to work on a human body with ligaments.

My ankle disagreed. Loudly.

'Noted. Ankle voted no. Overruled. Democracy is dead.'

I dodged three more strikes on spite alone.

Then my body simply... ran out of road.

---

There's a specific moment in every no-hit run.

You know it if you've ever tried one. It's the moment where your hands know the whole fight, your eyes are reading every telegraph, your heart rate is a flat line of pure focus —

—and then the boss does the thing 0.3 seconds faster than your thumb can physically move.

And you watch it happen. Perfect understanding. Zero capacity.

The gap between *seeing* and *doing*.

Rhea's blade came in on a looping diagonal I'd never seen, transitioning out of a feint her father had probably invented in a war I only knew from a lore codex entry, and my nerves sent the correct order to my legs, and my legs replied with the physical equivalent of a dial-up tone.

Too slow.

'Configured for gaming chair. Not compatible with reality.'

She was inside my reaction time. Fully. Permanently. The next strike would be the last, and I could see it loading in her shoulders — a full-commitment diagonal cut, right shoulder to left hip.

Beautiful form. Textbook. Her father's signature finisher. The one the game rendered in slow motion with falling embers in the background.

*[Bad End No. 34: "The Severed Crown"] would follow shortly after, presumably.*

And in that half-second, watching the end of the duel assemble itself like furniture, some very tired, very honest part of my brain finally clocked out and asked:

*Why are you still trying to dodge?*

...

Huh.

Yeah.

*Why was I?*

Not to win. Winning had left the chat three minutes ago. Not to survive — the barrier guaranteed my survival; the worst case here was a bruised rib and a[job well done] from the referee.

I was dodging to *maintain the story*. The hidden heir. The Seventh Legion survivor. The mysterious operative with a dead mentor whose name he couldn't speak.

The lie. I was taking hits to protect a *lie*.

'Min-jun, you absolute— you farmed this game for 347 hours. You know what the lore says about the Ashenveil Retreat.'

The final doctrine. The one the game only shows you in a single missable mural in the Ashenveil ruins, which exactly nobody reads because it's behind a fake wall and players skip cutscenes.

*"When the soldier can no longer defend, the soldier stops. To stand without defense, before a blade one cannot escape, and to refuse to lie about it — this is the last movement. The Eighth Movement. The Empty Stance."*

*"It is not surrender. Surrender is a strategy. The Empty Stance is the truth."*

I'd thought it was flavor text.

Turns out it was instructions.

---

I stopped.

Everything stopped. Guard, stance, footwork, breath — all of it. I let my shoulders drop, let my arms hang loose at my sides, let the practice sword dangle from two fingers like something I'd found on the ground and forgotten to throw away.

I stood in the center of the ring, as open as a field.

No technique. No angle. No plan.

The Empty Stance is not a stance. That's the entire point. You can't read it, because there's nothing written.

Rhea's final cut was already committed.

I watched it come.

And here's the thing I couldn't have done if I'd tried: I met her eyes.

No mask. No mysterious-heir gravitas. No careful blankness. Just me — Kang Min-jun, 23, unemployed, whose greatest achievement before tonight was a harem ending and a full achievement list — looking up at a girl whose grief had chased me across five provinces, and letting her see *everything*.

The fear. The exhaustion. The sorry-I-can't. The I'm-still-here-anyway.

All of it. Unedited. Day-one build, no patches.

For a fraction of a second, her eyes went wide.

Not because of the stance. Not because of what she was seeing in my *technique*.

Because of what she wasn't.

I watched her search my face — for the survivor's haunted guilt, for the disciple's reverence, for the soldier's oath, for *any* of the hundred stories she had built around me in the dark —

—and I watched her find none of them.

Nothing to find.

Just a boy. Frightened. Honest. Standing still because running had stopped working and pretending had become heavier than the sword.

The blade hit me.

The barrier caught it — a bloom of white-blue light, a thunderclap of displaced mana, the whole hall flashing for one bright instant like the world's most violent photograph being taken.

The physics still transferred.

I left the ground.

For one long, graceful moment I was airborne, and I had time for exactly one thought, which was: *'so this is what a launcher attack feels like from the inside.'*

Then the floor introduced itself to my back.

Skid. Tumble. Roll. Stop.

Stars. So many stars. The chandelier had somehow multiplied into five chandeliers, which seemed extravagant even for Stellaris.

'Noted.'

The hall was dead silent.

Somewhere above the ringing in my ears, I heard five hundred aristocrats collectively fail to understand what they had just witnessed. A boy who fought like a legend for three minutes and then simply... stopped. Put everything down. Took the hit like a man accepting a letter.

From a distance, it probably looked insane.

From a distance, it was insane.

From inside my skull, it was the first fully honest thing I'd done since waking up in this world.

I stared at the ceiling and breathed. Every breath hurt in a new and educational location. My shoulder reported severe weather. My ankle had filed for independence. My ribs were composing a strongly-worded letter.

'So,' I thought. 'I lost.'

'First real sword fight of my afterlife. Final score: Her — one clean finisher. Me — one honest moment and at least seven new bruises.'

'...0.009 mana and pure stubbornness. Really gave it the whole community college try there, Min-jun.'

Footsteps.

The lighting dimmed as a shadow crossed over me, and Rhea Valtor stood above me the way she had in a hundred bad endings — except there was no blood, no broken kingdom, no [BAD END] screen.

Just her. Breathing hard. Blade lowered. Face doing something complicated.

She looked down at me for a long moment.

Then she extended her hand.

I stared at it.

In the game, this is where the player character's companion says something scripted and the trust variable increments.

My hand moved on its own. Hers closed around my wrist — sword-callused, warm, impossibly steady — and she pulled me up out of the floor like it cost her nothing.

We stood there, close. Closer than the audience should've been allowed to witness.

"You don't know, do you," she said.

Quiet. Under the noise the hall was making now — the rising applause, the roaring speculation, Vance bellowing something about the result. A sentence pitched for an audience of one.

"You don't actually know who taught you."

'Here it is.'

The confessional. The altar. The place where every road I'd walked since Chapter One had been heading.

I could lie. I'd gotten so *good* at lying. The mysterious operative. The forbidden oath. *My master's name died with the Seventh.* One more elegant fiction and she'd probably believe it forever.

She was giving me the opening. I could feel it. She'd half-forgiven the lie in advance, in the way you forgive grief anything.

I looked at her.

And for once, I did the math and chose the truth-shaped thing.

"No," I said. "I don't."

It was true. Every word.

No *person* had taught me. No soldier of the Seventh Legion had pressed his dying testament into my hands. No hidden master had found me in the snow.

A machine had shown me. Through a screen. In another life entirely. A dead man's sword had been reduced to polygons and timing windows, and a lonely guy in a studio apartment had downloaded it into his hands between instant noodles.

I didn't know who taught me. Because *no one* did.

It was the truest thing I'd ever said to her, and I would never be able to explain why it was true, and somehow — standing there with my ribs on fire — that felt like the only offering I had that was actually worth anything.

Rhea looked at me for a long, long time.

Something in her face moved through seasons. Anger. Grief. Confusion. And then, slowly, like dawn taking its sweet time — something that wasn't any of those.

Acceptance, maybe. Or the decision to *try* acceptance, which amounts to the same thing if you keep at it.

She nodded. Once.

"I accept your silence," she said. "As promised."

'Wait. Seriously? That's— that's it? No follow-up questions? No final boss second phase?'

'In the game, refusing to answer this question reduced her affection by—'

I stopped.

I looked at her — really looked — and decided, deliberately, to stop checking her against a wiki that no longer existed.

She wasn't a decision tree. She never had been. I'd just been too scared to notice.

She stepped back. Set her practice blade across her palms. And then, to the audience — to five hundred nobles, to the heir of the realm, to Instructor Vance, to the whole gawking machinery of Stellaris gossip — Rhea Valtor bowed.

Not a duelist's bow to a beaten opponent.

A soldier's bow. Deep. Formal. The kind you give to a flag.

Then she turned and walked off the floor, and she didn't look back — but this time it wasn't because she knew I'd follow.

This time it was because she'd carry what happened here with her, and she didn't need to check whether it was following.

The hall erupted.

Applause. Shouts. The aristocracy's noise crashing over me like weather. I heard my name — or rather, "Gray" — in about nine different speculative tones. *The heir. The hidden heir. Did you see form seven—? Was that really Ashenveil—? Who IS he?*

I barely heard any of it.

I was watching Rhea's back as she walked through the parting crowd, and my chest hurt with a pain that had absolutely nothing to do with the diagonal cut, and everything to do with the truth I could never give her.

I wanted to tell her. God, I wanted to.

*Your father's soldiers didn't all die forgotten. Someone carried the flame. His sword is still being practiced, still being perfected, still alive — just in a place you can't walk to.*

But the actual truth was so much worse than silence.

The actual truth was: a boy sat in the dark pressing buttons, and your father's entire legacy was a game mechanic, and I memorized his life's work to save forty seconds on a speedrun.

Some truths are not gifts. Some truths are just cruelty wearing truth's coat.

So I kept it. The way you keep a stone in your pocket.

Mine to carry. Hers to never have to.

Arms found me before I made it three steps off the ring.

Alice materialized at my left, taking my weight with a firmness entirely at odds with how much she'd apparently cried during the last minute — her eyes were red-rimmed and furious about it. "You *idiot*. You complete— you *stopped*. In the middle of— I watched you just *stop*—"

"It was a tactical stop."

"It was not a tactical anything! Your face did the thing where you've given up!"

"The tactical giving-up face. Very advanced. Requires certification."

She made a sound halfway between a sob and a laugh and punched me very lightly in the uninjured ribs.

On my other side, Iris appeared like an ambush predator in evening dress, silver eyes dissecting me.

"That final posture," she said. "There is no such form in any recorded school on the continent."

"There is now. I call it the 'I've made a huge mistake.' Patent pending."

"Lucen."

"...It's the last movement of Ashenveil," I said quietly. "The one nobody teaches. Because you can't teach it. You can only arrive at it."

Iris went very still.

Then she smiled — small, genuine, and privately pleased, like I'd finally turned in an assignment she hadn't known she'd set. "I'll want a full account. Eventually. When you're ready to have one."

'Translation: mystery retained. Flag secure. Oh good. My nine simultaneous doomed routes remain alive and well.'

Over her shoulder, I caught Lucas Ashford watching me — not with suspicion, which would've been safer, but with the bright, uncomplicated respect of a protagonist recognizing something. He raised two fingers off his sword-hand in a small salute.

I nodded back, two nobodies alike, one of us just not knowing yet that he was the main character.

And above us all — past the chandeliers, past the false stars of the enchanted ceiling, somewhere in the silver dark where patient ancient attention made its home — I felt it.

Amusement. Warmth. The faint, monstrous fondness of a goddess eating popcorn.

["Little gamer,"] the silence seemed to say. ["You were supposed to survive Volume 1 by being clever."]

'Yeah?'

["You survived it by being honest. Cheeky. I'm keeping the sequel."]

'...There's a sequel?'

The silence said nothing, which was somehow the loudest answer I'd ever received.

I stood there between a saint and a serpent, bruised in seven places, one Bad End dormant and a dozen more asleep in the walls, and did the only thing left to do.

I put my hand in my pocket.

The butterscotch was still there. Still impossibly warm.

I unwrapped it, put it in my mouth, and let the sweetness hit, and for one stolen second the whole shadow banquet — the nobles, the schemes, the routes and flags and ticking dooms — shrank down to the size of candy.

'Volume 1 cleared,' I thought.

'Run stats: zero deaths. One defeat. Fourteen Bad Ends dodged, deferred, or talked down off a ledge. One goddess mildly entertained. One dragon nibbling cookies on a ballroom ledge.'

'And one impossible question, finally answered—'

I looked toward the doors where Rhea Valtor had disappeared, carrying my silence like something she'd chosen to keep.

'—can a lie and the truth fight to a draw?'

'Yeah. Tonight, in this hall, with this much bruising.'

'They can.'
