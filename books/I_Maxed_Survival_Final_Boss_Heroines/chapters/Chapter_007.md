# Chapter 7: The Evil Goddess Has Opinions

Day three.

I lay on my dormitory bed, staring at the ceiling, and conducted a formal review of my life choices.

The ceiling, for the record, was not worth reviewing. Water stain in the corner shaped vaguely like a disappointed face. A crack running the length of the room like the building itself was trying to escape. One spider who had been living rent-free above my pillow since I arrived, whom I had named Gerald, because if I was going to die in this world I at least wanted someone to mourn me.

My room was a converted storage closet in the provisional student wing.

And I don't mean that as a joke. There were still hooks on the wall where brooms used to hang. The door opened *outward* because there wasn't room for it to open inward. If I stretched out both arms, I could touch both walls, which was great for doing horizontal push-ups in bed and absolutely terrible for everything else.

But it was mine. It had a lock. And right now, a lock was the single most valuable resource in the known universe.

'Three days,' I thought, counting on my fingers like a war criminal tallying sins. 'It's been three days. Let's review the damage.'

Day one: broke the aptitude measuring crystal at the entrance examination. The crystal that had survived three hundred years of students, including, historically, *two actual Saints*. Cracked it like an egg. The proctor made a sound like a deflating bagpipe.

Day two: discovered that my sponsor — the mysterious benefactor paying my provisional tuition — was Vice-Headmistress Voss. The hidden villain. The *final revelation boss of the political intrigue subplot*. The woman the entire player base had agreed was "definitely a fibrant" until Chapter 9 of the main story proved she'd been pulling strings the whole time.

Day three, today: made direct eye contact with Iris Aurelia, the Dawn Tyrant, future Burner of the Western Provinces, and — instead of running, instead of bowing, instead of doing literally any of the fourteen things my strategy guides said to do —

— apologized to her.

Like she was a *person*.

Like an idiot.

I closed my eyes.

*Min-jun.*

She was the only one in this entire world who used that name. Kim Min-jun, twenty-six years old, former office worker, proud owner of a platinum-rated completionist save file and one (1) fatal weakness for games sold by suspicious elderly shopkeepers.

In this world, I was Lucen Gray. Seventeen. Commoner. No house, no mana, no future, a file so empty the administration office had flagged it twice for suspected forgery.

Two people. One body. Zero prospects.

'Okay,' I thought, sitting up. 'Enough moping. Moping doesn't complete routes.'

I reached under my mattress and pulled out the one thing I'd spent real money on since arriving.

A leather-bound journal.

It had cost me my last four copper coins. The shopkeeper had thrown in a stub of charcoal pencil out of what I can only describe as profound pity. The leather was cheap. The pages were rough, the kind of paper that drank ink like it was thirsty.

It was the most valuable object on this continent.

Because I was going to fill it with *everything*.

I remembered every one of them. The routes where I'd tried being boring (she got bored *of* ignoring me, which is different). The routes where I'd transferred schools (the academy in the next province, reader, *also burned*). The route where I'd tried to preemptively make her hate me, which had produced the single most terrifying playthrough of my life and an ending CG I still had nightmares about.

The only route where BE #12 didn't kill you was the route where it never triggered at all.

And the trigger was a five-word sentence.

*"Interesting. I'll be watching you."*

'She said the line,' I whispered to Gerald the spider. 'Yesterday. She said the actual line.'

Gerald, sympathetically, hid inside his crack.

"Alright," I said out loud, because talking to myself had stopped being a warning sign somewhere around hour thirty of this isekai. "Terminology check. For the reader."

A pause.

"I don't know what reader. Future me. Gerald. Whatever."

Because here was the thing about the floating text boxes — the *System* — that I'd been dancing around since day one.

It wasn't a System.

Not in the standard webnovel sense, anyway. I'd read enough of the genre to know how these things went. Cold, blue, mechanical. *[Quest Received.]* *[Reward: 5 Stat Points.]* Numbers and functions, as emotional as a rice cooker.

Mine had *opinions*.

Mine used sarcasm as a primary communication protocol. Mine kept what she called a "memorial book" and had once, during the entrance examination, spent four full text windows describing the architectural quality of the building I was about to humiliate myself in, purely for ambience.

She called herself the Evil Goddess.

I had no proof she was either of those things. But back in my old life, in the game's files, buried in the lore most players skipped, there *had* been a mention. A primordial being, sealed outside the world, whose only entertainment was watching mortals. The lore had made her sound terrifying.

The lore had not mentioned that she was *like this*.

Four hours.

That's how long I wrote.

My hand cramped. My charcoal shrank to a nub. The window — my room had a window the size of a hardcover book, through which the weather of the world filtered in theory — went from pale afternoon gold to the deep amber of early evening, and still I wrote.

Because every page was armor.

I mapped the first semester events. The midterm tournament — *avoid entering, the preliminary bracket contains a scripted injury event*. The autumn ball — *the crown prince attends; three heroine flags fire that night; a commoner in the wrong corner of the ballroom becomes a scandal's collateral damage*. I sank lower over my bowl.

And that was when I felt it.

Do you know the feeling? Every gamer knows it. The prickle at the back of the neck when the camera is about to pan. The loading-screen silence before a boss door opens. The sense — older than games, older than anything — of a *predator's attention* finding you in tall grass.

Slowly, very slowly, I raised my eyes.

Across the hall. East side. Under the banners.

The imperial section.

Iris Aurelia sat alone at a table built for twelve, because of *course* she sat alone, because the Dawn Tyrant ate alone the way she trained alone, in a radius of emptiness that the entire academy maintained by unspoken treaty.

She had a book open beside her plate. From a distance, a model student — dining, reading, minding her imperial business.

But I'd spent two hundred hours with that character's sprites, her animations, her idle micro-behaviors. I knew how her eyes moved when she read. I'd watched it a thousand times.

Her eyes weren't moving.

The page hadn't turned in the entire time I'd been watching.

She was holding a book, and looking *over* it, directly across the length of the hall, at the wobbling bench in the far corner —

— at me.

And when our eyes met, Iris Aurelia did not look away.

She did not look away at *all*.

She tilted her head. Just slightly. The exact, fractional angle of a question mark.

Then, unhurried, she lifted her cup and drank — eyes on me the whole time — and the corner of her mouth did something that in a lesser woman would have been called a smile.

My soul briefly left my body through the ceiling, said goodbye to Gerald, and came back.
