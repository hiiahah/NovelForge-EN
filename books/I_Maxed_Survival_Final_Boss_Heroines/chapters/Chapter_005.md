# Chapter 5: The Protagonist Does Protagonist Things

There are moments in every man's life when God looks down at him, checks a clipboard, and says, "Not enough suffering. Double it."

Mine was scheduled for immediately after lunch.

```
[You have been enrolled in: Practical Mana Assessment — Period 4]
[Attendance: Mandatory.]
[Skipping Class Penalty: Death.]
[That's not a system penalty. That's just the general penalty for you at this point.]
```

'Even the class schedule is mocking me now.'

The outdoor training grounds of Stellaris Royal Academy were, to put it in terms any modern person would understand, criminal.

An arena the size of three football fields, ringed by translucent enchantment barriers that shimmered like heat haze. Observation towers at each corner. Weapon racks polished to a mirror shine. Training dummies that, I knew from the game, cost more mana-stone each than the average commoner family earned in a decade.

This place had a gymnasium budget that could fund a small war.

In the game, this was a cutscene. You clicked past it. Maybe you read the flavor text if you were a lore nerd like me.

Standing in it, under the actual sun, wearing actual academy training clothes that were somehow both too warm and embarrassing?

Different genre of experience.

I positioned myself, with the practiced instinct of a man who has survived 635 calculated Bad Endings, at the very back of the line.

Directly behind a boy whose shoulders were approximately the width of a door.

*Yes. Good. Perfect.*

This absolute unit of a classmate — I think his name was Berg? Bram? Something with a B and the energy of a glacier — functioned flawlessly as a human shield. From the front, I was invisible. From the side, I was a rumor. From above, I was a rounding error.

'This is it. This is the optimal play. Be the burp in a crowded room. Be the ninth-most-interesting person in a group of forty.'

The plan was simple. Painfully simple. Elegantly simple, even.

Step one: when my turn came, place hand on the measuring crystal.

Step two: produce a reading so mediocre it would be forgotten before the next student finished walking to the platform.

Step three: return to position behind Door-Boy.

Step four: graduate quietly in three years, move to a coastal town, open a shop selling fishing equipment, and die of old age surrounded by nothing.

It was a beautiful plan.

It had survived exactly one day of this academy so far, which made it my longest-running strategy of the week.

---

Instructor Kaelin stood at the assessment platform with the posture of a man who had personally killed three wars and been disappointed by all of them.

"Mana index and physical conditioning," he announced, in the voice of gravel being filed. "Today we take your baselines. Place your hand on the crystal. Channel. Do not strain. Anyone who faints will be laughed at, then treated, in that order."

A few students laughed nervously.

Kaelin did not.

'He wasn't joking. He was never joking. In the game he had a side quest where he literally carried a collapsed student to the infirmary while lecturing him about hydration the entire way.'

The assessments began.

"Nesia Portman. Index: 88. Acceptable."

"Finn Alderic. Index: 121. Good."

"Marguerite Solen. Index: 75. Work on your circulation form, it's sloppy."

One by one. Hand on crystal. Crystal glows. Number called. Student walks away either glowing or deflated. A neat little conveyor belt of adolescent self-esteem, crushed and inflated at industrial efficiency.

I watched the numbers with genuine, professional interest.

Fifties to one-eighties, just as the game had said. For first-years who hadn't awakened properly yet, that was the expected band. Anything over 150 was "promising." Anything over 200 got a raised eyebrow from Kaelin.

Nobody had cracked 200 yet.

And then—

"Lucas Ashford."

The whole yard subtly straightened.

The protagonist walked forward the way protagonists do — without hurry, without swagger, with that infuriating natural gravity that makes everyone check their posture.

Hand on crystal.

The crystal lit up like it had been waiting its whole life for this specific moment.

"...Index: 312."

The silence lasted exactly one second.

Then the whispers hit like a wave.

"Three hundred—?"

"On a *baseline*? That's knightly order standards—"

"Isn't that guy a commoner? Where does a commoner even get—"

Kaelin's scarred face did something complicated. On another man, it might have been a smile.

"Well done," he said, which from Kaelin was roughly equivalent to a standing ovation and a marriage proposal.

Lucas just nodded, a little embarrassed, and stepped down.

And let me tell you — watching from behind my human fortification, I nearly teared up.

*Beautiful. Magnificent. Ten out of ten. Five stars.*

'Be strong, Lucas. Be unreasonably, unfairly, narratively strong. Become the sun. Pull every flag, every enemy, every demon lord, and every dragon onto that broad protagonist back of yours.'

'Carry the plot.'

'So I never have to carry anything heavier than a fishing rod.'

The System, because it couldn't let me have anything nice for more than four seconds, chimed in:

```
[Protagonist detected performing protagonist activities.]
[Your emotional state: Relieved.]
[The Evil Goddess adds: "You've outsourced being the hero to a teenager.]
["Honestly? Smartest thing you've done all week."]
```

'Thank you. I'll take the compliment. I'll frame it.'

---

Then the plot gave me a gift.

It started with a shove.

Three stations over, a commoner girl — brown hair, thin, the kind of nervous posture that screams *scholarship student* — stumbled past Derrick Holt's station and clipped his shoulder.

Derrick Holt.

Ah, Derrick Holt.

Baron's son. Blond, shiny, and possessed of the single most punchable jawline in the first-year class. In the game, he existed for one purpose: to be an asshole for ten minutes so Lucas Ashford could look good correcting him.

A tutorial boss. A warm-up antagonist. The narrative equivalent of the first goomba.

And right on schedule, he performed his one job.

"Watch where you're going, peasant," Derrick said, loud enough for the entire yard, because jerks like him never understood the concept of an inside voice. "Do you know how much this uniform costs? More than your father's farm."

The girl stammered an apology, face red, eyes wet.

The thirty-eight students present did what thirty-eight students always do in this scene — absolutely nothing, while radiating intense discomfort.

And then, from the center of the yard, with no fanfare whatsoever—

"Hey."

Lucas Ashford walked over. Picked up the girl's dropped assessment slate. Handed it back. Then turned to Derrick.

"The uniform costs the same as yours," Lucas said, evenly. "The academy issues them."

"...What?"

"You said hers cost less. It doesn't. So the only thing you're actually angry about is that someone like her is allowed to stand on the same field as you." A pause. "That's not her problem. That's yours."

*Oooooh.*

The crowd noise was immediate. The collective intake of breath could have inflated a hot air balloon.

Derrick's face went through about four colors before settling on an ugly mottled red. He stepped forward — then caught Kaelin's gaze from the platform, flat and heavy as an anvil.

"...Tch." Derrick spat on the ground, turned, and stalked back to his station.

Textbook. *Flawless.* Chapter One, Scene Four of the source material, executed word-perfect.

'The plot is working,' I thought, with the warm satisfaction of a man watching his train arrive exactly on time. 'The story is alive. Lucas is doing the hero things. The flags are all pointing at the correct flag-bearer. The natural order is restored.'

'Today is a good day.'

'Nothing can go wrong today.'

I should note, for the record, that the moment I finished that thought, somewhere far beyond the sky, something ancient and amused laughed so hard it had to sit down.

"Lucen Gray!"

'...'

'Oh.'

'Oh, that's my name. That's my name that he just—'

'I have a name, and it was just said out loud, into a microphone-rich environment.'

---

I walked to the platform.

Thirty-nine pairs of eyes. Plus Kaelin's. Plus the training staff. Plus — and my soul genuinely tried to leave my body when I clocked this — the advanced class had set up on the far side of the field for their own assessments.

Among them, one head of imperial crimson hair.

Sitting at the far assessment station, posture perfect, waiting her turn.

Iris Aurelia.

Not looking at me. Looking at her own station's prep work.

*She is not looking at me. Repeat: she is not looking at me. The flag is on PROXIMITY, which means proximity is the danger, which means I will now minimize proximity by completing this assessment at the speed of a coward and vacating the area.'

I reached the crystal.

It sat on its pedestal — a sphere of flawless cut quartz, half a meter across, gently humming with the pale-blue light of measurement enchantment. In the game, this thing was a prop. A background object with a UI element.

In front of me, it looked like an executioner's block that had been cryogenically frozen.

*Okay. Math. Do the math, Lucen.*

My actual mana. Total. The complete sum of the magical power in my entire body, as measured by the System this morning, during a calm moment, while I was doing breathing exercises under my blanket:

**0.009.**

The average student reading today: about 100.

The scoring band I needed to hit: roughly 60 to 90. Forgettable. Gray. Beige.

*I've got this. Channeling is just moving mana along circulation pathways. Size of the pool doesn't matter — even a tiny pool circulates. The crystal reads flow and density. I just have to push what I have through my arm at a nice, respectable, medium-wattage speed, and the density reading will look...*

My hand touched the crystal.

Cold.

I pushed.

0.009 units of mana — the full sovereign treasury of the Kingdom of Lucen — trickled into the crystal's reception lattice like a single drop of water entering an ocean.

The crystal glowed.

Flickered.

Then the light inside it *stuttered.*

Like a candle in a windstorm. Like a man on a staircase who missed a step.

The receiver lattice, forged by master artificers and rated to parse anything between 1 unit of mana and 50,000, received my reading, chased the decimal point to the right, kept chasing it, kept chasing it, hit the end of its calibration table, and—

*KRRK.*

A crack split down the center of a crystal rated for archmages.

Clean. Loud. Audible in Boise.

The two halves of the lattice sagged apart with a sad little *tink.*

Silence.

Total, comprehensive, cathedral-grade silence.

Somewhere behind me, someone dropped a slate.

"..."

"..."

"...What," Derrick Holt said, in a small voice, "the hell."

Instructor Kaelin crossed the platform in four strides. He looked at the crystal. He looked at my hand. He looked at the crystal again, longer this time, with the expression of a man re-checking whether his entire life had been a typo.

"That crystal," Kaelin said, very slowly, "is rated to measure up to fifty thousand mana index."

*Yes. Correct. And if you flip the sign on that fact you'll get closer to the truth—*

"The last time one broke," Kaelin continued, "was fifteen years ago. When Archmage Solren visited the academy and touched one out of curiosity."

Murmurs. Rising. Multiplying. A hive of whispers slamming into high gear.

"It *broke*—"

"Solren? THE Solren? The guy who parted the Vessian Strait?"

"He broke it just by touching—"

"Who even IS that guy? Lucen who? Gray?? Is that even a noble house—"

'No,' my brain screamed. 'No no no, STOP— you've got it backwards, you've got it EXACTLY BACKWARDS—'

"I—" I opened my mouth. "It's not what you—"

*Explain it. Explain it right now. Say "my mana is zero point zero zero nine, the crystal died of starvation, I am the magical equivalent of a potato." Say it. It's embarrassing but it's SURVIVABLE—*

Kaelin raised a hand.

The yard went quiet again, out of pure reflex.

He pulled the broken crystal away from me — gently. *Carefully.* The way you'd handle a relic. Then he looked me in the eye with an expression I can only describe as a man filing paperwork in his soul.

"Clearly," Instructor Kaelin said, "you have some... *unique circumstances*, Mr. Gray."

'No. No I don't. My circumstances are the LEAST unique. I am the store brand of students—'

"We'll arrange a private re-assessment. With appropriate instruments." His eyes narrowed slightly, professionally curious in the way of a man who had just been handed a fascinating problem. "Suppressing your output that thoroughly, unconsciously, on contact... I've read of it. I've never seen it."

*Suppressing.*

*He said suppressing.*

*He thinks I did that ON PURPOSE. He thinks there is a buried ocean in me and that I am currently sitting on the lid.*

Every student was staring.

Derrick Holt was staring, all the fight visibly draining out of his punchable face as he recalculated his entire bullying priority list.

The commoner girl Lucas had just saved was staring.

Lucas Ashford was staring, with the open, warm curiosity of a protagonist spotting a mystery.

And across the field — past the enchanted barriers, past two full stations of the advanced class assessment —

Iris Aurelia had stopped pretending to look at her prep work.

Her molten-gold eyes were fixed on me.

Still. Unblinking.

The way a hawk looks at a field mouse that has just done something *interesting.*

The yard was hot, the sun was high, and I was sweating so hard my uniform collar had gone a full shade darker.

I walked back to my spot behind Door-Boy on legs that had the structural integrity of wet paper, and I did what any man does when his life is over.

I opened the System.

```
[SYSTEM NOTE]

[Your mana index of 0.009 caused an integer underflow error
in the Crystal of Measurement.]

[Fun fact: the crystal expected a value between 1 and 50,000.]
[You submitted 0.009.]
[The crystal's parser wrapped around to the maximum value
and reported: OVERFLOW — VALUE EXCEEDS MAXIMUM.]

[In layman's terms: it took one look at your magical power,
assumed you were TOO POWERFUL TO MEASURE,
and committed suicide.]
```

'...'

```
[This error cannot be corrected without revealing the System's existence.]

[Congratulations, Player.]
[Every witness now believes you are suppressing the mana pool
of an Archmage.]

[Current public reputation: "Terrifying hidden master."]
[Actual status: "Terrified hidden man with the mana output
of a moderately enchanted dinner plate."]
```

'A dinner plate. It said a dinner plate. The System — the entity that is supposed to keep me alive — just described me as crockery.'

*And the worst part is it's generous. A dinner plate at least gets enchanted. I'm a regular plate. I'm the plate they give to guests they don't like.*

The window pulsed.

Repainted itself in that familiar, dreadful lilac.

```
[A Note From Your Sponsor:]

[The Evil Goddess has been informed of recent events.]

[She has no words.]

[She has simply been sitting in silence
for the past ninety seconds,
massaging her temples
with the crushing weariness of a woman
who has watched a hamster drown in a teaspoon.]

[Her official statement is a sigh.]

[It lasted eleven seconds.]

[She would also like you to know that your memorial
has been expanded again. It now includes a gift shop.
The gift shop sells plates.]
```

'Get out of my head.'

```
[She heard that.]
[She says, and I quote:]
["I'm all you have, sweetheart."]
```

And then, the final line. The one that made the sweat on my back go cold.

```
[ALERT]

[Bad End No. 12: 'The Tyrant's Curiosity']
[Flag status updated: PROXIMITY → ACTIVE]

[The entity 'Iris Aurelia' has moved you from the category:]
[B = Background Scenery]
[to:]
[A = Anomaly Requiring Investigation]

[Historical resolution rate of Category A cases: 100%.]
[Average resolution time: 4 days.]
[Your expected lifespan has been recalculated.]
[It is shorter than the queue for the cafeteria on pasta day.]
```

I stood behind my human shield and stared at nothing.

Across the field, the advanced class resumed. Somewhere over there, a girl who could crack the crust of the earth had just added me to a list, and girls like her did not keep long lists. They kept short ones. Short, and thoroughly completed.

To my left, students were still sneaking glances at me and whispering *fifty thousand* like it was a curse word.

In front of me, Kaelin was making a note. An actual, physical note. In a ledger. Notes were how it started. Notes became files. Files became private re-assessments with "appropriate instruments," and I knew — I *knew*, with the weary certainty of 635 deaths — that no instrument in this world could measure me, because every instrument in this world expected a mage, and what they were going to get was a frightened Korean man's debt-ridden soul stapled into a seventeen-year-old body.

'The plan,' I thought, hollowly, staring at the back of Door-Boy's gigantic head. 'The plan was beige.'

'I had ONE job. Be beige.'

'And instead I broke industrial equipment with my poverty.'

Somewhere very far away, the Evil Goddess finally exhaled, poured herself a drink, and started a betting pool on whether I'd survive the week.

The odds, I would later learn, were not in my favor.

Six hundred and thirty-four Bad Endings to go.

'Fuck my life,' I thought.

The System, helpfully, chimed:

```
[Acknowledged.]
```
