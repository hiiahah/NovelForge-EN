# Chapter 21: Decryption Session #3: The Map

---

There were many types of rooms in Stellaris Royal Academy.

Classrooms. Training halls. Libraries. Dining courts. Punishment chambers, probably, somewhere, given this game's track record.

And then there was *this* room.

Iris Aurelia's private study room, fourth floor of the Aurelius Wing — a wing that existed purely because the Imperial family had donated enough money to make the rules politely look away.

Silk curtains. Mahogany desk. A fireplace that crackled with actual enchanted blue flames. A tea set that probably cost more than my entire childhood.

'I have stepped into the final boss's lair. On purpose. For the third time.'

Somebody please take notes on my suicidal tendencies. Future historians will want to know whether I was brave or just defective.

Spoiler: defective.

"Sit."

"Yes, Your Highness."

I sat.

The chair was absurdly comfortable. Which made it worse. Traps in Eschaton Hearts were always the most comfortable ones. The poisons were in the finest wines. The knives came out during the nicest dinners.

This wasn't paranoia.

This was 347 hours of verified empirical data.

---

Iris sat across from me, legs crossed, posture perfect in that effortless way that made normal humans feel like sacks of potatoes held together by string.

My notebook — *my confiscated, illicit, please-God-don't-look-inside notebook* — sat on the table between us.

This was Decryption Session No. 3.

The arrangement, if you could call my total lack of choice an "arrangement," was simple. Twice a week, I came to this room, and Princess Iris Aurelia of the Empire sat across from me and "studied" my notebook full of indecipherable scribbles.

The scribbles were Hangul.

Korean. The language of Earth. Of Kang Min-jun, age 23, unemployed completionist who had spent 347 hours speedrunning a game everyone thought was a joke.

To the entire world of Eschaton Hearts, my notebook was an untranslatable cipher. A personal code-system invented by a strange commoner boy.

To me, it was a diary of every flag, every route, every bad ending, and — critically — *every secret location in the game.*

The first two sessions had gone... fine.

Suspiciously fine.

She asked questions like "why does your cipher include pictograms?" and "why does this symbol repeat one hundred and forty times?"

The repeated symbol was the Hanja-like scribble I used for the word 'die.'

I told her it meant "safety."

She'd accepted that. Filed it away. I'd watched her file it away, in the way a cat watches a mouse-hole and takes a note for later.

Session three, though.

Session three, she'd flipped to a page I had been * praying* she'd skip.

The tea arrived.

Blue flame licked up in the grate. A maid set down porcelain cups and vanished like a well-trained ghost.

Iris opened the notebook.

One page.

The margin sketch.

"It's a map," she said mildly.

My cup froze halfway to my mouth.

"You drew a map of the academy." Her violet eyes didn't rise from the page. "Including rooms that don't appear on any public blueprint."

...

...

*Shit.*

---

The margin map.

The fucking margin map.

Of course she'd found it. Of course. She'd held this notebook for hours across three sessions. Did I think she wouldn't notice the tiny inked diagram I'd doodled in the corner of page fifty-two, between notes on 'Lucas Ashford's stat growth (abnormal, cheat-class, report to no one)' and 'the cafeteria's eel dish is poisonous to 12% of students, avoid'?

That map was a habit from my speedrun days.

A memory exercise.

In Eschaton Hearts, the academy was honeycombed with secret passages. The kind of routes a completionist *lived* for. Skip corridors. Hidden stairwells. Sealed chambers with unique items that only appeared if you triggered obscure NPC dialogue trees at 3 AM on rainy in-game days.

I'd drawn it without thinking.

Just muscle memory. Just my hand doodling while I listened to a hallucinated lecture in my head.

And now the crown princess of the Empire was staring at it with the exact expression of a lioness who'd just found the rabbit's burrow marked with a flag.

'Interested predator.'

That's what I'd privately started calling that look.

It was the same look she'd worn when she watched me fail my practical exam on purpose, then pass part of it by accident.

The same look she wore when she realized I was faking incompetence in a hundred subtle ways.

And the same look she wore, apparently, when she found a map of the Imperial academy's *secret passageways* in a commoner's personal cipher-journal.

'I'm going to die.'

'Bad ending incoming.'

'Nice run, Kang Min-jun. Should've bought the game and then thrown it into the ocean.'

"You've gone pale," Iris observed pleasantly, sipping her tea.

"I have a condition."

"Which is?"

"Being alive."

Her lips twitched. A micro-expression. A fraction of a centimeter of genuine amusement that she immediately suppressed beneath her imperial porcelain mask.

I caught it because I was looking for it.

I caught it the way a gambler catches a tell.

"Explain," she said, "the map."

Here we go.

Final boss encounter phase one.

Begin.

---

The panic lasted exactly three seconds.

After that, the speedrunner brain kicked in.

Step one: Identify the stakes.

Espionage. Treason. Unauthorized knowledge of restricted Imperial infrastructure. Any of those, minimum ten thousand gold fine — or a very quiet disappearance into a hole.

Step two: Identify the goal.

Not arrest. Not execution. Walk out of this room alive with the notebook and the lie intact.

Step three: Execute.

Don't freeze. Don't babble. Don't overexplain.

Cool and calm.

Like a man who has done this before.

Which... I technically had.

In my head.

Against the *same character.*

A hundred and twenty times across various runs.

In zero of those runs did I have this conversation while sitting in her private study room smiling over tea, so please, Goddess, if you're watching —

— *go away.*

"Your Highness," I said, setting down the cup with a steadiness I absolutely did not feel. "Look at the margin again. Specifically at the walls around the Headmaster's corridor."

She looked.

I watched her look. Her eyes tracked the little pencil lines, the slight offset where the wall boundary shifted thickness, the geometry of the building as I, Kang Min-jun-ish-Lucen Gray, had sketched it from memory.

"What do you notice?"

"...The wall thickness changes."

"Exactly."

I let out a measured breath.

"The masonry near those sections uses a different joint pattern than the surrounding stonework. That's a different phase of construction. Stellaris was built in three stages over two centuries, wasn't it?"

It was. I knew this because the game's lore text about it appeared verbatim on a loading screen between routes. I'd seen that loading screen approximately four hundred times.

She didn't confirm it. She just watched me.

"When construction periods change," I continued, "especially in older fortified buildings, the changes rarely happen in an open line. Builders patch, they cover, they seal. A wall that gets thicker between two sections — without an obvious reason on the existing blueprint — almost always means a sealed space on the other side."

Silence.

The fireplace murmured.

"A void," I said. "An old stairwell, a maintenance shaft, a storage chamber that was sealed when the floor plan shifted. Every old building has them. Every cathedral, every fortress, every palace."

Every video game with level design.

But she didn't need to know that part.

"I followed the stones," I finished quietly. "That's... all I did."

Iris Aurelia sat utterly still.

Her eyes traveled from the map, to my face, back to the map again. Like she was cross-referencing two documents. One was made of ink. The other was made of my panicking flesh.

A long, long, carefully measured moment passed.

I could hear my own heartbeat in my ears.

'It's a good lie. It has to be a good lie. I tested that theory in real life. The architecture logic is legitimately sound. Any structural engineer on Earth would back me up—'

"...How old," she finally asked, "do you think I am?"

"...I'd rather not answer that."

Her mouth tightened — and then, unaccountably, the corner of it pulled upward.

Barely.

A quarter-millimeter of genuine amusement.

"That answer," she said softly, "was the smartest thing you've said all evening."

'Oh no.'

'Did I just get complimented by the Tyrant Butterfly herself?'

'This is a death flag. This is literally a death flag.'

She flipped the notebook page. Back to the margin map. Her finger alighted on a specific point.

"The Headmaster's corridor," she said. "Here. You marked this door as a sealed stairwell, descending."

"I guessed it from the wall geometry."

"Correct."

Pause.

Her finger slid west.

"The sub-basement entry on the east side of the old chapel."

"Same reasoning. The foundation pattern around that alcove doesn't match the original chapel footprint."

"Correct."

Another pause.

Longer this time.

"And this one." Her finger rested on the Western Tower annotations. "You marked a key location here. Behind the library annex."

"*I* didn't mark that one," I heard myself say. "That's where the three-phase masonry ends without explanation. It's the only wall in the tower that was built to a structural standard from the second construction era. It's out of place. That usually means it's a replacement for something older that was destroyed or sealed."

The words came out smooth.

Too smooth.

Perfectly reasoned. Perfectly defensible. The kind of argument a prodigy would make.

'Shut up, Lucen, you sound like a genius, being a genius is a death sentence around this woman—'

But the words were already out.

Iris studied me for what felt like a century.

Her expression was still perfectly composed. Royal. Unreadable.

But her eyes.

Her eyes were *hungry.*

"The entries you've drawn," she said finally, "are accurate. I've verified two of the three personally."

'Of course you have. You're Iris Aurelia. You've probably already had your people crawl through them in the last forty-eight hours—'

"The third," she continued, "I can verify through Imperial records."

'Oh no.'

"Lucen Gray." She said my name like a jeweler naming a gemstone. "You are either the most intuitively gifted structural analyst I have ever met..."

Beat.

"...or the most reckless liar."

'Both.'

'Can it be both?'

'Please let it be both.'

I gave her a weak smile. "Which would you prefer?"

"I haven't decided yet."

That was, in its own way, the single most terrifying sentence I'd heard since arriving in this world.

---

Then she did something worse.

She reached into her *own* notebook.

Her imperial notebook. Black leather, gold trim, pages filled with handwriting I couldn't read from this distance.

She flipped it open to a marked page. There was a small scale drawing of a light latticework, sketched in military drafting style.

"Then indulge me," she said. "A thought experiment."

"Of course."

"The eastern dormitory wing, fifth floor. There's a stretch of exterior wall along the lecture corridor where the floor resonance changes."

'Oh no.'

"Old construction. The floorboards sound hollow when struck, but only at one point, roughly twelve feet long. The wall beyond that length is just... stone."

'Oh no no no no—'

"From the room adjacent," she said, voice perfectly level, "the wall is standard-width. No anomaly. But the *length* of the corridor, the floor's slight dip at that point, and a faint acoustic echo suggest a chamber beyond the stone wall that exists on no floor plan."

'I know EXACTLY what that is.'

'Volume 5. The Sealed Reliquary.'

'Pre-Collapse artifact. Holy-class item. Three game runs. You enter through that wall by offering a prayer at midnight during a lunar eclipse with a blood-stained relic key—'

"What would you say," Iris asked, watching me, "is behind it?"

And then.

Time.

Stopped.

---

In a game, this would be a dialogue choice.

Three options, probably, labeled in white text:

1. *Tell her about the Sealed Reliquary.*
2. *Say you don't know.*
3. *Say something clever but evasive.*

Option 1 raised her affinity and unlocked hidden intel but raised suspicion. Option 2 sounded suspicious but safe. Option 3 bought time but implied knowledge.

Except this wasn't a game.

No, wait — it was. Technically. But I was *inside* it now, and there were no save points and no way to try all three options.

This was real.

This was a real princess, staring at me with those unreadable violet eyes, asking a question whose answer contained a navigational key to Volume 5's most crucial locked content.

'She knows something is there.'

'She asked me on purpose.'

'This is a trap.'

'This is 100%, without a doubt, a trap.'

"I..."

My mouth was dry.

I forced myself to swallow. Took a polite sip of tea. Bought a second. Two seconds. Three.

'Don't hesitate. Container yourself. Say nothing useful. Don't—'

"I haven't been to the eastern wing, Your Highness."

Technically true.

Technically, *technically* true. My body hadn't been there. My hands had never run down those actual walls. I'd explored them in the game — pixel by pixel, in a digital world — but the physical version of those walls existed in this real Harem-Eschaton-World for the first time.

Semantics. Pure semantics.

But a technically true statement was the hardest kind of lie to detect.

And Iris Aurelia's eyes — which had been watching me the entire time — flickered.

A spark.

Something caught.

Whatever it was — the micro-pause, the flicker of my pupils, the exact degree of my throat movement as I swallowed before answering — she caught it.

Like a hawk.

I felt the moment it happened. Like a pin dropping in a quiet room.

"Of course," Iris said mildly.

And without comment, without expression, she opened her own notebook to a fresh page, dipped her pen, and wrote something down.

A few careful letters.

I couldn't see them.

'She's writing down that I lied.'

'She's writing "Lucen Gray lies about east wing."'

'She's writing "knows about sealed door."'

'She's filing it away. She's COLLECTING. Collecting like a sociopathic butterfly collector—

'Hi there, sorry, but does anyone have a spare escape route—'

Then she smiled.

"I believe you've said enough about maps for today," she murmured. "Shall we continue with the translation portion of the session?"

"...Yeah," I croaked. "Sure."

The session continued.

For the next forty minutes, I translated meaningless cipher symbols into meaninglessly plausible fake meanings, all while my heart rate refused to drop below triple digits.

Iris was pleasant.

Cordial.

Almost warm, even.

And it was so, *so* much worse than if she'd just called the Imperial guard.

---

Later — later — when the sky outside had bruised into evening and the candles had burned halfway down their holders, the session wound to an end.

She flipped the notebook shut.

"Before you go."

"Your Highness?"

"Your performance in the First Evaluation Exam."

I stiffened.

"...What about it?"

She turned her teacup slowly on its saucer. Porcelain whispered against porcelain.

"You fight," she said softly, "like you're going to die."

I didn't respond.

"Not like you're afraid of dying." She said it the way one might describe a bird they'd been observing. "Like you've already decided you're going to die. And you're just... delaying it."

The fire popped.

"As long as possible."

I couldn't move.

'...'

In 347 hours of gameplay, across every route, every ending, every heartbreak and every triumphant victory screen, no one — *no one* — had ever said that out loud.

Not one of the heroines. Not Lucas Ashford, with his cheat-class eyes. Not the Imperial generals. Not the narrators.

They saw me at my most desperate and described courage. They saw me at my coldest and called it calculation. They saw my long grinding campaigns across academy and battlefield alike and labeled it determination.

Nobody ever said what Iris Aurelia just said.

That I fought like a dead man.

Like a corpse still buying hours.

Because that's what I was.

"I'm not—" I started.

"Don't."

Her voice wasn't cold. It wasn't warm, either.

Just level. Absolute. Imperial.

"Don't insult me with an immediate denial," she said. "Not tonight. I've grown... attached to accurate data."

Attached to—

"Observe, then," I said weakly, "may I ask what you've concluded?"

Iris set down her cup.

"That your survival," she said, "is not an accident."

I stood to go.

My legs carried me. Miraculously. Nineteen years of muscular training and thirty-four minutes of sheer adrenal panic and they still remembered how to work, bless them.

I made it to the door.

My hand touched the handle.

And then her voice, quiet and calm and so very *final*, came from behind me.

"Lucen."

I froze.

I didn't turn.

"The passage in the eastern wing," she said. "There's a sealed door behind the changed stonework. It's warded against detection. No student should be able to sense it."

Pause.

A long one.

"But you knew it was there."

...

...

Not a question.

A statement.

My pulse hammered in my temples. My hand lay on the bronze doorknob, cold and inert.

'Don't turn around.'

'Don't explain.'

'Don't even blink the wrong way.'

Because if I denied it, I'd be lying to her face, and she'd see it, and she'd add it to the growing collection.

And if I confirmed it...

Confirmed that a powerless, mana-index-0.009, bottom-of-the-barrel first-year commoner somehow *knew* about a magically sealed, undetectable door known only to the highest level of Imperial intelligence—

'The word "espionage" doesn't even begin to cover it.'

'I'd be signing my own death warrant in triplicate.'

So I said the only thing left.

Nothing.

I opened the door. Stepped out into the corridor. And walked, slowly, carefully, like a man leaving a shrine he does not dare desecrate.

I didn't look back.

Behind me, the door closed softly.

Click.

The silence of the hallway swallowed everything.

And for nearly a full minute, I just stood there, stared at the wall, and quietly, fervently, did not breathe.
