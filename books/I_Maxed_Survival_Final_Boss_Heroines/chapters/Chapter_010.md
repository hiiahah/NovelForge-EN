*SURVIVAL STATUS REPORT — WEEK ONE.*

*Prepared by: Lucen Gray. Former completionist. Current bread-based lifeform.*

Let's start with the numbers, because numbers don't lie.

That's my job now.

**[Remaining Strategy: Don't die.]**

That's it. That's the whole strategy.

Five days in this academy and my grand master plan has been distilled down from thirty-seven documented bullet points into two words and a prayer.

I stared at the bread roll in my hand.

The bread roll stared back.

We had an understanding, the two of us. It was going to sacrifice itself for the cause, and I was going to feel guilty about it in about forty-five seconds when the hunger came back.

*Provisional student stipend, ladies and gentlemen.*

In the game, the stipend screen was a cute little UI window. *"You've received your weekly allowance!"* Sparkle sound effect. Little coin animation.

In real life, it was a clerk sliding three copper coins across a counter without making eye contact, like I was a stain on the academy's budget ledger.

Three copper coins a week.

Do you know what three copper buys at the academy commissary?

One bread roll a day. One. The kind that doubles as masonry material. I'm fairly certain the could kill a man if thrown with sufficient velocity and intent.

Two kilograms.

I've lost two kilograms in five days.

At this rate of linear decay, I did the math in my head — and I did the math twice, because when you're poor, arithmetic is the only entertainment you can afford — and the conclusion was that I had roughly *nine weeks* before my body achieved 'ghost' class classification and I could phase through walls.

Silver lining: wall-phasing would dramatically improve my escape options.

*'Priorities, Lucen.'*

I took a bite. It fought back. I won.

Barely.

The Starfall Courtyard was quiet this time of day. Safer. The noble students took their meals in the grand dining hall, which had chandeliers, house banners, and a strict unspoken hierarchy of seating that made the Korean subway at rush hour look egalitarian.

I sat alone on a bench at the courtyard's edge.

Not because I was brooding.

Because the bench was free, the sightlines were good, and from here I could see all four courtyard exits.

What?

It's called *defensive dining*. Very common among the prey population.

I chewed my weapon-grade bread and watched the students flow past in little clusters — laughing, talking, complaining about professors, comparing dueling scores.

Normal people. Living the normal fantasy-academy life.

For half a second, something ugly and small curled up in my chest.

I'd played this academy so many times on a screen. I knew where the hidden events triggered. I knew which bench had a secret dialogue option if you sat on it at midnight during a blood moon.

But sitting here — actually *sitting* here — with stale bread and a weekly duel with a Tyrant on my calendar...

'Yeah. The graphics are better in person. That's about it.'

I was halfway through the roll when I noticed him.

Golden hair. Straight posture. The kind of face that came pre-installed with orchestral background music.

Lucas Ashford. The protagonist. The Hero of Route Canon.

He was standing near the fountain with his usual orbit of admirers, laughing at something someone said — and then, mid-laugh, his eyes swept the courtyard the way a searchlight sweeps a prison yard.

They landed on me.

They stayed on me.

One second. Two.

*'Nope.'*

I looked down at my bread with the sudden, intense fascination of a man who had just discovered the meaning of life baked into a dinner roll.

When I risked a glance back up, he was looking away again.

But something cold was crawling up my spine. The same feeling I'd gotten in the game, right before an unskippable cutscene.

Lucas Ashford was not listening to the conversation around him.

He was smiling in all the right places — he'd long since learned that the smile mattered more than the listening — but his thoughts were three courtyards away, sitting on a bench with stale bread and gray eyes.

*"Lucas? Lucas, you're doing it again."* Mira from Class 1-A nudged his shoulder. "You spaced out. What's so interesting over there?"

"Nothing," he said.

It wasn't nothing.

He'd seen the boy twice now since the entrance procession. Both times from a distance. Both times alone. A provisional student — servant class, if the rumors were right — enrolled through the emergency provision, the one almost nobody used because almost nobody qualified.

The boy sat like a soldier. Ate like a prisoner. Watched the courtyard like he was memorizing it.

And he never once looked like he wanted company.

Most commoners who clawed their way into Asterion Academy wore their hunger openly — the desperate networking, the clingy flattery, the frantic grabbing at any noble hand extended their way. Lucas had seen it a hundred times. He understood it. He even pitied it.

This boy did none of it.

He just... sat alone. With his single roll of bread. Refusing, by some quiet act of will, to be ashamed of it.

*'He doesn't want to burden anyone,'* Lucas thought, and something in his chest pulled tight.

Nobility wasn't about blood. His sword instructor had told him that, years ago, and Lucas had built himself around the idea. *Nobility is how a man carries what he's given.*

That boy had been given almost nothing.

And he carried it like a king.

"Lucas." Mira again, laughing. "Seriously, what are you looking at?"

"A classmate," Lucas said.

And then, quieter, to himself — because he hadn't told anyone yet. He hadn't even sorted it out in his own head.

Three weeks before the term started, on the southern road, there'd been a patrol report. A merchant convoy hit by bandits. Survivors scattered. And among the dead — a boy. A servant, the report said. Young. Unclaimed by anyone. No family came forward.

Lucas had read that report because he read *all* of them, because his father said a future lord reads the casualty lists, not just the victory tallies.

An unclaimed servant boy. Right age. Road running toward the capital.

And now, an academy with a bruised, gray-eyed servant boy enrolled through an emergency clause, on a scholarship path that existed for almost nobody.

Lucas Ashford did not believe in coincidence.

He believed in names, and debts, and the things a person owed the dead — even the dead he'd never met.

*'Tomorrow,'* he decided, finishing his drink and setting the cup down with quiet finality. *'Tomorrow, I'll talk to him.'*

If the boy wouldn't come to anyone for help, then someone would have to go to him.

That was just the kind of man Lucas Ashford intended to be.

He had absolutely no idea that the boy in question would, upon learning of this plan, have willingly chewed through the courtyard wall to escape it.

Evening came down over Asterion like a lid on a pot.

I took the long way back to the provisional dormitory. Not for the scenery.

For the memory.

*Left at the armory annex. Past the old chapel. Service corridor behind the refectory — ten seconds faster than the main path, but only after the seventh bell, when the cleaning staff clears out. Drainage grate near the west wall — loose, three bolts, big enough for a desperate idiot of my exact dimensions.*

Route memorization.

Back when I was a gamer punished for my hubris, I speedran the academy's stealth sections. Faulty habit? No. *Investment.* I was reconstructing the map in muscle memory, one pathway at a time, until my legs knew the escape routes better than my brain did.

Did it look suspicious? A lone student walking in slow loops, muttering, occasionally touching walls?

Probably.

Do I care?

Also probably. But not enough to stop.

The halls thinned as I walked. Lamplight. Long shadows. My shoes making lonely sounds against old stone.

And then I turned a corner I'd never turned before — a shortcut the old map in my head said should exist — and stopped.

Glass.

An entire wing made of glass walls, rising out of the side of the academy like the ribcage of some enormous frozen animal. Fog rolled inside it. Frost feathered across every pane in white fractal whorls.

The Frozen Greenhouse.

I knew this place.

Oh, I *knew* this place.

It was a location from the game. A sub-zero botanical wing, maintained by enchantment for arctic mana-flora. On screen, it had been pretty. A date spot. A place where one particular heroine had an affection event under aurora-lit ice flowers.

My footsteps had gone silent.

There was someone inside.

A girl, sitting on a bench among the frozen blooms, with a book open on her lap.

Her hair was pale blue — so pale it was nearly white, the color of the first ten minutes of a sunrise seen through a blizzard. It fell over her shoulders and pooled beside her like spilled moonlight.

Ice flowers bloomed all around her — real ones, blue and silver, the kind that could freeze the finger off any idiot who touched them.

And from her fingertips, resting casual as anything against the bench, frost was *growing*.

Crystalline patterns spread from her hand across the wood in slow fractals — blooming, branching, dying back, blooming again. Idle. Absent-minded. The way another girl might doodle in the margins of her notebook.

Except what she was doodling could flash-freeze a human being solid in 2.4 seconds.

I knew the number. It was in the boss-fight mechanics.

She turned a page.

She looked...

Peaceful. Angelic. Like something a Renaissance painter would invent if he'd been hired to paint *winter itself, but make her kind.*

My blood had gone cold, and not from the greenhouse.

Celestia Rimehart.

Alice.

*The Abyssal Witch.*

That's right. Six-plus catalogued bad endings just for her, and she was sitting there reading a book like a finance major killing time between lectures.

Route 2. The playful one. The teasing one. The one who'd grab your sleeve and whine until you carried her books, and then giggle into your shoulder, and you would think — the player would think — *what a clingy, cute, harmless girl.*

Right up until you found the circles.

The summoning geometry hidden under her dormitory floorboards. The thing she was contractually bound to. The way her affection stat wasn't a stat at all — it was an *appetite.*

And the bad end. God, the bad end.

[Bad End No. 63: Skin for Love.]

I'd triggered it once, as a completionist, the way you'd lick a battery. Out of sheer dumb completion hunger.

I had nightmares for a week.

Because Alice didn't do jealous rage. Alice didn't do murder. Alice did something so much worse: Alice *loved you past the point where you ended*, and she simply... refused to accept the border between herself and the thing she loved.

She'd decided, in that route, that separation was a distance, and distance was a wound, and wounds should be *closed.*

The CG had been beautifully drawn. That's the worst part. It had been *beautifully drawn.*

I'd put the game down and stared at my ceiling until 4 AM.

And now she was twenty meters away, behind frosted glass, in a world with no exit button.

*Walk away.*

I started walking.

Slow. Casual. A random student on a random evening, nothing to see here, just a background asset trundling past the scenery—

She looked up from her book.

Through the frosted glass, through the fog, her eyes found mine like they'd been waiting at that exact spot for me to arrive.

Pale eyes. Glass-blue. The color of deep ice, the kind they find ancient things frozen inside.

She smiled.

It was a lovely smile.

Soft. Sweet. First-day-of-spring smile.

And on the bench beside her, the frost fractals — which had been blooming in random, idle patterns all this time — stopped.

Stopped all at once. Like music cutting off.

Then, slowly, they began to grow again.

Not randomly this time.

In a shape.

Five branches, fanning from a single stem. Longer at the edges, thicker at the base. The frost curled and articulated itself with dreadful, patient precision — and I stood there watching a hand take shape in ice against the glass, fingers spreading, palm pressing outward.

Toward the pane.

Toward me.

Reaching.

Through the frost-misted glass, Alice tilted her head. Still smiling. And I could see her lips move, shaping words I couldn't hear through the wall.

I didn't need to hear them.

I'd played this game. I could lip-read dialogue options in my sleep.

*"What are you?"*

I walked.

I did not run, because Predator Awareness 101: running triggers the chase response, and my chase response statistics against an S-class ice witch were a funeral announcement.

So I walked. Steady. Even. Dignified, if you squinted.

The moment I cleared the greenhouse's sightline around the corner, my legs filed for independence from the rest of my body and I *sprinted*.

Down the corridor. Left at the armory. Service corridor. The works — every memorized shortcut, deployed all at once, a greatest-hits album of cowardice.

Behind me, faintly, through three walls and most of my panic, I thought I heard a bell.

No.

A laugh.

Light and silver and delighted, like wind chimes in a blizzard.

*'She found me interesting.'*

Of all the heroines in all the routes in all the godforsaken academies, the Abyssal Witch looked at a 0.009-mana background extra and found him *interesting.*

My stats were so low they wrapped back around to being exotic.

To everyone else, I was a weakling. To her senses — to whatever abyss-class perception looked out from behind those winter eyes — a mana reading of 0.009 wasn't weakness.

It was a hole.

A person-shaped hole in the fabric of the world, walking around eating terrible bread.

*'Of course. Of COURSE. I spent five years avoiding detection by maxing survival, and the universe makes my shitty mana level the fantasy equivalent of a neon sign that says — ANOMALY HERE. FASCINATING. COLLECT ONE.'*

I made it back to the provisional dormitory, locked the door, wedged my chair under the handle, and sat on my bed with my back against the wall and my eyes on the window.

Thirty minutes later, my heart rate finally dropped below 'hummingbird on espresso'.

I added a new rule to the list. The mental list, because paper was a snitch and a co-conspirator.

*Rule 6: Never take the shortcut past the Frozen Greenhouse again.*

*Rule 6 addendum: Actually, never take shortcuts at all. Shortcuts are how DLC characters find you.*

*Rule 6 addendum 2: She asked "what are you." Not "who." Not "why were you staring." WHAT. Inanimate-object grammar. Do with that information what you will, and then sleep.* (I will not sleep.)

I stared at the ceiling.

Two heroines at full activation. One at proximity. The protagonist scheduling me into his beautiful heroic calendar. A decryption duel with a Tyrant starting Tuesday. A stipend of three rocks a week. And now an abyssal ice witch decorating furniture with frost hieroglyphs addressed specifically to me.

Week One, and this was the report card.

*'Week Two is going to kill me.'*

I didn't know it yet.

But I was half right.

Two wings, one courtyard, and an entire social hierarchy away, in a private study with curtains the color of wine and old victories, Iris Aurelia had not moved in two hours.

Lamps burned low on either side of her desk.

Between them lay the notebook.

She had filled six pages of her own with notes. Frequency tallies — the strange angular symbols, counted and ranked, the recurring ones separated from the rare ones. Spacing analysis. Line-break structure. Hypotheses crossed out and redrawn.

The script was unlike anything in the Imperial archives. Not Old Valthessian. Not the coastal trade-cants. Not any cipher family her tutors had drilled into her across a decade of education designed, quite literally, to make her impossible to lie to.

And yet it was *obviously* structured.

That was the maddening part. You could feel the grammar under the symbols, like bones under skin. Clusters repeated in consistent positions. Cadence. Intention.

This was not noise.

This was a language, or the next thing to one — and a servant boy from nowhere had filled a whole notebook with it.

Iris leaned back in her chair and pressed her fingertips together.

Frequency. Structure. Repetition.

Her eyes caught on one cluster she had circled three times already.

Four symbols. Compact. Appearing once, sometimes twice, in every section of the notebook — in the early pages and the late ones, in the long blocks and the short fragmented notes that ended in violent scratch-outs.

A recurring signature.

In her experience, things a person wrote over and over, involuntarily, across every subject and every mood, were almost always one of three things.

A prayer.

A fear.

Or a name.

She traced the four symbols with one fingertip, slowly, the way you'd trace the lettering on a sealed letter you hadn't decided whether to open.

아이리스.

She did not know what it said.

She would have stood up and walked out of the room if she'd known. Perhaps. She was honest enough with herself, alone at her desk, to admit that the 'perhaps' existed.

"What are you hiding, Lucen Gray?" she asked the empty study.

The candles offered no opinion.

She thought about his face. The flat gray composure he'd worn across her desk, that too-smooth surface with hairline cracks in it — cracks she had noticed, and catalogued, and decided not to mention yet, because you did not tell an interesting puzzle that it was almost solved.

Two meetings a week. Tuesday and Friday.

Plenty of time.

She'd crack the script first. Then the boy.

And she made a small, private note at the bottom of her sixth page, in immaculate Imperial cursive, unaware that the sentiment was spreading independently, at that very hour, across three completely different minds in three completely different corners of the academy:

*He is hiding something. I will know what it is.*

None of them — not the Princess, not the Hero, not the Witch — had guessed correctly yet.

None of them were even asking the right question.

Iris closed the notebook.

Tuesday, something in her chest noted, with a quiet precision that had nothing to do with politics and that she chose not to examine, was only four days away.
