# Chapter 35: Mock Duel Festival — Day 1: Opening Ceremonies

Day 28.

Morning.

The Mock Duel Festival had swallowed Stellaris Royal Academy whole.

Banners stretched between every tower — enchanted fabric that rippled with the school crest, igniting in harmless bursts of gold and silver flame every time a breeze passed. Floating lanterns bobbed at head height down every walkway. Temporary barriers shimmered around the Grand Arena like heat distortion, visible only when the sun hit them at the right angle.

Food stalls lined the path from the dormitories.

Actual stalls. Sugar-glazed mana fruit on skewers. Fried dough dusted with moon-sugar. Some kid was selling "hero-themed commemorative tankards" that he had definitely carved himself, badly, from enchanted oak.

There was a fortune teller.

There was a booth where students could test their mana output by punching a training golem.

There was a magical demonstration pavilion where third-year enchanters were showing off some kind of levitation array while a professor supervised with the exhausted expression of a man who knew exactly what was about to go wrong.

It looked like fun.

If you squinted, ignored the apocalyptic countdown ticking behind your eyes, and weren't scheduled to fight a noble's champion in front of the entire student body before a Lovecraftian horror detonated under everyone's feet, you might even call it festive.

I couldn't notice any of it.

I was too terrified.

'Eleven hours and forty-two minutes.'

'That's enough time to walk the grounds, monitor Alice's containment regression, mentally rehearse the Void Counter seventy-seven more times, and still have an hour left over to hyperventilate.'

'Peak time management.'

'Truly, I am a legend.'

I stood just outside the arena grounds, watching students flow past toward the morning matches, and ran the numbers until they smeared into meaningless static.

Then I felt a presence beside me.

"Hey."

I flipped my head.

Alice.

She had her hands clasped behind her back and was smiling up at me in that way she had — the smile that made the air around her shimmer slightly, the way a winter lake glitters.

The smile that made my chest constrict every time.

"Alice," I said. "You... came to watch?"

"Of course." She tilted her head. "You're fighting tonight, aren't you?"

"Yeah."

"Then obviously I'm here."

Simple as that.

No hesitation. No qualification. 'Obviously.'

'She has no idea what she does to me.'

"Also," she added, glancing toward the festival stalls, "I saw a stand selling candied frost-pears, and I want one."

"You're just here for the snacks."

"I'm here for both. Don't be greedy."

"...Fair."

---

We walked the festival grounds together. Alice bought her candied frost-pear. I bought something involving bread and questionable meat because my combat instincts demanded sustenance at irregular intervals and I hadn't eaten since last night's anxiety-dried crackers.

The morning matches were starting inside the arena.

The stands were filling up.

"Want to go watch?" Alice asked, her ice-crystallized fingers brushing a frost pattern onto the vendor's counter as she set down her coins.

The frost was getting faster.

'I noticed.'

'I didn't say anything.'

'Not yet.'

'Not yet.'

"Sure," I said. "Let's go."

---

---

The Grand Arena was, as always, arrogant.

White stone terraces ascending in concentric rings. Enchanted combat wards visible as translucent hexagonal lattices across the fighting floor. The VIP gallery hovered above everything — a floating tier where professors and nobles observed with the comfortable detachment of people whose children weren't about to fight each other.

We took seats in the middle section. Upper rows, decent sight lines, close enough to the fighter's entrance that Lucen could keep an eye on the corridor where the archers and spearmen — no, Knight cadets — would emerge.

I scanned the stands.

There.

Three rows below, slightly left. Finn. Sitting straight up, jiggling one leg, already talking at a pace that made my lip twitch.

I didn't acknowledge him yet.

I had other priorities.

The light was shifting. The morning matches were beginning.

---

Lucas Ashford walked out first.

Of course he did.

The crowd's reaction was nothing short of religious ecstasy. Girls in the front rows screamed. Boys in the back rows started chanting his name. Someone unrolled a homemade banner that said 'LUCAS ASHFORD, HEIR OF THE SUN,' which was legally questionable given that he was a minor and also not actually affiliated with any sun-related deity, but I kept that editorialization to myself.

Lucas waved.

Casual. Easy. His golden hair caught the morning light like it had been designed for this moment.

'Protagonist.'

'Actual protagonist.'

'In a world that has one. Insane.'

His first opponent stepped out. A third-year, broad-shouldered, heavy sword. The boy looked solid. Competent. Probably had a week of training, a decent manor, an okay family sigil.

He was going to lose.

The referee signaled. The wards hummed.

Lucas drew his blade.

Three seconds.

One feint, one disarm, one clean strike to the flat of the opponent's blade that sent the heavy sword spinning across the arena floor and embedding itself in a ward barrier with a comical *thunk.*

The crowd erupted.

Lucas extended his hand to the defeated boy, helped him up, clapped his shoulder with a modest smile.

'He's good at this.'

'The "heroic prince" thing. The hand-up after the win. He does it perfectly. Without even thinking about it.'

'I wonder... how much of that is training, and how much is just who he is.'

'I'm not jealous.'

'I'm not jealous.'

The crowd roared louder.

Fine, maybe a little jealous.

---

Lucas fought three opponents in total that morning.

Same pattern every time. Elegant footwork, precise counters, no wasted motion. He toyed with a spear-wielder for almost ninety seconds — I counted, because I was bored and also because I was already cataloging his sparring data against the hero-tier reflexes I'd need tonight — before executing a move I recognized from the advanced game routes:

'He dodged left, let the thrust eat his shield, and then...'

Lucas's blade swept low, caught the spear-boy's ankle, and sent him rolling across the ward floor like a sack of potatoes.

'Sun-Breaker Rotation. He used the Sun-Breaker Rotation.'

'That's a B-tier technique.'

'He's thirteen.'

'Life isn't fair.'

---

Then came the afternoon matches, and the mood shifted entirely.

Rhea Valtor.

The crowd's energy didn't drop. It changed. It became heavy, expectant, uncomfortable — like breath held past its natural duration.

She stepped onto the arena floor in standard-issue sparring gear. No wait — that was far too dramatic a word for her entry. She simply was walking, the way a predator moves from one place to another because it's going there regardless of whether anyone is watching.

Her first opponent was a mage-knightsman. Fourth year. Solid mana index. Good tactical discipline.

The referee signaled.

Rhea moved.

I blinked.

The arena floor was empty where the mage-knightsman had been standing. He was on the other side of the ward, pinned against the barrier, weapon gone, uniform torn at a precise line across his chest that hadn't quite broken skin.

Eleven seconds.

The crowd didn't cheer.

They made a sound like the intake of breath before a scream.

Rhea turned toward the exit without looking back.

Her expression was flat. Not bored. Bored would imply engagement with the idea of boredom. She looked like she'd conducted a complex calculation and arrived at the only possible answer with zero margin for narrative satisfaction.

'She's strong.'

'I knew she was strong. I trained with her yesterday. I know her speed, her reach, her precision.'

'But knowing and seeing are different.'

'And when you see it...'

'...you remember that she's the one who tried to kill me in the library. And that she's stronger than she was then.'

'And that Voss knows we trained together.'

'And that the two people Voss understands least in this entire academy are sitting in this arena right now, and one of them is me.'

I looked away from the arena.

Tried to settle my breathing.

'Focus. The plan. Fourteen minutes. Nine hundred meters. Two hundred kids.'

'Don't think about Rhea.'

'Don't think about the Evil Goddess not laughing.'

---

Finn found me two matches later.

Wedged himself into the seat on my left — Alice was on my right — and immediately started talking at combat speed.

"DID YOU SEE THAT? Did you SEE her?! She knocked that guy's sword OUT OF HIS HAND with her BARE HANDS. Bare. HANDS. I didn't even see it. I blinked and he was on the FLOOR."

"Finn."

"And Lucas! LUCAS! Did you see the Sun-Breaker Rotation? That was a SUN-BREAKER ROTATION. I've only ever seen that in scroll illustrations. In the ARCHIVES. And he did it on his FIRST TRY, in front of an actual crowd—"

"Finn."

"And YOURS! Your match! Tonight! It's the closing bout! The whole school is going to be there! Everyone knows who you are now — the provisional student who broke the crystal! The one who read the dead language! You didn't even FLINCH when Derrick Holt challenged you! They're calling you the "Ice-Cold Provisional!" I heard it in the hallways! Someone made a nickname, Lucen! A NICKNAME!"

"Finn."

"Are you scared? You look scared. You look really scared. Are you actually scared? Should I be scared for you? I can be scared for you if you're too busy to be scared yourself—"

"Finn."

"Yeah?"

"Please stop talking."

"...Okay."

Three seconds of silence.

Then, quieter: "But like — really though. Are you scared?"

Alice leaned her head against my shoulder.

Her breath was even. Relaxed. The way it had been in the dorm common room, in the library, in all those small, quiet moments that had somehow accumulated into something I hadn't fully accounted for.

"I'm not scared," Alice said softly, not lifting her head. "He's going to win."

'No pressure though.'

'No pressure.'

"Thanks, Alice," I said.

"Don't mention it. I have faith."

'I wish I shared it.'

---

By late afternoon, the arena had emptied for the midday break. The crowd drifted toward the food stalls. The stalls were doing good business — even the heroic-tankard kid had accumulated a small crowd of sympathetic buyers.

I found a bench on the east terrace, overlooking the tournament brackets I'd studiously avoided investigating. Alice followed. She was still carrying her frost-pear, which she'd eaten painfully slowly, licking the sugar off her fingers in a way that made me stare at the ceiling and recite multiplication tables.

She sat down.

Beside me.

Close.

'Normal couple behavior. We're normal.'

"We're not normal," I muttered.

Alice blinked.

"You said that out loud, didn't you."

"...Yeah."

She grinned. The grin was dangerous. It had a specific angle to it — I had catalogued it as Smirk Variant 4, deployable when I was vulnerable and she felt playful.

"This is fun," she said. "I haven't been to a festival since I was little."

Her hand dropped from her lap.

Between us.

Resting on the wooden seat bench.

Ice bloomed.

It started subtly — a latticework of frost creeping outward from her fingertips, spreading along the grain of the wood in branching fractals. Not the gentle decorative frost of her usual ambient leakage. This was dense. Intricate. Deliberate in a way her deliberate magic never was.

I glanced down.

The crystals were forming geometric patterns.

'...oh.'

'Oh no.'

'Those aren't random.'

'Those are Abyssal containment sigils.'

'Tier 2.'

'Gods.'

She's not doing this. This isn't her intention. These are involuntary structural outputs — the entity underneath Alice's skin is running containment protocols, imprinting control architecture onto the material world through her fingertips, and she doesn't even know it's happening.

"Alice."

"Mm?"

"How are you feeling?"

She tilted her head toward me. Her amber eyes caught the afternoon light and threw back something pale and distant, like looking into two windows from the wrong side.

"Happy," she said. "I'm with you."

The frost beneath her hand deepened.

I watched a single crystalline structure extend past the edge of the bench and freeze a thread of grass beneath it solid.

Tier 3.

'Five hours to the duel.'

'Six hours to containment failure.'

'And she just used the word "happy" the way a bomb technician reports the countdown approaching zero.'

"The noise," I said carefully. "All the people. The excitement. Is that—does that feel like... too much?"

Alice considered this with the solemnity of a drunk philosopher.

"It's a lot. But it's okay. Because you're here." She leaned her head back against my shoulder, and I felt the cold of her cheek through the fabric of my uniform. "Everything's always okay when you're here."

'She means it.'

'That's the problem.'

'That's exactly the problem.'

The entity didn't feed on ambient mana.

The entity fed on emotional intensity. On the turbulence of a fifteen-year-old girl in a crowd for the first time in her life, leaning against the boy she'd chosen, one hour until his public combat, six hours until her containment ruptured, and her fingertip currently freezing decorative fracture patterns into public property.

'Crowds. Noise. Excitement. Me fighting in front of everyone. Stress about my duel.'

'Every single trigger in the same three-hour window.'

'This isn't a date.'

'This is a countdown with a girl sitting on it.'

I stared forward.

The arena stretched empty before us, waiting for tonight.

'My duel is at 8:30.'

'Containment fails at approximately 11:30.'

'That leaves exactly one hour of margin between winning, surviving, and somehow calming Alice down AND simultaneously getting to the dorm to intercept the rampaging entity before the audience disperses AND before Voss connects two more data points AND—'

'You know what.'

'I'm just going to keep breathing.'

'Breathing is good.'

---

I escorted Alice back toward the festival grounds as afternoon tilted toward evening. Her frost hadn't melted on the bench. Someone had thrown a cloth over it and was standing nearby, arguing with a professor about whether it was an ice magic exercise or an act of vandalism.

I bought her another candied frost-pear at the stall because I couldn't think of any other efficient coping mechanism and my own panic was calibrated badly.

We stood near the edge of the arena entrance as the late-day light began to fail.

The crowds were returning. The evening matches ran from sunset to midnight — the festival's main event stretch. The atmosphere was thickening. Students were already filling the stands.

"Tonight," Alice said, "I'm going to watch."

"I know."

"I'm going to sit in the front row."

"That's — you don't have to do that. The front row is the worst. It's — it's very loud."

"I want to see you."

'She's... sincere.'

'Completely sincere.'

'And completely naive to the fact that her own subconscious is actively preparing to kill everyone in that front row.'

"I'll wave to you," she continued, obliviously.

"Please don't," I said. "Waving is — actually, could you sit anywhere but the front row? I need... I need room. To — to breathe. I'll do better if I know you're not watching."

The look she gave me was skeptical but accepting.

"Okay. I'll sit three rows back."

"Perfect."

"And you'll wave to ME."

"...Fine."

Compromise.

'I just bargained with the vessel of a Lovecraftian entity over seating arrangements. This is my life now. This is who I am. Kang Min-jun, 23, suddenly in a body he's had for eleven years, negotiating ripple effects with a god's daughter.'

---

That's when I saw her.

Crossing the quadrangle between the arena and the faculty building, moving at the unhurried pace of a seasoned administrator who had seen seven thousand festivals and was immune to atmosphere.

Vice-Headmistress Elara Voss.

Silver hair swept back. Dark uniform-edgest robes. The kind of presence that recalibrated ambient temperature without trying. She was smiling.

She was always smiling.

It was her weapon.

She passed close to us — not close enough to speak, but close enough to track our faces, the way a teacher clocking two students would.

Alice did not look at her.

Alice was examining a floating poster for tonight's duel bracket and didn't notice.

Voss's gaze swept past her.

Landed on me.

Brief.

Courteous.

The smile didn't shift. The eyes didn't change shape. The recognition, if there was any, was invisible.

And yet —

She glanced down.

At the bench we'd been sitting on, twenty meters behind us, covered in frost-blooming geometric patterns that Alice had left behind without noticing.

Voss's eyes —

Flickered.

Not much. A fraction of a second. The dimensionless recognition of a mind that had spent forty years cataloging unusual phenomena and had just catalogued this one without effort.

Her smile did not waver.

She walked on.

Toward the faculty entrance. Toward her office, which contained approximately four thousand dossiers on every active anomaly in Stellaris's student body.

Toward — I suddenly and viscerally knew — a mental filing system where Alice's frost patterns and the dormitory's corrupted crystal and the provisional student's void-signature signature and the mysterious library visitation and Derrick Holt's noble-backed challenge were all being cross-referenced at machine speed.

'She saw it.'

'She saw the frost.'

'She saw me sitting next to Alice.'

'She connected the node disruption last week with the ice patterns and the strange boy who keeps showing up in places he shouldn't, and she just filed it away like she files everything else — efficiently, completely, with the smile that means she already knows what step two is.'

'She doesn't know what the entity is yet. But she knows something is wrong. And she knows I'm involved.'

'And she knows Alice is the vector.'

The smile never changed.

That's what made it terrifying.

I stood in the middle of a festival full of happy teenagers, with frost blooming on a bench behind me and a love-struck entity in human form beside me, and felt the ground tilt.

'Five hours to the duel.'

'Six hours to the calamity.'

'And the headmistress just decided that Alice Gray is a problem to be solved.'

'No.'

'Not yet.'

'But soon. When she connects the last piece, she will act. Teachers always act. It's their job. They can't help it.'

'And if Voss moves against Alice — if she separates her from the only stabilizing influence in Alice's life — if she puts her in "protective containment" while the entity is still reaching critical mass...'

'Then it doesn't matter how fast I run my match. It doesn't matter how many late-night training sessions I survived with Rhea Valtor. It doesn't matter how perfectly I execute the Void Counter.'

'Because Alice will be alone in that room. And the entity will escalate.'

'And the Bad End happens anyway.'

I looked down at Alice.

She was watching a group of first-years try to climb a demonstration pillar while a professor yelled at them, and she was laughing.

A real laugh. Unrestrained. Nothing holding it in place.

'I can't let Voss isolate her.'

'I can't let the entity rupture.'

'I have to win the duel.'

'I have to keep Alice calm through the event window.'

'And I have to do all of it while pretending that none of this is happening.'
