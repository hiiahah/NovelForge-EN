# Chapter 17: The Evaluation Brackets

The healing mage at the infirmary had looked at my shoulder, frowned, and asked what training equipment had done it.

"A person," I'd said.

"I meant what *weapon*."

"Hands."

She hadn't believed me. Fair enough. I wouldn't have believed me either.

The good news: dislocation, not a break, and magical healing at Stellaris Royal Academy was efficient enough that by Sunday evening I could lift my arm to shoulder height without making sounds that set off the wildlife.

The bad news: everything else.

---

Sunday evening. First-year dormitory common hall. The bulletin board.

Or, as it had apparently become while I was being mended, the single hottest tourist destination in the entire academy.

There were at least forty students crammed around a cork board roughly the size of a dinner table.

'Huh. I've seen smaller crowds at MMO server launches.'

The Evaluation Exam brackets had been posted.

And judging by the ambient noise level — the gasps, the groans, the one guy in the back quietly saying "I'm dead, I'm actually dead, tell my mother I fought bravely" — the bracket draw had not been kind to the general population.

I approached from the side. Casual. Unhurried. A background character performing background maintenance.

Nobody looked at me for a full three seconds.

Then someone whispered, "That's him."

'Ah. Yes. The folder has gained legs and is walking among us.'

I kept my eyes forward and did the math: tomorrow, the whole first year would know about the spar anyway. Fine. Unavoidable. The trick was to be *boring* about it. Boring people generate no follow-up questions.

I slipped into a gap between two shorter students and started reading over their heads.

They both went very still.

'Sorry. Pretend I'm wallpaper. Please. I'm begging you. I've had a week.'

---

The bracket was standard single-elimination format. Thirty-eight first-years, seeding by entrance assessment, consolation rounds to determine full ranking for the bottom half. Clean design. Efficient. Familiar.

'Okay. Okay okay okay. This I can do.'

Because this — *this* — was a bracket.

Not a dragon. Not a demon contract. Not an Evil Goddess playing 4D chess with my corpse.

A bracket.

I had mapped, optimized, and datamined more brackets in Eschaton Hearts than I had eaten hot meals in my previous life. Three hundred and forty-seven hours of playtime meant I'd run the simulation on this exact evaluation exam something like a dozen times across different routes.

My eyes scanned. My brain compiled.

First: find myself.

Bottom right quadrant. There.

**Lucen Gray (Provisional) vs. Edric Morel.**

'Edric Morel. Edric Morel...'

I pulled the file from memory. Minor baron's son. Mana index 62. No notable skills — not in the census data, not in the route flags, not anywhere. In the game, this kid existed for exactly one purpose: to be an early-round NPC whose defeat made someone else look good.

A pure background character.

'I'm fighting... me. I'm fighting the off-brand version of myself.'

Except he had a mana index of 62 and I had 0.009, which meant in any exchange of actual magic I would lose to a stiff breeze. My path to victory was narrow, ugly, and involved absolutely no magic whatsoever.

Winnable, though. Barely. If everything went right. The same way a coin flip is winnable if the coin lands on its edge first.

I traced upward.

Winner of my match faces the winner of Derrick Holt vs. Fenrick Dunn.

Derrick Holt.

'Ah. The friendly neighborhood siege weapon.'

Fenrick Dunn — Court Rank 31, decent fundamentals — would lose. Derrick was a brute, but he was a *trained* brute, the kind of guy whose family paid for combat tutors the way mine used to pay for electricity. Raw force, decent footwork, aggression dialed to eleven.

So: Round 2, me versus Derrick Holt.

'Perfect. Wonderful. I dodge one monster and get fed to another.'

Except — no. Actually. I ran it twice. This was *fine*.

Derrick wasn't lethal-dangerous in a sanctioned evaluation match. There were healers. There were rules. Losing to Derrick Holt in Round 2 meant a final ranking somewhere in the high 20s to low 30s depending on consolation results, assuming my Round 1 win held weight.

Win one. Lose one with dignity. Emerge with a ranking respectable enough to keep the expulsion paperwork in its drawer.

That was the play. That was the whole play. Win once, lose once, graduate from this event as a footnote.

Beyond Round 2 was academic anyway — the bracket's semifinal slot fed into Lucas Ashford's quarter, and I would be dead or losing long before that mattered.

'Okay. My quarter is solved. Now the important part.'

My eyes flicked across the board to the opposite side.

Lucas Ashford's path.

Round 1: Lucas vs. Court Rank 29. Irrelevant. Ten seconds, tops.

Round 2: Lucas vs. Court Rank 14. Twenty seconds, if Lucas felt like being polite.

Quarterfinal: Lucas Ashford vs. **Rhea Valtor.**

I stopped breathing for a second.

'There it is.'

*There it is.*

That match. I knew that match. I'd *seen* that match — rendered in the game's engine, voice-acted, scored with the kind of soundtrack the devs only rolled out when they knew they were cooking.

The Sparring of Equals. The fight where Lucas Ashford, golden boy protagonist, meets the one person in his year who doesn't fold. Where Rhea Valtor, walking war crime, meets the one person who doesn't bore her.

Mutual respect. Understanding. The first domino of her entire route.

'That match has to happen. Exactly like that. No interference. No butterfly wings. No gray-haired speed bumps anywhere within fifty meters of that arena.'

Because if that match didn't happen — if some idiot derailed the bracket, if injuries shuffled the seeding, if *anything* disrupted it — Rhea's route collapsed at the foundation. And when Rhea's route collapsed...

[Bad End No. 4: The Dragon's Boredom] had a cinematic. I'd watched it. I hadn't slept well for two days.

'So the plan is: I win my one match, I bow out gracefully, I stay three hundred meters away from Lucas's quarter, and I let the canon flow like a river.'

Everything was...

...manageable.

For the first time since I'd woken up in this world, the board in front of me looked *solvable*.

I almost smiled.

```
[Congratulations! You feel hope.]
[The Evil Goddess has marked this moment for later use.]
```

'Don't you dare.'

---

Then I looked at the bottom of the sheet.

There was an addendum. A single paragraph, printed in smaller type, the kind of bureaucratic footnote that most students would skim past the way players skip tutorial text.

I did not skip it. Completing 347 hours of content teaches you to read the fine print, because the fine print is where the devs hide the things that kill you.

*"All full-assessment examinees are additionally reminded: Panel assessment candidates will face additional evaluation criteria as determined by the assessment committee. These criteria may differ from standard match victory conditions."*

I read it again.

'Panel assessment candidates.'

'CANDIDATES. Plural — no, wait.'

I scanned the entire bracket sheet. Names. Seeds. Ranks. Thirty-eight students, divided into standard evaluation matches, and —

One.

There was one panel assessment candidate on the entire sheet.

Guess who.

'Instructor Kaelin. You absolute smiling snake. This is the favor. THIS is the favor, isn't it?'

Because I knew what "panel assessment" meant in this academy's history. Everyone who'd read the lore documents knew. It wasn't a *harsher* evaluation. It was a *different* one. Designed for students the faculty believed were hiding their real level — suppressing power, sandbagging the entrance exams, concealing a hidden class or bloodline.

They didn't just watch you fight.

They tried to *make you reveal*.

A finger poked into my shoulder.

"Whoa!" said a voice directly behind my ear at a volume that could charge batteries. "Luce, you've got one of the little dagger marks by your name! What's the dagger mean?"

Finn.

Finn, who had apparently been reading over my shoulder for an unknown but certainly devastating length of time.

"Finn. Volume. Please."

"Dude, hold on." His eyes tracked to the addendum. He read it. His eyes went wide as saucers. "Wait. Wait wait wait. You're getting the *special assessment*?"

"Finn—"

"That's the thing they do for students they think are *hiding their real level*!" Finn announced, to me, to the board, to the continent. "They only did it once before! They did it for the Crown Prince twelve years ago!"

Silence.

The kind of silence that arrives when forty people simultaneously stop perceiving their own drama and lock onto yours.

Heads turned. Not one at a time. In waves. Like a wheat field hit by wind.

'No. No no no no—'

"THE CROWN PRINCE?!" someone repeated.

"Who's hiding his level—"

"Gray? The provisional crystal-breaker guy?"

"The one who fought Lucas Ashford for four minutes—"

'Instructor Vance's folder just grew a second drawer.'

I opened my mouth to release a denial — calm, measured, forgettable.

Finn got there first.

"Guys, guys, you should've SEEN him just now." Finn gestured at me like a museum docent presenting a crown jewel. "He walked up, looked at the board for like thirty seconds, and just... nodded. He mapped the *entire bracket* in thirty seconds. He already knows who's winning every match."

'That is — okay, technically that's true, but NOT in the way you're—'

"He was doing math earlier," Finn added helpfully. "With his fingers. I saw fractions."

'THERE WERE NO FRACTIONS. THERE WERE NEVER FRACTIONS.'

The crowd's murmur pitch-shifted upward. I watched the rumor assemble itself in real time, the way you watch a potion you brewed slightly wrong begin to change color.

*He mapped the bracket instantly. He knows every matchup. The Crown Prince assessment. The crystal. The spar.* The students weren't even whispering anymore. They were just... staring. At a gray-haired stick of a boy with a mana index lower than furniture, as if he were an unsolved equation.

```
[Attention captured: ELEVATED.]
[The 'invisible background character' strategy has now officially generated less invisibility than being on fire.]
[The Evil Goddess would like it noted that she said this would happen. Specifically on Friday. In writing.]
```

'Noted. Etched into my soul. Thank you.'

I did the only thing available.

I took one step backward.

Then another.

"Well," I said, voice flat, addressing the room's assembled curiosity with the full charisma of a lampshade, "I hope everyone wins their matches."

Then I left.

Not running. Running created stories. Walking *slightly too fast* merely created questions, and questions were cheaper than stories.

Behind me, I heard Finn say, "See? He doesn't even need to say anything. That's how you know he already knows how it ends."

'I do know how it ends, Finn. That's the entire problem. It ends with me in a folder.'

---

My dormitory room. Door locked. Chair pushed against it, because the chair wouldn't stop anything and did it anyway. Ritual was ritual.

I sat on the bed and laid out the situation the way I'd lay out a run. Clean. Cold. Itemized.

**Objective One:** Defeat Edric Morel. Mana 62, no skills, minor noble, heavily reliant on basic casting patterns. My counter: physical pressure, close distance before the first chant finishes, make it ugly. The kind of fight that looks like a mugging, not a duel. Winnable. Seventy percent, maybe, if the shoulder held.

**Objective Two:** Lose to Derrick Holt. Safely. Convincingly. Injure nothing that can't be un-injured. A controlled loss to a mid-tier opponent keeps the ranking healthy and keeps the narrative boring.

**Objective Three:** Survive the panel assessment.

I stared at the wall.

'Objectives Two and Three are the same event.'

The committee would be watching my Derrick Holt match with eyes designed to catch concealed power. Watching for the suppression artist. Waiting for the moment the "hiding archmage" slipped, or — worse — the moment he *judged* the threat worthy and unveiled a fraction of his true strength.

Meaning: if I lost to Derrick the way my skill level actually dictated — quickly, stupidly, face-first — the panel wouldn't read it as a loss.

They'd read it as *acting*.

'He's throwing the match.' 'He's refusing to reveal himself.' 'Interesting. Raise the pressure. Find his limit.'

And there was no limit waiting underneath. There was nothing to reveal. I had the combat capacity of an especially motivated broom — a broom that knew the theory of sweeping at a deep and philosophical level, but a broom nonetheless.

No matter what I did, the panel would find an interpretation that grew the legend.

Lose fast? *Consummate deception.*

Lose slow? *Testing his opponent.*

Win? *AH. THE MASK SLIPS.*

'It's a rigged three-card monte game and every card says "you looked suspicious today."'

"System," I said out loud. "Status window. Full readout."

The air shimmered. Obligingly, ominously.

```
[Lucen Gray — Provisional Student, Stellaris Royal Academy]
[Mana Index: 0.009 / 100,000]
[Note: The enchanted training dummies (0.01) have filed a formal complaint about being compared to you. They feel it damages their reputation.]
[Evaluation Exam: T-minus 48 hours]
[Current combat power ranking among first-years: 38th of 38]
[Required rank to avoid expulsion review: 35th or higher]
[Required performance to satisfy panel assessment: 'Demonstrate behavior consistent with controlled suppression of significant concealed power.']
[These two objectives are mutually contradictory.]
```

"Yeah," I said. "I know."

```
[Additional note: If expelled from Stellaris Royal Academy, your location, wellbeing, and continued skeletal integrity will fall outside the academy's protective wards.]
```

My hand paused midway through the motion of rubbing my face.

"...And inside whose, exactly?"

The System took its time. The window flickered. And then, in a font I had learned to associate with the particular flavor of dread reserved for things the game only showed you *once*:

```
[Recall: Iris von Solachter currently believes you are 'interesting.']
[Recall: The Empire's Imperial Princess does not permit interesting things to leave her reach.]
[In the game data, no player has ever been expelled while holding her attention. Not because the path doesn't exist.]
[Because no player has survived long enough on that path to register it.]
[The devs left one line of code in that event. One line. It reads: 'Retention protocol engaged.']
[What the retention protocol IS:
— Unknown.
[What the Evil Goddess thinks it is:
— She has declined to say. She giggled. For quite a while.]
```

The room felt colder.

```
[NEW BAD END UNLOCKED FOR MONITORING]
[Bad End No. 12: The Tyrant's Curiosity]
[Trigger condition: Expulsion while flagged under Imperial interest.]
[Description: You are removed from the game's board — and claimed as a piece on someone else's. There is no route back. There is no route at all. There is only her, forever, and the word 'interesting.']
[Current risk: RISING.]
[Princess's last known remark about you, per court gossip channels: "I hope they fail him. I'd so like to meet him properly."]
```

I sat very still.

'She's *cheering for my expulsion.*'

'The scariest person on the continent has my academic failure on her vision board.'

Then, a final window. Smaller. Almost gentle, in the way a knife laid flat was gentle:

```
[The Evil Goddess has begun taking bets on your cause of death.]
[Current favorite: 'Death by Confused Academics' — odds 3:1.]
[Second place: 'Iris Simply Eats Him' — odds 5:2. Note: 'eats' may be metaphorical. The Evil Goddess refuses to specify, citing 'entertainment value.']
[She has also offered you one piece of genuine advice, which she stresses is NOT encouragement:]
['Win the first one, little anomaly. Lose the second one beautifully. And on the third — improvise.']
[['It's what you do, isn't it?']]
```

I read the last line three times.

Then I lay back on the bed, stared at the ceiling, and laughed. Quietly. The slightly unwell laugh of an unemployed speedrunner who had just discovered that the final exam of his life was a three-phase raid with contradictory objectives, a hidden boss, and a spectator section full of princesses.

"Forty-eight hours," I told the ceiling. "Win once. Lose once. Improvise once."

'And don't become a toy.'

'And don't let anyone else become a widower of a route.'

Lucas and Rhea's quarterfinal. Derrick's heavy hands. Edric's boring, winnable nobility. The panel's patient, academic knives.

One bracket. Four problems. Zero mana.

I pulled the bracket sheet — I'd torn the corner off the public posting without thinking, old min-maxer reflex, you always pocket the loot table — and pinned it to the wall above my desk.

Then, beside my name, I wrote in small letters a note from the only coach I'd ever had.

*She said: you don't need to be strong. You need to be un-hittable for long enough that the other guy makes a mistake.*

And below that, my own addition.

*Make the mistake expensive. Make my failure look like a decision. Make my defeat look like a plan.*

I blew out the candle.

In the dark, through the window, the academy grounds glittered — two nights from judgment, one folder deeper, and somehow, against everything, still on the board.

'Restraint,' I reminded myself, as sleep crept up. 'I have restraint.'

Somewhere above the sky, the Evil Goddess laughed until she cried.

The bet, I would later learn, had hit record volume.
