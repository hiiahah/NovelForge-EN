# Chapter 19: First Evaluation Exam: Round 1

Sleep, it turned out, was a DLC I hadn't purchased.

I opened my eyes to a ceiling that looked exactly like the ceiling of a man about to be publicly executed by a minor noble.

Because it was.

'Breathe. Great. Riveting content. Ten out of ten divine intervention.'

I sat up. My ribs filed a formal complaint. My nose sent a strongly worded letter from the trauma department.

Both were overruled by management.

Management was terrified.

---

The Grand Arena of Stellaris Royal Academy did not do anything by halves.

Because Stellaris didn't believe in halves. Stellaris believed in *excess, but make it architecture.*

Floating stone tiers spiraled up into enchanted sky. Barrier arrays shimmered around the central ring like heat haze — translucent walls of woven mana that could, according to the game lore, contain a rampaging wyvern.

They had never, in recorded history, needed to contain a rampaging wyvern.

Today they would be containing my career ending in real time.

Thirty-eight classmates filled the staging benches. Faculty lined the judge's platform like a tribunal. Three senior assessment officers sat in the elevated box with their clipboards and their cold, credential-stamping eyes.

And above them all, the gallery.

*Don't look up. Don't look up. Don't look—*

I looked up.

Three faces.

Princess Iris Aurelia, seated like the concept of monarchy had invented chairs specifically for her. Golden hair. Perfect posture. Eyes that had already read the day's events twice.

Rhea Valtor, leaning forward on her elbows, watching me with the focused stillness of a woman mentally correcting my stance from eighty meters away.

And Lucas Ashford.

The protagonist. Smiling. Actually *smiling* at me, with that warm, sparkling, main-character energy, like we were old friends about to share a grand adventure.

We were not friends.

He was a natural disaster with good cheekbones, and I was a piece of scenery that had accidentally developed a heartbeat.

I looked back down at my practice sword.

Blunted steel. Cheap grip. The sword equivalent of a participation trophy.

'Okay. Kang Min-jun. Twenty-three. Unemployed. Three hundred forty-seven hours. You have died to worse things than a rich teenager.'

A pause.

'Actually, no. No, I haven't. The worst things I died to were rich teenagers with better armor. This is literally the genre.'

---

"Lucen Gray!"

Instructor Kaelin's voice cracked across the arena like a whip with a teaching certificate.

"To the ring."

I walked.

Every step echoed. The arena had that specific acoustic design where your footsteps sounded important and your survival odds sounded fictional.

I took my mark on the white circle.

Across the ring, the gate opened, and my executioner walked in wearing a smile his father had purchased.

Edric Morel.

Soft-faced. Soft-handed. Armor polished to a mirror sheen that had definitely never been touched by dirt, sweat, or consequences. His practice sword was master-work — balanced, rune-etched along the fuller, the kind of blade that said *my family owns ships* without the vulgarity of saying it out loud.

He looked at me.

He looked through me.

Then he did the smile. The sneer. The little aristocratic nose-lift that translated, in every language ever spoken, to: *I paid more for my boots than your bloodline is worth.*

'Wonderful. Noble Knight — Type C. Same model as the tutorial-tier trash mob from the Berengar arc. Same overhead habits. Same mana-boosted charge. Same face, somehow.'

Three hundred forty-seven hours.

I had killed this exact man, mathematically speaking, several hundred times.

Just never with my *own* body.

Instructor Vance raised his hand from the judge's platform. His voice rolled out, magically amplified, flat and procedural.

"Round One. Lucen Gray, provisional. Edric Morel, House Morel. Combat concludes at disarm, submission, ring-out, or incapacitation. Assessment panel will be scoring form, mana efficiency, and tactical judgment."

A pause.

"Begin."

---

Edric charged immediately.

No testing. No probing. No respect for the scientific method.

Just mana flooding his legs — actual, visible, shimmering mana, the cheat code I had been born without — and suddenly sixty kilograms of polished aristocracy was crossing ten meters like an expensive cannonball.

The overhead came down at my skull.

I was already not there.

Ashenveil Retreat.

Back-pedal, half-beat early. The blade hissed past my nose close enough to rearrange my future grandchildren.

Edric's eyes widened — fractionally — and then he reset and swung again. Horizontal. Rib height.

I ducked under it. The wind of the swing parted my hair into a style I'd call *recently deceased*.

'Mana-boosted speed, telegraphed like a holiday parade. Classic Type C. All throttle, no steering.'

The crowd murmured.

I heard it. Even through the blood hammering in my ears, I heard it — that low, confused ripple of three hundred students realizing the nobody wasn't dying on schedule.

Edric pressed. Slash. Slash. Thrust. The mana-boost made him fast. Genuinely fast. Faster than any human had a right to be.

And I moved.

Step. Angle. Shave the distance. Never block — *blocking with 0.009 mana was how arms become archaeology* — just get out of the shape of the attack by the width of a sneeze, and be somewhere else when the sword arrived at the place I used to be.

It wasn't skill.

Let's be honest with each other, dear reader. It was not skill.

It was pattern recognition drilled into my skull across three hundred forty-seven hours of being murdered by this exact animation set, executed by a body running on pure, uncut, weapon-grade terror.

Terror is a heck of a personal trainer.

---

One minute.

Edric's face was changing. The sneer was starting to crack at the edges, like cheap paint in the rain.

"Hold still!" he snapped.

'No.'

I backpedaled through his follow-up, circled off the center line, and let him carve up a generous portion of empty air.

Two minutes.

The arena had gone weirdly quiet — and then, slowly, un-quiet. The murmur was building. Students leaning forward on the benches. I caught a flash of movement on the judge's platform: Instructor Vance, scribbling. Kaelin, arms crossed, eyebrows climbing her forehead like they were attempting a summit.

In the gallery, Rhea was carved from stone.

Iris hadn't blinked.

I knew this without looking, the way you know where the sun is without staring at it.

Three minutes.

Edric was breathing hard now. Mana boosting burned fuel, and this boy had a big engine and a tiny tank. Sweat streaked the polished face. The swings were getting wider. Bigger. Angrier.

And angrier swings have a very specific mathematical property.

They get slower.

"STAND—" he sucked air, "—STILL!"

He lunged. I slipped left. His blade ate stone-dust. The crowd made a sound — an actual sound, an *ooooh* — and it was at that precise moment I understood I had made a catastrophic error.

Not the dodging.

The *audience.*

'You idiot. You complete, monumental idiot. This isn't dodging anymore. This is a *performance.* Three hundred people are watching a Level 1 extra humiliate a sponsored noble for four straight minutes. This isn't surviving. This is making CONTENT.'

In Eschaton Hearts, there was a saying among completionists.

*Never let the NPCs see the frame-perfect run.*

Because NPCs don't see frames. They see *intent.*

And intent, to an audience, looked like this: a boy who was never in danger. A boy who could have ended it at any second. A boy choosing not to.

A boy *toying* with his prey.

'No no no no. I'm not toying! I'm TERMINALLY AFRAID. There's a difference! It's a very important legal distinction!'

Nobody could hear my internal screaming.

From the outside, I must have looked like a glacier with a pulse.

---

Four minutes.

Edric Morel hit his limit.

I could see it the way a speedrunner sees a boss's desperation phase — the tells, the posture, the final all-in. His jaw clenched. Mana flared across his whole body, last of the tank, everything on one number.

He leapt.

Full overhead. Maximum commitment. Both hands. Total weight. Zero defense. The kind of attack that either ends the fight or ends the attacker, and nobles were never taught the second outcome existed.

The blade screamed down.

*Now,* said Rhea's voice in my memory. *Step, shave, borrow, break wrist tendon geometry. One shot. One surprise.*

My body began the counter-disarm.

Step one: rotate off the center line—

My heel caught a groove in the arena stone.

Ah.

'Ah.'

Time did the thing. The slow thing. The thing where the universe leans over your shoulder to watch you die.

I tripped.

Full, graceless, magnificent trip. Both feet leaving their assigned positions, my entire body pitching forward like a felled coat rack, the famous counter-disarm collapsing into a sprawling lunge face-first toward the stone.

My arms windmilled.

The practice sword — that cheap, blunt, participation-trophy sword — swung in a wild, flailing arc dictated entirely by panic and gravity.

And physics, that smug, punctual bastard, cashed the check.

The flat of my blade caught Edric's descending wrist.

At the exact angle.

At the exact rotation.

At the exact fraction of a second where his grip was fully committed and his tendons were loaded — the *exact* geometry Rhea had drilled into my palm the night before, achieved not through mastery but by falling down at a lucky timestamp.

*CRACK-ting.*

Edric's sword tore out of his hands like it had remembered an appointment elsewhere.

It spun end over end through the air — a beautiful, glittering arc of expensive noble steel — and clattered across the arena floor, ringing, ringing, coming to rest twenty meters away.

I hit the ground face-first.

Stone. Gravel. The intimate, full-contact texture of the Grand Arena floor.

The world rang like a bell someone had dropped.

---

Silence.

Total, cathedral silence.

I lay there. Cheek on the stone. One eye open. My nose was doing a slow, warm faucet thing that strongly suggested I had re-injured the re-injury.

Across the ring, Edric Morel stood frozen mid-swing, both hands still wrapped around a sword that no longer existed, staring at his empty fingers like they'd betrayed the family name.

Then, from the judge's platform, Instructor Kaelin's voice.

Calm. Final. Echoing off every stone in the building.

"Disarm. Match to Lucen Gray."

The arena *detonated.*

I have heard crowds in my life. Concert crowds. Sports crowds. A Seoul bus terminal at holiday rush. Nothing — *nothing* — compared to three hundred academy students who had just watched the known order of the universe get tripped over.

"WHAT—"

"Did you see—he didn't even LOOK—"

"Four minutes! He dodged for FOUR MINUTES and ended it in ONE MOVE—"

And then, cutting through all of it, a voice I knew. A voice I regretted knowing. A voice with the acoustic power of a foghorn that had discovered friendship.

Finn.

Standing on the bench, fists in the air, screaming himself hoarse:

"HE WAITED FOUR MINUTES FOR THAT ONE MOVE! FOUR MINUTES! HE LET HIM SWING FOR FOUR MINUTES! DID YOU SEE IT?! HE WAS READING HIM THE WHOLE TIME! HE'S A MONSTER! A BEAUTIFUL, TERRIFYING MONSTER!"

'Finn. Finn, please. Finn, I fell down. I FELL DOWN, FINN.'

But the crowd had the story now.

And the story was so much better than the truth that the truth never stood a chance.

I pushed myself up onto my elbows. Blood dripped from my nose onto the arena stone — a small, dignified puddle — and I became aware, with the slow dread of a man hearing the boss music change, that the arena had gone quiet in a *different* way.

The panel was conferring.

The three senior assessors over their clipboards, heads together, glancing at me, glancing down. Writing. *Underlining.* I could feel the underlines from here.

Instructor Vance said something.

The eldest assessor nodded and wrote something else.

I did not need to read lips.

I had played this game. I knew what bureaucratic enthusiasm looked like. I had watched it ruin lives in seven different routes.

---

And then I made my second mistake of the day.

I looked at the gallery.

Rhea Valtor was on her feet.

Not cheering. Not celebrating. *Frowning* — a deep, precise, professional frown, the frown of a master craftsman examining a forgery. Her eyes flicked from my feet, to my hands, to the fallen sword, and I could watch the calculation happening behind them in real time.

*The footwork was wrong. He tripped. That wasn't my form. But the angle— the angle was perfect. That's not possible. How—*

'Sword mom has question marks. This is bad. Sword mom cannot be allowed to have question marks. Question marks become conversations. Conversations become mornings. Mornings become violence with education.'

Lucas Ashford was standing too.

Smiling.

That warm, radiant, protagonist smile — but focused now, sharpened, aimed at me like a spotlight with ambitions. He said something to the student beside him, and even from here I could lip-read the shape of it, because protagonists enunciate.

*"He's even better than I thought."*

'No. No I'm not. I'm significantly worse than you thought. I'm so much worse that it's actually a kind of achievement. I demand a recount of your expectations—'

And then there was Iris.

The princess had not stood.

She sat, perfectly still, and as I watched, she closed her eyes.

Held them closed for one long beat.

Opened them.

And smiled.

Not the political smile. Not the porcelain smile she wore for the court and the crowds and the machinery of empire. A small smile. A real one. The smile of a collector who has just watched a locked display case quietly open itself from the inside.

Her lips moved.

"Fascinating."

I didn't hear it. The arena was too loud.

I didn't need to hear it.

Some words arrive by mail regardless.

I got to my feet. Swayed. Steadied. Edric Morel was still standing in the middle of the ring, staring at nothing, while two classmates gently collected him like a piece of misdelivered furniture.

He looked at me as I passed.

'Approximately until I died of natural causes, Edric.'

"…As long as it took," I said, because my social interface had crashed and this was the error message it printed.

Something in his eyes broke.

Something in everyone else's eyes *lit up.*

I walked out of the ring on legs made of borrowed resolve, and the System bloomed one final window, large and gentle and horrible, like a funeral wreath with my name spelled correctly.

I stood at the edge of the arena, blood drying on my lip, staring at that last line.

'She's frightened.'

The goddess who found bad endings funny.

The goddess who watched me die in this world like it was a rerun she owned on disc.

*She was frightened.*

From the gallery, I could feel three gazes resting on my back — warm, sharp, and golden respectively.

Round Two was coming.

The bracket sheet had my name on it, against Derrick Holt's heavy hands, in a match I was explicitly planning to lose.

I had planned to lose it beautifully.

Gracefully. Forgettably.

And now three hundred people, one protagonist, one sword master, and one princess believed I was a patient professional killer who ended fights with a single perfect strike.

'Lose beautifully. How exactly am I supposed to lose beautifully NOW?'

Somewhere above the sky, the Evil Goddess pulled out a fresh bracket of her own, and wrote my name in the center of it, and laughed until the stars flickered.

The Grand Arena cheered.

The gravel in my mouth did not.
