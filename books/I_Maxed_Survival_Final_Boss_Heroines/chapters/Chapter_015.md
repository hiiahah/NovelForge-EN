# Chapter 15: The Protagonist's Approach

Saturday morning arrived like a pardon from the governor.

No classes. No evaluation prep lectures. No Professor Helmand's terrifying pop quizzes about noble genealogies I had absolutely memorized the night before with the desperation of a condemned man.

Just me, my closet of a dormitory room, and twelve uninterrupted hours of Ashenveil Retreat footwork practice.

'Three days until the evaluation.'

Three days to turn a half-learned evacuation technique into something that might keep me from getting hospitalized by first-years with actual combat training.

Doable. Barely. Like parallel parking a battleship.

I rolled out of bed, did my warm-up stretches, and began the footwork sequence in the two square meters of floor space my room generously provided.

Step left. Shift weight. Pivot on the back heel. Fade —

*Knock knock.*

I froze mid-pivot like a mannequin with a criminal record.

'Who?'

Nobody visited me. I had engineered my entire social presence at this academy to be the human equivalent of beige wallpaper. Even the cleaning staff forgot I existed, which was genuinely impressive considering they cleaned my room.

*Knock knock knock.*

Cheerful knocking. Persistent. The knock of someone who had never once considered that the person inside might not want visitors.

I opened the door.

Sunlight poured in, and standing in it, glowing with the kind of protagonist-tier radiance that probably showed up on satellite imagery, was Lucas Ashford.

Golden hair. Earnest blue eyes. A smile that could've ended small regional conflicts.

He looked like a golden retriever who'd just discovered where I buried the tennis balls.

"Hey," he said. "You're Lucen, right? Want to grab lunch?"

'...'

My brain did the thing computers do when you open forty tabs at once.

*Loading. Loading. Loading. Critical error.*

"Uh," I said, with the eloquence of the true intellectual. "Now?"

"Yeah! It's lunchtime." He said this with the innocent confidence of someone for whom lunch was simply a meal and not a tactical encounter. "I noticed you eat alone a lot. Nobody should eat alone on a Saturday."

'I can't eat alone on a Saturday. I can't eat at ALL. This is a disaster.'

My mental tactical board lit up like a war room.

Fact one: Lucas Ashford was the protagonist. Protagonists are event magnets. Standing next to one statistically increases your chances of dragon attacks, assassin encounters, and emotionally devastating plot twists by four hundred percent.

Fact two: I had a complete, tested, airtight strategy for surviving this world, and every step of it involved being as far from the plot as physically possible.

Fact three: refusing the protagonist's first friendly overture, flatly and coldly, in a hallway, was the single most protagonist-attracting behavior known to narrative physics. Cold mysterious rejections were catnip. I'd played this game. I'd SEEN the flags. "There's something about that standoffish boy..." No. Absolutely not. I refuse to be the standoffish boy.

Fact four: there was, unfortunately, a much worse option hiding behind fact three.

Because in the original game, Lucas Ashford's early social bonds weren't flavor text. They were load-bearing. The friends he made in his first month determined his emotional resilience stat for every crisis in Volumes 2 through 5. A well-friended Lucas was a stable Lucas. A rejected, ostracized Lucas got a hidden debuff called *[Isolation]* that contributed to exactly four of the game's seventeen bad endings.

'If I cold-shoulder him and he develops wrong, the continent burns.'

'If I get close to him, *I* burn.'

'There is no winning move. There is only choosing which fire to stand in.'

The whole assessment took about two seconds. Lucas was still standing there, smiling patiently, head tilted slightly, like a puppy waiting for a human to finish being indecisive about the ball.

"Sure," I said, and died a little inside. "Lunch sounds great."

"Awesome!" His grin widened by a measurable fifteen percent. "I'll grab Cedric and Mariel. Meet you in the courtyard in five?"

"Five," I confirmed, and closed the door.

Then I leaned my forehead against it and stood there in silence.

The Academy dining hall on Saturdays was a different animal from the weekday chaos.

Half-empty. Sun-drenched. The long tables scattered with students too lazy or too wealthy to go into town, plus a handful of scholarship kids eating the free Saturday stew with the focused gratitude of people who'd budgeted for exactly zero restaurant meals.

I was one of those kids, technically. My cover story was 'impoverished northern fishing village orphan on provisional scholarship,' and I'd dressed the part so thoroughly that even I felt a little poor looking at myself.

Lucas waved from a table near the window like he'd reserved us headliner spots at a concert.

"Lucen! Over here!"

'Please stop saying my name with so much joy. Shouting a background extra's name across a crowded room is how background extras get promoted into scenes.'

I crossed the hall with the measured, unremarkable gait of a man trying very hard to be forgettable while walking toward the most memorable person in the building.

Two other students sat at the table. I recognized them instantly, because of course I did. I'd watched their portrait sprites deliver four hundred thousand words of dialogue.

Cedric Valdemar. Third son of Duke Valdemar. Broad shoulders, sandy hair, a face structured entirely around the concept of sincere confusion. In the game: comic relief, loyal muscle, the guy who misunderstood metaphors. Liked: sparring, meat pies, believing in his friends. Disliked: reading, subtext.

Mariel Fenton. Viscountess's daughter. Dark hair pinned with ruthless efficiency, gray eyes that missed nothing, permanently carrying a book the way other people carried emotional baggage. In the game: the strategist of the friend group, the dry wit, the one who noticed things.

'The starter squad. The tutorial party. Be polite. Be boring. Be the human equivalent of a loading screen.'

"Guys, this is Lucen," Lucas announced. "He's in the provisional program. Lucen — Cedric, Mariel."

"Hey!" Cedric stuck out a hand the size of a dinner plate and shook mine with enough force to realign my spine. "Any friend of Lucas is a friend of — wait, actually, how do you two know each other? I thought we were his only friends. Did we get replaced? Mariel, did we get replaced?"

"You don't get replaced after two weeks, Cedric," Mariel said, without looking up from her book. Then she did look up, and her eyes did a quick top-to-bottom scan of me that I felt in my soul. "Provisional program, northern accent, secondhand uniform very carefully maintained. Scholarship?"

"...Yes," I said.

"Good for you," she said, and went back to her book. In Mariel-speak, this was roughly a standing ovation.

"Ignore her, she hates everyone until she's read the same room as them three times," Cedric said, shoving a tray at me. "Stew's good today. Get the stew. Get two."

I got the stew. I sat down. I ate the stew.

And for one horrible, irresponsible moment, it was... nice.

'No. NO. None of that. Nostalgia for lunch is how people die in Volume 2.'

---

"So, Lucen," Lucas said, leaning forward with that terrifying earnest interest protagonists deploy like siege weapons, "where are you from? Lucas mentioned— I mean, *I'd* like to know."

'Here it comes. Deploy cover story. Operation Begorrah.'

I set down my spoon and performed the lie I'd rehearsed in front of my mirror until my own face looked suspicious of it.

"A fishing village," I said. "Up in Fenmore. Way north. Small place — two hundred people, maybe. My father was a fisherman. My mother died when I was young, and he... passed last year. Storm." I lowered my eyes a calibrated amount. "The village put together what they could for the application fee. I'm the first person from Fenmore to even test for the Academy."

The details were perfect. I'd spent ninety hours in this game's codex and there genuinely was a Fenmore, population listed as 214, fishing economy, and — crucially — located on a stretch of coast devastated by a storm two in-game years ago. Any investigator would find a village full of people who'd lost somebody. Nobody would be able to confirm or deny one quiet orphan boy.

'Flawless. Ten out of ten. The perfect crime.'

Cedric had stopped chewing. Even Mariel's book had dipped an inch.

"That's..." Cedric started, and visibly failed to finish.

Lucas was looking at me with an expression I knew from the game files. It was the face he made during the heavy story beats. The 'the protagonist is about to say something that recontextualizes everything' face.

"I'm glad you're here," he said.

Simple. No drama. No speech. Just four words, dropped on the table like a hand on a shoulder.

'Oh no.'

Guilt hit me like a knife between the ribs.

Because I knew what Lucas Ashford had been through. I knew about the journey to the Academy. The robbery on the road. The servant boy who'd traveled with him, who'd died protecting him in the game's tutorial backstory.

A boy whose death Lucas had never stopped carrying. The weight underneath every 'I'm glad you're here.' The reason his friendship stat had an asterisk next to it in the developer notes.

And I knew — because I was sitting here, alive, in a body I'd woken up in years ago on the side of that very road — that in this world's version of events, things had gone differently.

Lucas was mourning something I couldn't explain. Something connected to me.

'He's being kind to a person who don't exist, to fill a hole left by a boy who also doesn't exist.'

'I am a ghost eating free stew next to my own unfinished business.'

"...Thanks," I managed, and the word came out rougher than intended, because some traitorous part of my throat had decided to mean it.

Mariel's eyes flicked up over her book. Filed something away. Said nothing. Terrifying woman.

"Anyway!" Lucas said, either not noticing my internal collapse or graciously bulldozing past it, which — knowing him — was a deliberate mercy. "Mariel, tell Lucen what you found out about the evaluation."

"Me? You found it out. I just read the notice board."

"Tomayto, tomahto."

"The written evaluation is Tuesday, combat assessment Thursday," Mariel recited. "Combat brackets are randomly seeded. First-years against first-years. Standard elimination format, non-lethal enchantments on the arena, healing staff on standby."

"Weapons?"

"Blunted steel or practice wood, your choice."

Cedric grinned. "I'm gonna get thrown out in the first round. I've accepted it. I've made peace. Mariel's had me doing conditioning runs at dawn and I still swing like I'm trying to chop down a tree with a frying pan."

"You do," Mariel confirmed. "It has a certain honesty to it."

Then Lucas turned back to me, and the celebratory atmosphere took on the freighted weight of an arriving plot point.

"So," he said. "The combat evaluation. How's your prep going?"

'Careful. CAREFUL.'

"It's... going," I said. "I've got my own training routine I work on. Solo stuff. Footwork, mostly."

"Footwork!" Lucas nodded enthusiastically, like I'd announced I was a fellow enthusiast at a convention. "That's smart, honestly. Most people overinvest in offense. Cedric."

"It *is* honest—" Cedric protested.

"Hey — I've been training hard for the exam," Lucas continued, and there it was, the train entering the station exactly on the canonical schedule. "Want to spar sometime? As practice. Before Thursday."

'No.'

'No no no no no.'

"I appreciate it," I said, keeping my voice level, "but I really do better with my solo routine. I don't want to waste your time — I'm probably not much of a match."

Every word was true and every word was a mistake, because Lucas Ashford did not hear 'I'm declining.'

Lucas Ashford heard: *a boy who doesn't believe he's worth anyone's time, gently pushing away kindness because he expects to be a burden.*

I could watch it happening in his eyes in real time. The shift. The softening. The protagonist instinct engaging like a pit bull's jaw.

'No. Stop perceiving my emotional damage. I don't HAVE emotional damage. I have SURVIVAL PROTOCOLS.'

"The brackets are random," Lucas said, gently, reasonably, like a man laying a perfect trap made of pure logic. "We might face each other on Thursday."

'...'

'Oh no. He's right.'

"It'd be good to know each other's styles," he went on. "Right? Better to find each other's rhythm now than at zero notice on the arena floor."

And there it was. The checkmate. The unavoidable truth wearing the friendly face of a lunch invitation.

Because my brain — my treacherous, completionist, speedrunner brain — had already started running the calculation, and the calculation was horrifying:

If I drew Lucas in the brackets, I was dead. Not 'lose the match' dead. Lucen Gray, Level 1, versus Lucas Ashford, Level 15-equivalent protagonist with a holy sword aptitude, meant a single missed dodge could turn 'evaluation' into 'evaluation-shaped funeral.' The Ashenveil Retreat was a survival technique, not a miracle.

I knew Lucas's attack patterns from the game. *In theory.* From controlling him across six playthroughs. From watching his animations ten thousand times.

But a moveset memorized from behind a screen and a moveset coming at your actual face were two different subjects taught at two different universities.

One sparring session would let me map his real-world speed. His reach. His tells. The gap between 'game knowledge' and 'body knowledge.'

One sparring session against the protagonist was also exactly the kind of flag-shaped activity that turned background extras into recurring characters.

'Potential death on Thursday versus narrative contamination today.'

'Survival rule number one: the death in front of you outranks the death down the road.'

"...One session," I heard myself say. "Tomorrow morning. Just one."

Lucas's whole face did the thing. The sunrise thing. The thing that had made six heroines across four story volumes make catastrophically romantic decisions.

"Tomorrow morning! East training yard, first bell. Mariel, you have to come referee, Cedric too—"

"I refuse to get up before the sun for someone ELSE'S sparring," said Mariel, in the tone of someone who would absolutely be there.

"Meat pie's on me if he lands one hit," Cedric offered cheerfully. "No offense, Lucen. I don't know anything about you. He just hits REALLY hard."

"None taken," I said faintly. "I've seen him."

Everyone at the table glanced at me.

"...Practice," I amended smoothly. "I've seen him practice. Around. The yard. He trains a lot. Publicly. Where anyone could see. Which I did. Casually. In passing."

'Nobody say anything. Nobody say anything—'

"Huh," said Mariel, and looked at me for a long moment over her book.

'One was too many.'

We parted in the courtyard after lunch, Lucas extracting promises of punctuality the way dragons extract tributes, Cedric clapping my spine into a new configuration, Mariel departing with a nod that was either approval or a death sentence, pending further data.

I was two corridors from the blissful anonymity of my dorm when a shadow detached itself from a pillar.

"Psst. Oi. Lucen."

Finn. My fellow provisional-program survivor — wiry, sharp-faced, the academy's leading distributor of information nobody asked for and everybody needed.

"You were eating with them," he hissed, grabbing my elbow with the gravity of a battlefield messenger. "In the window seats. I saw. The whole hall saw."

"We had stew," I said. "People eat stew, Finn, it's a documented—"

"Do you know who that WAS?" His voice dropped to a cathedral whisper. "That's Lucas Ashford. THE Lucas Ashford. Highest entrance score in a decade. A DECADE, Lucen. They had to recalibrate the written exam curve because of him. The professors are already talking about him for the Knight Commander track — first-years don't GET talked about for the Knight Commander track —"

"I know who he is," I said quietly.

Finn kept talking. Something about instructors placing bets. Something about a rumored noble house connection.

I heard almost none of it.

Because I knew exactly who Lucas Ashford was. Every stat. Every ending. Every grief. Every way the story used him and every way the story broke him.

More than you'll ever understand, Finn.

More than I'm allowed to explain.

And as I stood there in the corridor, the System unfolded a message across my vision with the gentle inevitability of a coffin lid.

I stood in the emptying corridor and stared at nothing.

I hadn't considered it. Not once, in all my careful little plans.

I'd thought surviving was a subtraction. Just one fewer tragedy on the world's ledger. Neat. Tidy. Victimless.

But stories don't do subtraction. They do *redistribution*.

The oath was still in him. All that grief and resolve and terrible radiant kindness — it hadn't vanished. It had gone looking for somewhere to land.

And today, at a sunlit lunch table, it had landed on a boy who didn't want it, sitting next to a meat pie, saying *I'm glad you're here*.

'What oath is Lucas swearing instead?'

I looked down at my hands. Steady. Level 1. Capable of exactly one advanced technique, which was running away.

"Finn," I said, cutting him off mid-sentence, "if I get drawn against him in the brackets on Thursday — be honest. How long do I last?"

Finn considered this with the gravity it deserved.

"Thirty seconds," he said. "Forty if you run."

'Forty if I run.'

'Good thing running is my entire build.'

I looked out the window, toward the east training yard, where tomorrow morning the protagonist of the world would draw a practice sword and smile at me like a friend.

"Then I'd better make the forty count," I said.
