# Chapter 37: The Calamity: Alice's Night

Running is a skill check.

That's what nobody tells you about life-or-death sprints. Everyone imagines the dramatic part — the pumping legs, the heroic expression, the wind in your hair. Nobody mentions the part where your lungs start filing formal complaints. Nobody mentions that a body with a mana index of 0.009 has the cardiovascular output of an asthmatic houseplant.

Four days of midnight training. One three-second duel that technically counted as the only exercise my doctor would approve of. And now, a five-hundred-meter sprint across campus at full sprint.

My legs were screaming.

My lungs were screaming.

My left foot was screaming in a slightly different dialect, which was concerning.

'Shut up, everyone. We're on a timer.'

[Time remaining: 17:42]

'THANK YOU. VERY HELPFUL. GREAT UI.'

The dormitory wing rose ahead of me, and for a second, my brain did something strange.

It stopped.

Because the building was wrong.

The girls' dormitory at Stellaris Royal Academy was a beautiful structure — white stone, arched windows, ivy climbing the walls in lazy green spirals. I'd seen it every day for weeks from across the courtyard. It looked like something from a postcard. Warm. Safe. Boring.

The thing in front of me now looked like the final dungeon of a winter-themed raid.

Every window was white.

Not lit white. Frosted white. Frost crawled across the glass from the inside, crystalline and deliberate, like the building was slowly being digested by something cold. Ice sheeted down the outer walls in fractal patterns — not random, not natural. *Geometric.*

I'd seen that geometry before.

In the library. In the hidden archive. In the Abyssal circles carved into stone older than the academy itself.

'It's not just freezing the building,' I realized, breath scraping in and out. 'It's *annotating* it.'

The temperature hit me ten meters from the door.

One breath, warm night air.

Next breath, winter.

The grass under my shoes crackled. The festival lanterns strung along the path flickered — flames guttering, shrinking, burning blue at the edges where the cold strangled them. In the distance behind me, ten thousand people at the arena were still screaming my name, drunk on the duel, oblivious.

Here, fifty meters away, the world was ending in a whisper.

[Time remaining: 15:09]

I hit the dormitory door at a full sprint, shoulder first.

It didn't move.

I did.

I bounced off like a tennis ball hitting a fortress wall and landed on my frozen ass in the courtyard grass, stars exploding across my vision.

'Okay.'

'Okay. Note to self. Door: frozen. Body: 0.009 mana commoner. Physics: still active.'

The door was sealed shut — ice a finger thick fusing oak to frame, cold radiating off it in waves I could feel against my face. I put both hands on it and shoved.

Nothing.

I slammed my shoulder into it again.

The door did not care. The door had never been less concerned about anything in its life. My body weight against an ice-sealed door was like a kitten headbutting a glacier.

Behind that door, muffled and distant, I could hear voices. Girls shouting. Pounding on walls.

'Right. Strength build? Failed. What would Kerman do — no, what would *I* do.'

Three hundred and forty-seven hours in Eschaton Hearts. Forty-seven of them on rain-slicked rooftops in stealth sections. I had speedrun this game's disaster scenarios more times than any human being alive.

'You don't fight the ice.'

'You bring a bigger rock.'

I scanned the courtyard. Festival decorations. Lantern poles. A flowerbed. And —

There.

A decorative stone bench. The kind rich schools install so parents on visiting day can sit and admire the topiaries. Granite. About waist-high. Easily two hundred kilos.

I grabbed one end and lifted.

My spine made a sound like a bag of microwave popcorn.

'AAAGH — okay — okay — natural 20 on the lift check, apparently —'

I've never been strong. Kang Min-jun's exercise routine consisted of walking to the convenience store. Lucen Gray was better, but "better" was still "can carry his own schoolbag without wheezing."

Adrenaline, though.

Adrenaline is the one buff that doesn't check your stats.

I got the bench up to chest height, staggered three steps, and swung it at the door like the world's least graceful battering ram.

*CRACK.*

The ice sheet across the door spider-webbed.

I swung again. My arms were jelly. My vision was tunneling.

*CRRRACK.*

The ice split with a sound like a frozen lake giving way, and the door burst inward, slamming against the corridor wall hard enough to shake dust from the ceiling.

I dropped the bench collapse of my life, sucked in one breath of the cold air pouring out of the doorway —

— and the breath froze in my chest.

'Oh,' I thought, staring into the corridor.

'Oh, that's not good.'

---

The inside of the dormitory was a nightmare someone's geometry homework had designed.

The corridor had become a tunnel of ice. Not a coating — a *structure*. Ice jutted from the walls in angled planes, fractal ridges, spiraling columns that branched and re-branched into smaller and smaller copies of themselves. The lanterns were encased, their light trapped inside the ice like fireflies in amber, turning the whole corridor a drowned, ghostly blue.

And every door was sealed.

Every single one.

From behind the nearest door: pounding. A girl's voice, high and scared.

"Hello?! Is someone — the door won't — HELLO?!"

"Hold on!" I yelled back, which was possibly the most confident lie I'd ever told, and I once told a guild leader I knew what I was doing during a raid.

'Triage. Triage, come on, think.'

'Seventy-odd students iced into their rooms. Entity event center of mass: Alice. The ice is a symptom, not the disease. Cut the head off the snake and the body stops moving.'

'Or in this case — hug the snake? The metaphor broke down. Moving on.'

[Time remaining: 12:31]

I ran down the corridor.

My footsteps crunched on a floor that had become a skating rink. Twice I nearly went down, catching myself on ice ridges that bit into my palms like razor wire. The cold was getting worse with every meter — a gradient, pulling me forward, and I understood with a speedrunner's grim clarity that I didn't need directions.

I just had to follow the temperature.

Down the main corridor. Up the stairs — the banister groaning under a skin of ice — down the second-floor hallway where the frost on the windows was so thick the moonlight barely survived it.

Cold.

Colder.

And then I heard her.

*"NO!"*

Alice's voice. Distorted underneath, doubled, like two people shouting from the same throat.

*"I WON'T. I WON'T LET YOU. STAY AWAY FROM —"*

Her door was open.

No — not open. *Blown* open. The door hung crooked on one hinge, chunks of it scattered across the hallway floor, the wood around the frame splintered outward and sheathed in rime.

I stopped in the doorway.

And for a moment, the gamer brain — the part of Kang Min-jun that had seen every graphic, every cutscene, every endgame boss intro this genre could produce — went completely, utterly silent.

Alice was floating.

Three inches above the floor, hair drifting upward like she was underwater, uniform disheveled, feet bare against nothing. Around her rotated a sphere of Abyssal circles — three, four, five concentric rings of shifting geometry, spinning at different speeds, glyphs crawling along their edges like insects.

The air around her didn't shimmer.

It *bowed.*

Light bent. The room's dimensions felt negotiable. My depth perception kept sliding around like the walls couldn't decide how far away they were.

And she was crying.

Even like this — floating, ringed in apocalypse math, eyes blown into pits of void-black from lid to lid — tears were cutting tracks down her cheeks, freezing before they reached her jaw, dropping off her chin as tiny beads of ice that clicked against the floor.

*"STAY AWAY,"* she screamed, and I couldn't tell who she was screaming at — the thing inside her, or me.

Then she saw me.

Both of her saw me.

The spinning circles *stopped.*

Every sphere, every ring, every crawling glyph froze in the air at once — and rotated, all together, to face me.

"You came."

Two voices from one mouth.

One was Alice. Small. Wrecked. Disbelieving — the voice of someone who'd been drowning alone and just heard a knock on the hull. Relieved. Grateful. Terrified it wasn't real.

The other voice was the ocean that was drowning her. It didn't speak in words so much as in *gravity*. It said *you came* the way a whirlpool says *welcome.*

[WARNING]

[You are at the epicenter of an Abyssal containment failure.]

[Recommend: run.]

[Statistically, you will not run. Because you are you.]

'Statistically, shut up.'

I stepped into the room.

---

The cold hit me like a freight train.

One step in and it punched through my clothes, through my skin, straight into bone. Frost bloomed across my eyebrows. My lips went numb. The tips of my ears caught fire the way they only do when they're freezing.

Each breath was a knife made of glass. My lungs burned, and I thought, absurdly — *oh, so this is where the stitch in my side was going. It was just waiting for the cold to really commit.*

Second step.

The circles resumed spinning — faster. Interested. The void-black pits of Alice's eyes tracked me, and underneath the eldritch geometry I could see her hands trembling.

"Lucen." Just her voice now. Just Alice. "You shouldn't — you can't be in here, I can't stop it, I can't — *it's pulling, it's been pulling all night and I'm so tired* —"

The second voice surfaced over hers like a fin breaking water:

*"Yes. Come closer. Closer. The empty one. The hollow one. We have felt you across the dark for so long —"*

'Screw this boss intro.'

Third step. Fourth.

I crossed the last meter of frozen floor, through the rotating rings of Abyssal geometry — which parted around me slowly, almost delicately, like fingers opening — and I took Alice Rimehart's hands.

'What I'm about to do,' some distant, rational part of me observed, 'is the magical equivalent of licking a leaking nuclear reactor.'

The entity surged.

It hit me through the contact points in our palms like a dam breaking — a flood of vast, ancient, *hungry* intent pouring down my arms and into my chest, and for one infinite second I understood everything about it.

It was old. Older than the academy, older than the empire, older than the concept of names. It had been sealed and sealed and sealed again, and every seal had taught it a new kind of loneliness. And now it had found a girl made of ice and sleepless nights, a girl who pressed her feelings down until they turned to frost — and it had whispered to her, for years, *I can fill that. I can fill all of it. Let me in and you will never be empty again.*

And now it had found *me.*

Me — the void. The zero. The 0.009. The walking absence where mana was supposed to live.

It dove into me like a starving thing diving into a well.

*Pouring.*

Desperate, endless, obliterating — pouring itself into the emptiness at the center of me, trying to fill the hollow, trying to take root, trying to *become* something that lived inside a human-shaped container —

And finding nothing.

Nothing to grip. Nothing to burn. Nothing to hold.

Because my void wasn't a container.

It was an *absence.*

You can't fill a hole with no bottom. You can't pour the ocean into a place where the concept of "full" doesn't exist. The entity plunged and plunged and plunged into me and every time it grasped, it grasped *nothing* —

My vision whited out.

Sound went away.

The cold became heat became nothing became —

I was standing in the dark.

My dark. I'd been here before, in a sense — that blank, featureless nowhere I brushed against every time a spell slid off me, every time my status window opened. The inside of 0.009.

Except now it had a resident, and the resident was having a meltdown.

The entity raged through the emptiness around me — a vastness of cold geometry, spinning, shredding itself against the nothing, screaming in a voice like glaciers calving:

*HOLD. HOLD. TAKE. KEEP. FILL —*

'You don't get it, do you?'

And there, in my own void, standing in absolute nowhere, Kang Min-jun — twenty-three, unemployed, 347 hours logged, man who had died or not-died playing a video game too hard — actually felt something like pity.

'There's nothing here for you. That's my whole stat sheet. I'm the only person on this continent who can't give you what you want.'

'I'm not a cup. I'm a leak.'

The vastness shuddered.

'But hey.' I shrugged into the dark. 'You can crash here. If you stop trying to eat her house.'

'Small place. No furniture. Terrible Yelp reviews. But the rent's free, and nobody's ever tried to seal you out of it.'

'Deal?'

The entity went still.

And then — I felt it. The pressure. Reversing. Slowly. Carefully. Almost... sheepishly. Like a cat that had been clawing the furniture and finally decided to just... sit in the cardboard box instead.

The void and the abyss regarded each other.

And reached equilibrium.

---

[Sound returned]

[Light returned]

I gasped — a huge, ragged, frozen gasp — and I was back in the room, on my feet somehow, still holding Alice's hands. My fingers were blue-gray at the tips. Frost dusted both our joined hands like we'd been dipped in sugar.

The circles had stopped spinning.

No — they were dissolving. Ring by ring, glyph by glyph, the Abyssal geometry unwound itself from the air and guttered out like embers, drifting down and vanishing before they touched the floor.

The temperature rose one degree. Then two.

Alice's feet touched the ground.

She swayed — I caught her weight — and her eyes found mine.

Half void-black. Half glacial blue. A horizon line drawn straight through her irises, and even then, even *then*, somehow, it was her looking out.

"Lucen," she whispered.

"Yo."

Brilliant. Incredible dialogue from the hero. Real protagonist hours. 'Don't let go,' I should have said, cool and steady —

"Don't let go," she whispered.

Oh. She said it first.

"I won't," I said, and meant it so hard my voice cracked.

So we stood there. In a room that looked like an ice sculpture's murder scene, in a dormitory full of students who would wake up with a cold and a story, hands clasped so tight my frostbitten fingers screamed — and slowly, painfully, degree by degree, the ice began to melt.

Water ran down the walls.

It pooled around our shoes. It dripped from the doorframe in a soft patter like rain. From the corridor, distantly, I heard a door crack open and a girl's bewildered voice: "…The door… the door just opened?"

[SYSTEM: CALAMITY EVENT — DEFUSED.]

[Entity Containment: RE-ASSERTING.]

'We did it,' I thought, with the weary flair of a man watching the victory screen load. 'Zero combat power. Zero spells cast. Technique used: hand-holding.'

'Somewhere, every combat instructor at this academy just felt a disturbance.'

Alice was looking at me.

Really looking. The black was retreating from her eyes in slow tides, glacial blue flooding back in — and her expression did something complicated. Search. Calculate. Conclude.

"Every time," she said. Her voice was wrecked. Quiet. "Every time I'm falling apart — you're there."

I tried to smile. My face was half-frostbitten, so it probably came out looking like a stroke. "Coincidence."

"Once is coincidence."

She squeezed my hands. Both of them were shaking. I couldn't tell whose trembling belonged to whom anymore.

"Twice is fate."

'Ah.'

'Ah, no. That's a flag. That is textbook, verbatim, bolded-and-italicized flag dialogue. I have READ this line. It appears in chapter select art.'

Then she stepped forward, and rested her forehead against my chest.

The world went very quiet.

She was cold. Not the room's cold — *her* cold, deep and bone-carried, the chill of someone who'd been fighting winter from the inside all night. Small. Trembling. Her hair smelled faintly of frost and candle smoke, which shouldn't have been a combination that existed, and was. My heart was slamming against my ribs like it was trying to escape the building via my sternum.

"Please don't leave me," she whispered.

I couldn't even see her face.

"I'm not going anywhere," I said.

Smart. Wonderful. Statistically the single most lethal sentence available to a man in my position. No regrets. Zero. I'd say it again.

And there — where our hands touched, pressed between us — I felt it.

The entity.

Dormant. Settled. Pulsing gently, rhythmically, in time with our joined pulse. Not grasping. Not clawing. Not pouring.

*Purring.*

Content.

The void and the abyss had found each other across an impossible gulf of making-no-sense, and had apparently decided to be roommates.

I stood in a melting ice cave, holding the Sleeping Calamity of the northern continent while an ancient sealed horror purred against my palm like an appliance, and I thought with total clarity:

'This is extremely, cosmically, catastrophically not part of the plan.'

[SYSTEM: CALAMITY EVENT — DEFUSED.]

[Alice / Celestia Rimehart — Entity Containment: RESTABILIZED.]

[Method: Void absorption. Extremely dangerous. Do not repeat.]

'The system is telling ME not to repeat it. I live here. I am the one it happened TO.'

[Your mana index: 0.009 → 0.007.]

I stared at the window.

[You LOST mana. You lost mana you did not have.]

'That's not how numbers work. That's not how ANYTHING works. You can't subtract from nearly-zero and get *less nearly-zero* — is my mana in *debt*? Did I just take out a magical LOAN?!'

[Your body cannot sustain this.]

'Noted!!'

[Alice / Celestia Rimehart — Affection Level: 8.]

The warmth that had been slowly returning to my veins turned to ice water all over again, and this time it had nothing to do with the room.

Eight.

I knew what eight was.

I had sat at my desk at 4 AM with the strategy wiki open and the affection formulas memorized, grinding route flags with a spreadsheet, and I knew exactly what that number meant in Eschaton Hearts.

[Note: In the original game, Affection Level 8 is the 'Irreversible Emotional Bond' threshold.]

[Alice Rimehart is now bonded to you in a way that cannot be undone.]

[If you die, her containment fails permanently.]

[You are now her anchor.]

[You did not consent to this.]

'NO I DID NOT.'

'I came up here to stop a Bad End! I performed emergency maintenance! Nobody mentioned becoming *load-bearing*!'

[Bad End No. 63: 'Skin for Love' — RE-CONFIGURED.]

[Status: No longer avoidable through distance.]

[Now avoidable only through survival.]

'Oh, perfect. Wonderful. Phenomenal UX design. The death flag used to have an escape clause — "just stay away from her" — and now it's "simply don't die, ever." Great. I'll add "immortality" to the build order right between "learn to run" and "cry less."'

Alice's forehead was still against my chest. Her breathing was evening out. Slow. Deep. The trembling was settling. I looked down at the top of her head and felt something in my chest do something structurally unsound.

And then the final window opened.

The text was different. Slower. Not the system's flat courier font — the other one. The one that only ever showed up when She deigned to comment.

[Note from Management:]

[I am going to say this once, Min-jun, and I want you to feel the weight of every syllable.]

[I am not frightened for you.]

[I am frightened *for her.*]

[Do you understand what you are holding right now? That girl is a sealed apocalypse who has spent seventeen years being handled, contained, assessed, and feared. You are the first living thing that ever ran *toward* her.]

[The entity isn't purring because of equilibrium, little void.]

[It's purring because the girl it lives inside finally slept soundly — in your arms, in the middle of a disaster — and ancient things are simple, in the end. They reflect.]

[So.]

[If you break that girl's heart, I will not need to arrange a Bad End for you.]

[She will unmake the continent with her own two hands, and I will sit in my theater seat and applaud.]

[Be worthy of this.]

[Both of you deserve that much.]

The window faded.

The dormitory dripped around us. Downstairs, doors were opening, voices rising — confused, cold, alive, safe. Somewhere far away across the campus, the distant roar of ten thousand festival-goers rolled on, celebrating a duel that already felt like it happened in another century, to another boy.

Alice shifted against my chest.

"Mm," she said, without lifting her head. "You're thinking loudly."

"I'm calculating my life expectancy."

"And?"

I looked at the frost melting off the ceiling. At my blue fingers laced through hers. At the faint, subliminal purr where our hands touched, small and ancient and content as a cat in a sunbeam.

'Forty-seven rain-slicked rooftop hours. Three hundred and forty-seven total. One hundred percent completion. Every route, every flag, every bad end catalogued.'

'None of them looked like this.'

"...Accommodating," I said. "Pending review."

She laughed — a tiny, exhausted, wrecked sound, muffled against my sternum.

And held on tighter.
