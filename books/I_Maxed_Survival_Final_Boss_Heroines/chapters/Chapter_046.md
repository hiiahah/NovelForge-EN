# Chapter 46: The Shadow Banquet: Social Warfare

---

Three seconds.

That was how long it took Iris Aurelia, First Princess of the Aurelian Empire, to read the Crystal Ballroom like a deployment map.

Alice Gramielle — west sector, dessert table orbit path, trajectory projected to intersect the boy by the pillar in approximately ninety seconds.

Rhea Valtor — east sector, motionless beneath the false stars, sword at her hip, radiating the kind of disinterest that only people who were very, very interested ever managed to perfect.

Countess Voss — center floor, circulating, watching everyone with the gentle smile of a woman cataloguing souls for future reference.

And Lucen Gray.

Pillar, fourth row of the gallery. Standing next to Lucas Ashford. Holding a glass of cider like a grenade with the pin half-pulled.

*Movement orders,* Iris thought.

"Sera."

Her attendant materialized at her elbow — quiet as folded paper, the way only royal staff could be.

"Alice Gramielle is heading toward the pillar. Intercept her at the pastry rotation. Compliment her cookies. Ask about the recipe. Be *fascinated*."

Sera's face did not change, because Sera's face never changed. "How fascinated, Your Highness?"

"Recipe-card fascinated. Measuring-spoon fascinated."

"Understood."

Sera flowed away into the crowd like water finding a crack.

Next problem.

Iris drifted — no, *glided*, posture perfect, chin at the exact angle that two hundred portraits had taught her bloodline — toward the solarium side of the floor. There. Baronet Halden, third son of a logistics fortune, desperate to matter to anyone above his station.

"Baronet Halden." She smiled the smile reserved for useful minor nobility. "I've been meaning to ask about your family's shipping tariffs along the eastern corridor."

The Baronet's soul visibly left his body and returned wearing a better cravat. "Y-Your Highness — of course — the tariffs —"

She positioned him precisely.

By the end of her second sentence, the Baronet stood between the main floor and Rhea Valtor's column like a decorative wall sconce with opinions about freight costs. For Rhea to reach the ballroom's center now, she would have to walk around them — or through a conversation about grain levies, which for a Valtor amounted to the same social impossibility.

*Battlespace secured.*

And only then — only then — did Iris allow herself to look at him.

Lucen Gray stood by his pillar in his borrowed coat, rumpled dark hair, expression doing that thing it did — that unfocused, inward-tilted stillness, like a man reading a book written on the inside of his own skull.

He was looking at Rhea.

Not nervously. Iris had catalogued his nervous look weeks ago — the way his eyes counted exits, the way his weight shifted to the balls of his feet.

This was different.

This was *concern*.

*He is worried,* Iris thought, with the slow, dawning horror of a general watching her own flank collapse from inside, *about the girl he is going to fight in forty minutes.*

What kind of person did that?

The kind who broke measuring crystals.

The kind who knew a dead empress's tea.

The kind who crawled across arena floors when standing had stopped being an option, and ran through ice storms to reach girls who might burn down the world.

Her kind of person.

She hadn't known she *had* a kind of person until four weeks ago.

*Sera,* she thought, watching her attendant laugh at something Alice Gramielle said about cardamom, *delay her a little longer.*

*I need to think about what I've become.*

I knew a zoning technique when I saw one. Four years of competitive PvP ranking teaches a man what controlled space looks like.

'Forty minutes,' I calculated, eyeballing the ballroom's grandfather clock — enchanted, probably, because at Stellaris Royal Academy even the furniture had a bloodline. 'Forty minutes to duel the second-ranked combat specialist of my year, in front of every important person I've accumulated like rare drops.'

My plan for the duel could be summarized in one word.

*Philosophy.*

The Empty Stance. Voss's term. The idea that my void-signature — my complete, 0.009-out-of-100,000 absence of meaningful mana — was not a hole in the world but a *shape* the world couldn't predict.

Beautiful. Elegant. Poetic, even.

As a combat strategy against a girl who had been swinging a sword since she could form object permanence, it ranked somewhere between "thoughts and prayers."

"You're doing the face again," Lucas said.

"What face?"

"The face where you're somewhere else entirely, doing math about your own death."

See, this was the problem with Lucas Ashford. In the game, he was the protagonist — brave, straightforward, earnest to a fault. The kind of hero who shouted his attack names.

In real life, he was all of that, *plus* terrifyingly perceptive at exactly the wrong moments.

"I'm doing breathing exercises," I said.

"You're doing arithmetic. I can hear it."

"Breathing exercises *have* arithmetic. It's called counting."

In *Eschaton Hearts*, my 347 hours of playtime had taught me exactly what happened to player avatars in a formal duel against Rhea Valtor. There were fourteen recorded duel events on her route.

Her win rate against players was ninety-four percent.

The other six percent were players who frankly cheated, and one guy who —

'Don't think about the Skyfall Skip now. Breathe. Count. In, two, three.'

"Lucen!"

A voice like sunlight breaking through tactical fog.

Alice Gramielle arrived at my elbow with a plate held in both hands, chin-up, eyes shining with the terrible wholesome energy of a final boss disguised as a bakery. On the plate: cookies. Golden, knobbly, arranged with the precision of artillery given a degree in pastry.

"Energy for the duel!" she announced. "These have cinnamon AND cardamom."

'Those are the same thing,' part of my brain said automatically. 'They're literally related spices.'

The rest of my brain said: *Don't you DARE say that out loud.*

"Thank you," I said, taking the plate, because I had read Alice's route and I knew that her cookies were safe, her intentions were sincere, and her affection was the single most dangerous resource on this continent.

"Cardamom is a warming spice," she informed me, very seriously. "It helps circulation. For *fighting*."

"That's... actually not wrong," Lucas said.

"It is ENTIRELY right," Alice corrected him, with the confidence of a person who had never once lost an argument she had made up.

Somewhere across the ballroom, I felt it. Like a draft. Like the temperature of *being observed* dropping five degrees.

I didn't need my gamer instincts to identify the source.

Iris's gaze, from thirty meters away, had the approximate weight of an empire.

'One day,' I thought, chewing cinnamon-cardamom courage, 'I am going to find whoever wrote this System and we are going to have *words*.'

"You've got a lot of supporters, Lucen," Lucas said, watching Alice bustle back toward the dessert table with the triumphant waddle of a provider who has provisioned.

"That's one word for it."

"Another word?"

"Targets of concern."

Lucas laughed.

He laughed because he thought it was self-deprecating humor — the funny little provisional student being modest about his sudden, inexplicable popularity.

It was not humor.

It was a *tactical assessment*.

Every heroine flag in this ballroom was a condition on a future Bad End losing screen. Every warm look was a countdown. I had maxed survival in *Eschaton Hearts* — three hundred and forty-seven hours, every route, every death, every severed, frozen, immolated, and *poetically tragic* ending in the compendium — and the one lesson all of those hours taught me was this:

In this game, being loved was the most dangerous status effect in the code.

And I was currently standing in the Catherine wheel center of four of them.

"Targets of concern," Lucas repeated, grinning. "You're ridiculous."

'If only.'

The rumor arrived the way ballroom rumors always do.

Not announced. *Assembled*.

I caught it in pieces as I drifted — because standing still invites conversation, and conversation invites questions, and questions at this particular event had a mortality rate.

"...the princess hasn't left his side all term, they say —"

"...Valtor. *Valtor*, are you listening? Dragon-lineage on the mother's side, my cousin swears the family crypts —"

"...and the Saintess-candidate girl brings him food. Brings him *food*. In public —"

"...a provisional. A *provisional with no mana* —"

And then, from a cluster of minor nobility near the ice sculpture, the piece de resistance. Delivered in the hushed, thrilled tone of a man who has just invented a conspiracy and already believes it:

"— which means he's not *missing* mana at all, he's *concealing* it, and who conceals a signature like that? No one alive. *No one alive.* Old blood has to be involved. Old *Empire* blood—"

'Oh,' I thought, with the flat calm of a man reading his own absurd obituary. 'Oh, they're speedrunning *this* route now.'

'Please stop helping,' I thought at the sky, at the ceiling, at whatever cosmic pervert was running this simulation.

The ceiling, charmingly hung with false stars and floating candlelight, said nothing.

The false stars twinkled.

'I know you're enjoying this,' I thought at them.

Something behind my left shoulder shifted. The air changed — rearranging itself around a presence the way water rearranges around a stone.

"Lucen, dear."

Countess Voss.

She arrived the way weather arrives — not walking *to* you so much as the room deciding she was now here. Silver-framed spectacles. Calm as a library. Warm as something that has decided to keep you.

"May I introduce you to someone?"

Every alarm in my body rang at once, and every single one was dressed as good manners, so I smiled and said, "Of course, Professor."

Because that is the thing about nets.

When you can see them, the correct move is still to walk in smiling — and count the knots.

## —

She guided me through the crowd with one hand resting — resting — on my elbow. The nobility parted for her the way they parted for no one else tonight, not even Iris.

That told me more than a character sheet ever could.

We stopped before a cluster of senior faculty near the head of the hall — old men and older women in formal robes, the kind of people who existed at the edges of *Eschaton Hearts'* Year One content like furniture the devs never fully implemented.

All except one.

He sat rather than stood. Robes the grey of deep winter. Hair white not like age but like *snow* — like the color had been snowed rather than lost. His face was turned toward us, and his eyes —

His eyes were wrong.

Not clouded. Not milky. Simply *unfocused* in a way that had nothing to do with vision — like a telescope pointed at forever, doing this room the courtesy of pretending.

'No,' I thought. My stomach was suddenly somewhere near Rhea's gifted boots. '*No, he's not in the Year One script. He doesn't appear. He's not supposed to appear until—*'

"Headmaster," Voss said, gently. "May I present one of our provisional students."

The Headmaster of Stellaris Royal Academy turned his face toward me.

Nothing happened.

That was the terrifying part. No pressure. No aura. None of the dramatic mana-crush the game loved to render in cutscenes. He simply attended to me — and the weight of being attended to *by that* settled onto my shoulders like snow onto a branch that is doing math about how much it can hold.

"So this is the boy who broke my crystal," the Headmaster said.

His voice was quiet. The entire cluster of senior faculty had gone still — the stillness of people pretending not to listen with their entire souls.

"A measuring crystal, Headmaster," Voss clarified, gently.

"I know what crystal."

A pause. The blind eyes fixed on the space in front of my chest — the space where, for every other human being in this ballroom, a mana signature burned. Bright ones. Dim ones. Every person in this room was a candle.

"There's... nothing there," the Headmaster said.

'0.009,' my brain offered, unhelpfully, hysterically. 'There is technically *0.009* there, sir, the enchanted wooden training dummies out-wrapper me—'

"Remarkable." He leaned forward — a fraction, no more — and I had the sudden, absolute certainty that if he chose to, he could look at me the way I'd once looked at *Eschaton Hearts'* splash screen. A thing to be loaded. A thing to be *played*. "Not a concealment. Concealment is a *lie*, and lies have texture. This..." He tilted his head, listening to a silence no one else could hear. "This is honest. A perfectly shaped nothing."

The words landed somewhere deep and cold.

Because he was right. That was the whole joke of my existence here — I wasn't hiding what I was. I was the only person in this entire academy with *nothing to hide*, and absence, it turns out, casts a shadow like anything else.

"Voss," the Headmaster said, never looking away from my nothing. "Keep an eye on this one. He's interesting."

"I intend to," Voss said warmly.

Behind the spectacles, something kind and bottomless agreed.

I smiled. I performed the smile of a grateful, harmless provisional student — seat 7-F, back row, decorative trash mob, certainly no one worth noting in any ledger that mattered.

And underneath the smile, in the part of my mind that had three hundred and forty-seven hours of this world's resignation letters memorized, I felt the net finish drawing closed around me.

'He wasn't supposed to know I exist until Year Two.'

'He's looking at me in *Year One*.'

'The script is off the rails and the rails were the only plan I had.'

## —

I escaped. I extracted myself with the polished cowardice of a man who has fled cutscenes professionally.

Back at my pillar — my pillar, my one true friend in this ballroom — I set down the plate of cookies and breathed.

'Inventory check,' I thought, because ritual is what terror does to feel organized.

The coat: borrowed, warm, carrying the lingering trace of someone who had wanted me to survive the night.

The cravat at my throat: silk, precisely tied — *her* hands had done this, an empress-in-waiting fiddling with a commoner's collar like it was a matter of state.

The boots on my feet: Valtor steel and leather, gifted with a glare that had meant something Valtors never said aloud.

And in my pocket, small and absurd and heavier than the stage itself: butterscotch.

Four items. Four heroines. Four different ways of saying *stay alive*.

I was wearing all of my death flags as formalwear.

'If this were the game,' I thought, 'this is where I'd quicksave.'

Then the bell.

Not a clock bell — a *ceremonial* bell, silver-sweet, the kind struck at Stellaris for two things: coronations, and sanctioned violence.

The banquet master's voice rolled across the Crystal Ballroom, warm with professional delight at getting to say something interesting:

"Honored guests — the exhibition duel will commence in **fifteen minutes.**"

"Combatants," he added, as the ballroom ignited into a murmur like a struck hive, "please prepare."

Movement, east sector.

Rhea Valtor was already walking.

She crossed the floor toward the ballroom's connected dueling hall without hurry, without drama — the parting of the crowd around her absolute and instinctive, the way crowds part for winter. Her hand rested on her hilt. Not gripping. *Resting.*

She did not look back.

She didn't need to. She knew — the way in the game she always knew — exactly where I was standing, exactly when I would follow, exactly how long it takes a man to walk to his own execution in borrowed shoes.

'Okay,' I thought.

I set Alice's cookies down on the ledge, one bite gone from the topmost — an offering to the goddess of cardamom and poor decisions.

'Okay.'

I straightened my collar. The silk cravat sat against my throat like a hand that believed in me.

I felt Rhea's boots, solid, planted, made for floors just like the one beneath the dueling hall's ceiling of chains and winter chandeliers.

I touched my pocket. Butterscotch. Still there. Still warm, which was impossible, which was the point.

Somewhere far above the false stars, I knew — *knew* — that patient, ancient attention was tilted downward, curious, wrapped in silver light, watching what the interesting nothing-boy would do.

Four pairs of eyes on my back.

One pair of blind eyes, seeing me clearest of all.

And one pair of kind, silver-framed eyes watching with the patience of a shepherd counting her flock — and being very pleased with the tally.

'Fifteen minutes,' I thought, and took the first step. 'Sixty-one steps to the dueling hall. One swordswoman. Fourteen known loss conditions.'

'And one provisional student with 0.009 mana, an empty stance, and a 347-hour education in every single way this night can kill him.'

The murmurs rose as I crossed the floor — *the heir, they say, the hidden heir, watch him walk, look how calm—*

If only they knew the calm was just fear that had finished its calculations and clocked out.

I walked beneath the false stars toward the dueling hall, toward the waiting blade, toward the final examination of Volume 1 of my very stupid afterlife.

Behind me, the Crystal Ballroom held its breath.
