# Chapter 31: The Mock Duel Festival (Announcement & The Challenge)

I overslept.

Which, given that a literal dragon demigod had spent the previous evening giggling at the stars outside my window while deciding I was her new favorite puzzle, was honestly a miracle in itself.

'Considering everything, I should get bonus points just for showing up to school.'

No such bonus existed. I checked.

I arrived at the Assembly Hall with four minutes to spare, hair still damp, uniform collar crooked, and approximately the energy level of a wet sock. The full first-year class was already filtering in — a river of pressed uniforms, polished boots, and inherited family crests gleaming on chests like little metal signs that read *I matter and you don't.*

I slid into my regular spot along the wall. Not a seat. A *spot*. Assembly Hall didn't believe in assigned seating, so I believed in standing near the exit.

They say speedrunners are always planning their escape route first.

They're right.

---

```
[Day 23 — 08:14]
[Location: Stellaris Royal Academy — Grand Assembly Hall]
[Attendance: Full first-year class]
[Your condition: Sleep-deprived, spiritually bruised, functionally cardboard]
[Active flags: Bad End No. 89 (DISTANT AWARENESS), plus assorted ambient doom]

[Evil Goddess: You look like death. And I've *seen* death. He's better dressed.]
```

'Good morning to you too.'

---

A chime rippled through the hall — mana-reinforced, the kind that vibrated pleasantly in your molars — and Instructor Kaelin stepped onto the raised platform at the front.

Kaelin was one of those instructors who looked like she'd been carved by a sculptor who got angry halfway through and decided *sharp* was a personality. Steel-gray hair. A posture that could be used to calibrate measuring instruments.

She didn't call for silence. Silence happened anyway. People at this academy had survival instincts — they'd just aimed them at the wrong targets.

"Good morning, cadets. I'll keep this brief, because you are teenagers and your attention span is legally classified as a rounding error."

A few nobles laughed in that careful, calibrated way of people checking to see if others were laughing first.

".As you know, the midterm evaluation window has closed. Your rankings are posted. And so, per academy tradition, the next major event on the first-year calendar is now upon us."

She gestured. The air behind her ignited — not with fire, but with calligraphy, golden letters assembling themselves midair like a particularly showy website loading screen.

**THE MOCK DUEL FESTIVAL.**

Oh no.

'No, no, no, no.'

I knew this event. I knew this event the way a speedrunner knows the boss that one-shots you at the end of the first phase — intimately, resentfully, and with detailed notes.

---

"The Mock Duel Festival," Kaelin announced, "will take place over three days, beginning next week. During this period, formal challenges may be issued and accepted between cadets. All matches are refereed, conducted under enchanted safety barriers, and observed from public spectator galleries."

My stomach was already descending like a slow elevator toward the basement.

"Blades are blunted. Lethal spells are prohibited. The barrier enchantments are rated to contain up to third-tier magical output and regenerate continuously."

'Rated to contain third-tier output. Great. Comforting. My entire body is one well-aimed sneeze away from the morgue, but at least the arena walls are sturdy.'

"More importantly —" Kaelin's voice sharpened, and every noble kid in the room suddenly leaned forward half an inch, like sunflowers rotating toward the sun, "— festival results are formally recorded. They factor into second-semester class placement, resource allocation, scholarship reviews, and—" she paused, letting it breathe, "— *social standing*."

The hall *buzzed.*

Not loudly. Nobles were too well-bred to actually gasp. But there was a collective ripple — a hundred strategic minds rebooting simultaneously. I could practically hear the gears grinding, the calculations, the vendettas being pulled out and dusted off like seasonal furniture.

'Second-semester placement. Resource allocation. Social standing.'

In game terms: the festival was the ranking recalibration event. The one where hidden stat differences turned into public humiliation. Where rivalries were born, broken, and occasionally resolved with therapy.

*In the game*, the Mock Duel Festival was a background event. Flavor text. You participated if you wanted, skipped it if you didn't, and it barely mattered to any route.

But here's the thing about living inside a game instead of playing one —

*Flavor text happens to real people now.*

And I was one of them.

---

'Okay. Okay. Think.'

My brain kicked into speedrunner mode, the way it always did when danger put its hand on my shoulder.

'Three-day festival. Open challenge format. Nobody is *required* to challenge anyone. Refusal is socially expensive but not against the rules... for most people.'

'I am not most people.'

Provisional enrollment. A commoner with a mana index of 0.009, riding the goodwill of an administrative anomaly and the vague interest of several extremely terrifying women. My continued presence at Stellaris was a balloon animal — impressive that it existed at all, and one firm poke from being scrap latex.

So my plan was simple.

'Plan A: Don't accept anything. Don't stand out. Spend three days in the infirmary nursing a mysteriously persistent stomachache. Plan B: the library. Plan C: fake my own death and start a bakery in a coastal village under a new name.'

Plan C was gaining ground fast.

---

I was halfway through sketching out the bakery's business model — sourdough specialty, low dragon flyover zone, far from anything narrative-shaped — when it happened.

A chair scraped.

Not at the back, where the rest of us peasants clustered. In the second row. Noble territory.

And Derrick Holt stood up.

---

I knew that silhouette. Broad shoulders stuffed into a custom-tailored uniform that whispered *my father owns things*. Hair so aggressively styled it probably had its own mana index. And a jaw set at the particular angle of a young man who had been rehearsing a moment in the mirror for several weeks.

'Oh no.'

'Oh *no* no no no.'

The hall went quiet. Even Kaelin paused mid-sentence, one eyebrow drifting upward like it was filing a formal inquiry.

Derrick's voice came out loud. Practiced. Just a little too rehearsed at the edges, like toast left in thirty seconds too long.

"Instructor, if I may."

"You may not," Kaelin said, "but you're going to anyway."

A few stifled snorts. Derrick's jaw ticked, but he powered through like a noble through a locked gate.

"I, Derrick Holt, son of Baron Holt—"

*'Here it comes.'*

"—do formally, in accordance with festival rules and academy tradition, challenge—"

*'Please say someone else. Please say someone else. I'll trade. I'll trade anything. My lunch. My seat by the window. One kidney—'*

"—**Lucen Gray**, of the provisional enrollment—"

'...Damn.'

"—to a sanctioned duel."

---

Silence.

The kind of silence that has *weight*. The kind that sits on your shoulders and comments on your posture.

Then the hall *erupted* — not into noise, exactly, but into that dense, crackling murmur of a hundred conversations igniting at once. Heads turning. Eyes finding me. I could feel it, the sudden swarm of attention, like walking through a spider web made of gazes.

*'He's the provisional one—'*

*'—the commoner who ranked well on the—'*

*'—Holt got beaten by a zero-mana nobody, of *course* he'd—'*

My heart was hammering in a way that was frankly rude.

Because I'd *seen* this scene. From the other side of the screen.

Derrick Holt. The evaluation. I'd beaten him — not in mana, not in strength, but in *standing*. Provisional nobody, 0.009 mana, scraping above a baron's son on the posting lists by pure statistical glitch of academic scores. In a meritocracy on paper, that was a fact.

In an aristocracy at heart, it was a *crime*.

Derrick had been humiliated. Publicly, silently, on a posted ranking sheet where he couldn't punch it. So he'd done what noble sons did when their pride got dented by a peasant — he shined up the family's best leverage and aimed it at my throat.

A sanctioned duel couldn't be argued with. It was the aristocracy's favorite tool: legal violence with a formal dress code.

---

"Formal challenge registered," Kaelin said flatly, like she was stamping a form. She looked at me. The whole hall looked at me. "Cadet Gray."

I stood.

Somehow, my legs did the job. Traitors.

"Do you accept?"

---

Here's what nobody in that hall knew:

Refusal was an option. It was *in the rulebook.* Right there, section whatever. Any challenged cadet may decline.

But the rulebook had an invisible second page. The one every noble child learned at the dinner table before they learned their multiplication tables.

*Declining a duel is a surrender notice.*

*To decline is to admit inferiority.*

*To admit inferiority, as a provisional student, is to volunteer for the academic review board.*

*The academic review board will open your file, find the word "provisional," and escort you to the gate.*

The unwritten page. My old friend. Every game had one; life had entire anthologies.

'So. My choices. One: accept, and get folded like laundry by a noble kid with a grudge and a training montage. Two: decline, socially self-destruct, get reviewed, get expelled, become homeless, and then — let's check the forecast — oh look, four apocalyptic women with interest tags on my soul.'

'Wonderful menu. Five stars.'

[You do NOT survive expulsion. Definitely.]

[Evil Goddess: Do the math, meat puppet.]

'I did the math. I hate the math.'

---

"I accept."

Two words. Calm. Even. Boring, even.

Inside my skull, a continuous, unbroken scream was entering its third trimester.

---

The hall detonated into whispers again.

And here — here is where the universe's sick sense of comedy decided to clock in for its shift.

Because my face, overwhelmed by terror processing at maximum capacity, had simply... given up. Gone blank. Expression buffering at 0%.

And blank, it turns out, reads as *serene.*

"He's not even *worried*," Finn whispered — loudly, with the precision volume control of a foghorn, from two rows over. "Look at his face. Nothing. Zero."

"Holy—" someone breathed.

"Man's *bored*. He *wants* this."

'I do not. I want a bakery. I want a coastal village. I want, at minimum, to be in a different country.'

"He accepted in two seconds," Finn continued, awed, narrating my demise like a sports announcer with a head injury. "Didn't even *think* about it."

'I thought about it. I thought about it at speeds that would qualify as a new mana subtype—'

And there it was. The class gossip engine, doing what it did best: converting my panic-induced paralysis into mystique. Somewhere in my 347 hours of gameplay I'd read about this trope — the unflappable hidden master, the stone face that conceals depth —

News flash: sometimes a stone face conceals a man who has forgotten how feet work.

---

"Challenge registered and scheduled," Kaelin said, ignoring the murmuring with the practiced ease of a woman who had stopped caring around the middle wars. "Duel to take place during the festival window. Dismiss the theatricality, Cadet Holt — sit down."

Derrick sat. But not before his eyes found mine across the hall.

And there was a smile there.

'Oh. He's confident. He knows something.'

'That's worse than rage. Rage is sloppy. Confidence has a *spreadsheet*.'

---

Assembly dragged on — logistics, schedules, bracket formats, safety waivers requiring *signatures* — while my soul quietly detached and floated somewhere around the chandelier.

I paid attention to exactly one more thing.

Around the hall, the other rhythms of my weird life were humming along unbothered. Iris Aurelia — the princess, perfection incarnate, a route flag with legs — sat near the front in flawless posture, and hadn't turned around all morning. Rhea Valtor sat with her arms crossed like she'd built the concept of arms-crossed and was licensing it. Lucas Ashford, the actual *protagonist* of this world — Homeroom 1-A, seat 4-C, glowing faintly with narrative gravity as always — was watching me with an expression I could only describe as *worried in a main-character way.*

And somewhere in this city, a ten-thousand-year-old dragon who'd decided I was interesting was probably filing her nails.

'Good. Fine. Everything's fine.'

'I have maxed every route in this game. I have survived three apocalyptic entities' worth of attention flags. I can survive one angry teenager with a broadsword.'

'Probably.'

'...Probably?'

---

The dismissal chime went, and the hall dissolved into motion.

I made it exactly nine steps toward the exit before the gauntlet began.

---

"Lucen!"

Lucas Ashford materialized at my shoulder with the effortless kinematics of a B-rank protagonist — which he was, literally. Brown hair, earnest eyes, the aura of a guy who would absolutely take an arrow for a stranger and then apologize for bleeding on them.

In the game, Lucas was the player character. The hero. The guy the story bends around.

He was looking at *me* like *I* was the impressive one. The universe had a drinking problem.

"That took guts," Lucas said, falling into step. "Accepting like that."

'It took having no alternatives, actually, but sure, let's go with guts.'

"It was the only move," I said.

He nodded, like I'd said something profound. My nonsense was becoming proverb again. I hated how good I was at it.

"Listen — Holt's no joke with a blade. Family tutors since he was six. If you need a training partner in the next five days, I'm there. Morning, evening, whenever. Seriously."

He meant it, too. The protagonist of Eschaton Hearts, offering me sparring hours for free.

"...I'd appreciate that," I said, because refusing the protagonist's friendship flag seemed somehow more dangerous than accepting it.

I made it twenty more steps before interception two.

Rhea Valtor didn't approach people. She *materialized at their flank* like a weather system. One moment I was walking, the next there was a red-blooded war-scion walking beside me, eyes forward.

"That was sloppy."

"Good morning to you too."

"Holt telegraphed that challenge for weeks. You let him take initiative. First rule of the field — if a fight is coming, *you* pick the ground."

'I'm sorry, was there a version of today where I picked the ground? What was I supposed to do, challenge *him* first? Pre-emptively duel a baron's son in the hallway?'

"I'm survival-strategy oriented," I said.

"You're *survival-biased,*" Rhea said flatly, "which is a polite word for *your plan is to not die hard enough that the ref calls it*." She glanced at me then, and there was, under the granite, something almost like assessment. Respect's ugly cousin. "He'll come at you angry and fast. You've got no mana to answer with. So we're changing the protocol."

My heart sank with terrible foreknowledge.

"Dawn training continues," Rhea said. "But we're upgrading. No more endurance circuits. Combat drills. You need offense — real offense — before Thursday."

"You've already decided my entire week?"

"You have five days and a 0.009 mana index, Gray. Your week doesn't get a vote."

'She's not wrong. That's the worst part. She's not wrong.'

I escaped the hall. I *almost* made it to the courtyard.

"Lu~cen!"

Alice.

Alice came skipping around a pillar like a side quest, practically vibrating, flour still ghosting one cheek from some morning errand I was afraid to ask about. She skidded to a stop in front of me, hands clasped, eyes shining like I'd just been cast in the lead role of my own funeral.

"I heard, I heard! A duel! A *festival* duel!"

"Traditionally administered with the phrase 'I'm so sorry,' rather than with that face."

"That's why," she announced, with the gravity of a royal decree, "I'm going to bake you victory cookies!"

The universe paused. Somewhere, a record scratched.

'No.'

'No, no. Absolutely not. I know this flag. I have seen this flag. I have died to this flag.'

In Eschaton Hearts, Alice's route had an infamous quasi-hidden event — the *Baked Goods of Doom* chain. Her cooking was a procedurally generated hazard. Accept Alice's cookies, roll a saving throw against effect categories including: *food poisoning (mild)*, *food poisoning (hospitalization)*, and — in one legendary community-documented run — *temporary soul displacement.*

The wiki had a warning banner. With a skull on it.

"That's really sweet, Alice. I can't accept food before the match. Superstition." I grabbed the nearest folk excuse like a floating plank. "Old family tradition. Commoner thing. You understand."

Alice squinted at me. "You've made up *three* family traditions this month."

"They're very tradit."

She puffed her cheeks, then brightened instantly, comedy goldfish attention span working in my favor. "FINE. Then after the duel! Victory cookies *and* consolation cookies. I'll make both. Just in case!"

'Just in case. She said "just in case." The cover of my funeral program will have a hand-drawn cookie on it.'

I finally got out of the building. Sunlight. Courtyard. Bench. Freedom.

I sat down, closed my eyes, and took one whole breath.

Then a shadow fell across my lap.

A girl in an impeccable uniform stood over me, silver-white hair arranged with geometric precision, expression calibrated to *neutral-professional #3.* I recognized her — barely, from fifty feet away, always at the princess's shoulder.

Sera. Iris Aurelia's personal attendant.

My soul left my body a second time today. New record.

She held out a folded note. Cream paper. Softly glowing with a privacy enchantment that likely cost more than my entire existence.

"For you," Sera said.

I took it like a man accepts a live bomb from a polite stranger.

She turned and left without another word. No elaboration. Royal retainers were optimized like that — I remember the flavor text.

I opened the note. The handwriting was exquisite, of course. Every letter looked like it had a degree.

> *Lucen Gray,*

I sat on that bench for a long moment, note in hand, morning sun on my face, doing silent mental bookkeeping.

Tally of my current week:

One scheduled duel I couldn't win. One noble kid with a grudge and, apparently, a plan. One red-haired war machine planning to beat combat instinct into me at dawn. One protagonist offering friendship I was powerless to refuse. One cookie-based biohazard in development. One summons from a princess whose affection was statistically indistinguishable from a natural disaster.

And one dragon, in the distance of the world, humming.

'I really just wanted to finish a video game.'

---

The rest of the day I learned, from gossip flowing past me like runoff — because gossip at Stellaris was a public utility — what Derrick's smile at the assembly had meant.

He'd been ready. All of it, planned.

Since the evaluation, Holt had gone private. Full-time pullout of regular conditioning for a personal course. His father had retained a combat tutor — a retired army officer, the kind gray-market rumor said had done actual frontline work, the kind whose fees had names of their own.

And then there was the other thing. The thing people whispered rather than said.

Alchemical supplements. Mana catalyzers. The aristocracy's favorite gray market — expensive, borderline under academy doping statutes, and technically legal if sourced through a licensed apothecary and *not taken within seven days of an official event.*

The festival was announced today. The supplements were already in his blood. Do the math on their timing. Derrick's father was many things, but sloppy wasn't one of them.

'Three point seven percent.'

I stared at the system window until it politely faded.

*Three point seven.* In Eschaton Hearts, I'd fought endgame bosses on worse. I'd wrestled 0.5% drop rates into submission over two hundred consecutive runs. Numbers that small weren't *odds* to people like me.

They were just *invitations with bad manners.*

"...Fine," I muttered, standing up from the bench, dusting off uniform pants that cost someone else's fortune.

'Derrick Holt has a tutor, a treasury, and 163 points of cheat-boosted mana.'

'I have five days, the world's most violent personal trainer, a protagonist, a princess strategy meeting, and a complete mental map of every duel mechanic this world's engine was built on.'
