Day 8.

The day of my execution, appointment only.

The private study room was on the second floor of the West Wing, past a corridor lined with paintings of dead Aurelias who all looked at me with the same expression.

The expression said: *You will be joining us shortly.*

Sera stood outside the door.

She'd examined me the way a pillow inspector examines a pillow — brief, thorough, and with a visible desire to punch it. Then she'd opened the door, gestured me in, and closed it behind me with the soft finality of a coffin lid being fastened by someone with excellent etiquette.

And now.

Let me describe the room for you, because the room was a lie.

Warm oak paneling. A fireplace crackling with real logs, not magical flame, which meant someone had actually chopped wood for this. Two armchairs angled toward each other across a low reading table. A tea set. A plate of small butter pastries arranged with the kind of geometric precision that screamed *this was measured.*

It was cozy.

It was intimate.

It was, to any outside observer, a romantic evening for two.

It was also the single most dangerous room on the continent, scaled to my personal threat level: one (1) me.

'It looks like a date. Why does it look like a date. This is an interrogation chamber wearing a date's skin.'

"Sit," said Iris Aurelia.

I sat.

She was across from me in the other armchair, one leg crossed over the other, teacup in hand, wearing academy black like it had been tailored for a coronation. The firelight caught her hair and her eyes did that thing where they went from 'gold' to 'molten gold' to 'gold, but the kind they mint execution warrants from.'

She smiled.

It was the smile of a creature that had decided, days ago, that it was going to open me, and was now simply enjoying the packaging.

And between us, on the reading table, equidistant from both chairs, placed by her hand with surgical exactness—

My notebook.

My beautiful, stupid, treasonous notebook.

Every route guide. Every flag chart. Every statistical breakdown of five heroines and one dragon tyrant and every bad ending including the one where *this exact woman* burned a cathedral with the protagonist inside it on a Tuesday.

It sat between us like an unexploded bomb.

Correction.

Unexploded bombs don't have eyes on both sides waiting to see who blinks first.

"Shall we begin?" Iris said.

As if this were a pleasant social visit.

'Yes. Let us begin. Let us begin the weird little theater piece I've rehearsed for four days, titled "Please Don't Unmake Me, Your Highness."'

"Yes, Your Highness," I said.

"Iris," she corrected, softly. "In this room, it's Iris. Formality wastes time, and I find I dislike wasting time around you."

I opened the notebook.

My hands did not shake, because I had spent the entire walk here negotiating with my hands.

The negotiation had gone: *If you shake, we die.*

They had agreed, reluctantly, out of self-preservation, which is the only honest alliance that exists.

"So," Iris said. She set her teacup down. She produced — from nowhere, like a magician, like an executioner — her own notebook and a pen. "Show me what you've written in your... unique little script."

"Hangul," I said.

The word was out before the filter could catch it.

Her pen paused. "The script has a *name*?"

'Idiot. Idiot idiot idiot. You don't name your fake script. Naming things implies culture. Culture implies origin. Origin implies questions. Questions imply death.'

"...That's what my village called it," I said, keeping my voice flat. Flat gray. Lucen Gray. My name was literally my camouflage; the least I could do was perform it. "Old word. Nobody remembers what it means."

"Hm."

She wrote it down.

She actually wrote it down, in her immaculate Imperial cursive, and having watched her write for one week now I could tell she was cataloguing me in real time, and there was nothing I could do about it except continue existing incorrectly.

"Begin," she said.

---

Page one.

I'd prepared page one like a speedrunner prepares a route. Every step rehearsed. Every dialogue option pre-selected. No improvisation. Improvisation is where runs go to die.

"This first section is herbal notes," I said. "My grandmother was the village herbalist. I was her... assistant, I suppose. This is her work, mostly. I wrote down what I could remember after—"

I gestured vaguely.

The vague gesture was doing a lot of heavy lifting. It was carrying my entire fictional dead village, my fictional dead family, and my fictional tragic backstory that I'd assembled out of genre scraps like a man building a raft out of other rafts' corpses.

"After," Iris repeated. Not a question. A mirror.

"There's not much left of the village," I said.

That part was easy to say flatly, because in every universe, some version of my home was gone. The truth dressed up as a lie is the sturdiest kind of lie. I'd learned that from the game, ironically. Route design 101.

Iris watched me for a long moment.

Then she nodded, once, and lifted her pen.

"Then translate. Word for word, as best you can. I want the texture of it, not a summary."

'Of course you do.'

---

"Rain-cress," I read, pointing at the first entry — which was, in truth, grocery-list-length notes about *academy cafeteria schedules,* but she didn't know that and that was the entire point. "Grows in wet lowland soil. Leaves bitter, root sweet. Boiled root tea for fever. Don't harvest after the first frost, the root turns — the word means 'sullen'? Bitter. Toxic, mildly."

"Mildly toxic." Iris's pen moved. "Define mildly."

"Stomach cramps. A bad night. Not dangerous unless you're already sick."

"And the harvesting window?"

"Three weeks in early autumn. After that the frost gets it."

"What altitude does it grow at?"

'THERE it is.'

She said it casually, the way you'd ask someone to pass the salt. But her eyes came up off the page as she said it, and those eyes were not casual at all. Those eyes were a doctoral defense committee compressed into a single beautiful face with an army behind it.

"Low altitude," I said. "Valley floors, riversides. My village was in a valley."

"The Valley of…?"

"We just called it the valley."

"Your village had no name?"

"It had a name,* *I said. "I don't use it anymore. There's no one left to say it to."

Silence.

The fire cracked.

'Good. Good. Sad, vague, non-falsifiable. The three food groups of lying.'

"Mm," Iris said, and wrote something down, and I would have traded a year of my life — my lifespan was currently trading at an all-time low anyway, so, fine, a month — to read what it was.

---

We went on like that.

For twenty minutes, Iris Aurelia, First Princess of the Empire, heir to the throne, future Tyrant of the Ashen Decade, cross-examined a fake herbalism journal with the rigor of a war tribunal.

And here was the thing.

It *worked.*

What altitude? Valley floor. What season? Early autumn, three-week window. Preparation method? Boiled, never dried — drying concentrates the toxin. Contradictions? None. Because I had memorized the Compendium. Because panic is the best teacher. Because three days ago I'd realized she was going to do *exactly this* and I'd prepared for exactly this.

Iris asked about soil. I cited page 88.

Iris asked about drying methods. I cited page 203.

Iris asked what my grandmother did with the surplus harvest, and I said *sold it to a traveling merchant who came through every autumn, a fat man with a donkey, he cheated us on the weight every year and we let him because he was the only buyer* — which wasn't from any book, it was from an old folk tale Min-jun's actual grandmother used to tell about her own childhood, and it landed so true that I watched Iris's pen *relax.*

She sat back.

She picked up her tea again.

And I saw it. Clear as a health bar draining. The interest level, dipping. The tension in her shoulders, loosening. The predator's posture melting, degree by degree, into something almost—

Almost disappointed.

'She wanted it to be something more,' I realized. 'She wanted a conspiracy and I'm giving her a garden.'

*'Good. Be boring, Lucen. Be the most boring boy who ever lived. Be oatmeal.'*

"Well," Iris said, and there was a wry note in it now, the first genuinely unguarded sound I'd heard from her. "It's very thorough. Your grandmother was a meticulous woman."

"She used to hit my knuckles with a ruler when I mislabeled jars."

That was a lie. Min-jun's grandmother ran a convenience store in Incheon. The only thing she'd ever hit anyone with was a broom, and that one time was a boxing day sale incident and the man deserved it, allegedly.

But grief and convenience stores don't translate. So: herbalist grandmother. Ruler. Jars.

Genre-appropriate trauma. Fifty percent off.

Iris smiled.

Actually smiled — small, crooked, *real* — at the ruler story.

'Oh no,' I thought, distantly. 'Don't do that. Do you know what your route does to people?'

---

And that was when it happened.

That was the exact moment — of course it was that exact moment, the universe has comedic timing and I am its favorite joke — that her eyes drifted down to the open page.

And stopped.

"This section."

Her finger came down on the page. Not touching the paper. Hovering a centimeter above it, like it was warm.

"This section here. The character density is different."

'Oh no.'

"More compact," she said. Her head tilted, and her hair slid over her shoulder, and her voice stayed perfectly mild, which was the worst possible sign. "The strokes are more urgent. See here — and here — your hand was moving faster when you wrote this. You pressed harder. You wrote this passage in a different state of mind than the rest."

She looked up.

"What does it say?"

---

Let me tell you what that section said.

That section was my Iris Route notes.

I'd written them on Day 1, at 3 AM, by guttering candlelight, while my heart rate was doing things that medically qualified as entertainment. Panic handwriting. Dense, cramped, desperate Hangul.

The section said, translated faithfully:

*IRIS AURELIA — FINAL BOSS / ROUTE NOTES*
*Triggered by: sustained interest. Do NOT be interesting. Do NOT be competent in front of her witnesses. Tea ceremony event fires Week 4 if affection ≥ 20. Her triggers: control loss, perceived betrayal, anyone knowing about her mother. DO NOT MENTION THE MOTHER. Mention of the mother = route lock = bad end cascade. Bad End 12 "The Tyrant's Curiosity" fires if she decides you're a spy for a rival power. SHE WILL KEEP YOU ALIVE. THAT IS THE PROBLEM. SHE WILL KEEP YOU.*

'She is asking me to translate my own execution warrant.'

'Out loud.'

'To her face.'

"Well?" said Iris.

My brain ran the numbers. The whole section was radioactive. Every real translation path led somewhere fatal. I couldn't refuse — refusing was an admission. I couldn't stumble — stumbling was a confession.

I needed something true-sounding, mundane, emotional enough to justify the urgent handwriting — because she'd *clocked the urgency,* damn her eyes, the terrifying golden hawk eyes — but totally harmless.

Something handwritten in a hurry. Something that justified panic.

My grandmother. The fictional one. The excuse already on the table —

"It's —" I swallowed. Half of that was real. "It's a recipe. A tea recipe."

Iris's eyebrows lifted a millimeter.

"The density, the speed — I wrote it the first night here. At the academy. I couldn't sleep and I was—" I gestured vaguely again. Vague Gesture: my most reliable skill, S-rank, fully mastered. "Homesick. It was the last thing she taught me. I was scared I'd forget it, so I wrote it down fast. All of it. Before it could fade."

Iris said nothing.

Her finger had not moved from above the page.

"Go on," she said quietly.

---

So I went on.

I want the record — her record, which at this point probably exists and has a romantic subplot in the margins — to show that I chose the words *semi-randomly.*

I grabbed from the memorized Compendium. Golden-colored things, because I was looking at the fire. Bitter-sweet things, because that was the emotional register of the lie. A grieving person. A tea.

"It's called sunrise tea," I said. "That's what she called it. The color's gold, like — like the first light through a window."

'Good. Fine. Poetic, harmless, keep going.'

"It's made from marigold petals — picked at dawn, she was strict about that, she said petals picked later had no heart left in them — and white chrysanthemum. The marigold for color and warmth, the chrysanthemum for the bitter edge. Steeped together, never boiled, boiling bruises them. And then honey. Not stirred in — drizzled over the petals first, before the water, so it—"

I was improvising.

I was, to be clear, ad-libbing a fake tea with the confidence of a man falling off a cliff and grading his own form on the way down.

"—so the honey catches the heat first. She said it was for people who carry grief. It doesn't make the grief smaller. It just helps you sleep without dreaming. She said — she said some nights, not dreaming is mercy enough."

I stopped.

I ran out of invented recipe, and the silence after was so complete that I could hear the fire settling, the wood ticking, my own pulse in my ears keeping time like a worried metronome.

'Okay. Decent save. Melodramatic, but she's a fantasy royal, melodrama is a food group here. B plus. We surviv—'

I looked up.

And everything stopped.

---

Iris's teacup had frozen halfway to her lips.

Not lowered. Not raised.

Just — stopped. Suspended in the air at a slight, precise angle, like someone had paused her.

Her face—

Look, I had 347 hours of gameplay on this game. I had seen every sprite, every CG, every expression in Iris Aurelia's portfolio. The cold fury. The regal amusement. The hollow, terrifying calm of the Tyrant route. The single tear in the true ending CG that made half the fanbase weep.

I had never seen this face.

There was no sprite for it. It was *raw.* The composure hadn't cracked; it had simply been quietly set aside, like a teacup, and underneath was something open and unguarded and so bare it felt obscene to look at. Like I'd walked in on her sleeping. Like I'd pulled a bandage off a wound she'd forgotten she had.

"Say that again," she whispered.

"I— the recipe?"

"The tea." Her voice was very soft. Soft was *bad.* Soft was the volume she used in Bad End 14 before the floor opened. "The exact preparation. Say it again."

"...Marigold petals, picked at dawn. White chrysanthemum. Steeped, not boiled. Honey drizzled over the petals first, before the water."

The teacup came down.

She set it on the saucer with a click that was too careful, a click that had been *managed,* and her hand took a moment too long to leave the handle.

The fire cracked.

Somewhere downstairs, very faintly, a bell rang the hour.

"My mother," Iris said, very quietly, "used to drink marigold and chrysanthemum tea with honey. Every evening."

No.

"After my father's decisions became difficult to bear."

*No.*

"She drizzled the honey over the petals first. Before the water. She said the honey should taste the heat before anyone else did."

**No no no no no—**

"She said it was for people who carry things." Iris's voice had gone flat and distant and young. Younger than I'd ever heard it, in any route, in any ending. "She said it helps you sleep without dreaming. She said some nights, not dreaming is the mercy."

'That's what I said.'

'That's EXACTLY what I—

'No. No, it's what SHE said. It's what Iris's mother said. I didn't know Iris's mother had said that. Nobody knows that. That's not in the game files, that's not in ANY route, that detail doesn't EXIST in 347 hours of content—'

"Lucen."

I realized I'd gone completely still.

"No one knows that," Iris said.

Her eyes had come up to mine, and they were enormous, and gold, and wet at the edges in a way that had nothing to do with weakness and everything to do with tectonic plates shifting somewhere very far down.

"It's not in any record. There is no portrait, no diary, no correspondence. My father had her chambers sealed and her household dissolved. The servants who prepared her evenings are dead, or pensioned to sealed estates under oaths I helped draft myself." A muscle moved in her jaw. "I have checked. I have checked *for years,* because I wanted—"

She stopped.

She visibly did not finish that sentence, and the sentence stood there anyway, unfinished and bleeding.

"I was six," she said. "I remember the smell."

Iris stood up.

I almost knocked my chair over — didn't, barely, through sheer reflex — because in *three separate bad endings,* her standing up abruptly in a small room was the last animation frame before everything went wrong.

But she didn't come toward me.

She went to the fireplace. Put one hand on the mantel. Stood with her back to me, shoulders drawn in a line so strict it looked painful, staring into the fire like it owed her an explanation.

When she spoke, her voice was back under control.

Mostly.

"So," she said to the fire. "The village boy. The herbalist's assistant. No family, no records, no script on file in any archive of any nation on the continent." Each word a bead on an abacus. "Who writes in a language no scholar has ever seen, who sits in my academy with no patrons and no fear worth the name —" 

"*Fear,*" I said, before I could stop myself. "Lots of fear. Considerable fear. The fear is honestly my most developed trait."

"—and who, in the first hour of our first private meeting—" she went right over me, and now she turned, and the firelight made a burning outline of her, "—recites to me the bedtime ritual of the Empress Eleanora. Word. For. Word."

The mantel creaked under her hand.

"That detail exists in two places in this world, Lucen Gray. One is my memory. The other is a grave."

She crossed the room.

She did not walk back to her chair. She came around the table — *around the bomb,* my notebook sitting there forgotten, the greatest intelligence leak in imperial history rendered suddenly obsolete — and stopped in front of my chair, and looked down at me from very close, and her eyes were doing something I had no menu option for.

"So I will ask you once." Her voice dropped to something barely above the fire. "And I will know if you lie. I have spent my whole life being lied to by professionals. You're not one. I watched you lie for twenty minutes about flowers and it was *endearing.*"

'Endearing. She said endearing. That's worse. Somehow that's so much worse.'

"Who are you?" Iris whispered.

I opened my mouth.

I had no plan. I had no script. Every prepared line had dissolved the moment a dead woman reached out of a sealed grave and shared her tea recipe with me through cosmic coincidence.

So I looked up at Iris Aurelia — tyrant, final boss, six-year-old girl who remembered the smell — and I said the only true thing I had.

"...I don't know," I whispered. "Your Highness — Iris. I *don't know.* I don't know what that was. I learned that from my grandmother, in a valley that doesn't exist anymore, and I have never met your mother, and I have no explanation, and I am — " my voice did something undignified, " — genuinely as frightened by this as you are."

The room held its breath.

Iris stared down at me for five seconds that lasted a geological age.

And then — slowly, with terrible, trembling precision, like a woman stepping out onto ice that might be a miracle or might be a drowning — she reached out.

And put her hand on my hair.

"Six," she murmured, to no one, eyes gone somewhere far away and warm. "I was six. And you're telling me it just... followed you here."

'I am so dead.'

'I am the deadest man in the history of the Empire.'

Her hand was warm. The fire cracked. The notebook lay open on the table, forgotten, its terrible contents unread —

— because Iris Aurelia had stopped investigating the puzzle, and started investigating the miracle.

Which was worse.

Which was so, so much worse.
