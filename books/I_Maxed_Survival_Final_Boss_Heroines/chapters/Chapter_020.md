# Chapter 20: First Evaluation Exam: Round 2 & The Panel's Verdict

There is a special kind of peace, I've learned, that comes right before disaster.

It's the peace of a man standing on a railway track, checking his watch, thinking, *Well, at least I'm early for something.*

That was me, walking back toward the ring for Round Two, flicking gravel off my sleeve, tasting copper, feeling three distinct gazes stapled to my spine like wanted posters.

'Lose gracefully. Lose forgettably. Dodge two minutes, eat one clean hit, lie down, collect my median score, and go home.'

Simple plan. Elegant plan. A twelve-step speedrun strat with zero RNG elements.

Surely nothing could go wrong.

What a beautiful idiot I was.

---

Across the ring, Derrick Holt was cracking his neck.

I don't mean one crack. I mean a full cervical percussion performance — left, right, roll, settle — each pop audible from the gallery, each one purchased exclusively to make someone nervous.

That someone was me. He was getting his money's worth.

Derrick Holt. Third son of Baronet Holt. Six feet four inches of legacy admission and unresolved father issues. Mana Index 145. Level 9.

One hundred and forty-five. Sixteen thousand times my mana. Let me put that in perspective: if our mana reserves were cups of water, his was a soup pot and mine was the condensation on a cold glass.

"The provisional student," he said, rolling his shoulders. His practice sword looked like a toy in his hand, which was deeply unfair, because in my hand the same sword looked like I'd stolen it from an adult.

"Let's see how long you run this time."

'Statistically? About two minutes, Derrick. I have a plan and everything.'

"Good luck," I said, because my mouth has a default setting and that setting is 'customer service.'

He blinked. I'd confused him. Good. Confusion is free.

The System, gentle as a nurse reading terminal test results, unfolded before my eyes.

```
[System: Round 2 — Lucen Gray vs. Derrick Holt]
[Derrick Holt: Mana 145, Level 9, Specialty: Power Strikes]
[Note: Derrick Holt has a 78% chance of deliberately exceeding the tournament's force limit.]
[Note: The Evil Goddess recommends you actually dodge.]
[Note: Not your fake dodge. Your REAL dodge. You know the difference.]
```

'Seventy-eight percent.'

In Eschaton Hearts, any probability over thirty percent is a plot device in a trench coat. Seventy-eight percent isn't a chance. Seventy-eight percent is a *schedule.*

'"Let's see how long you run this time,"' I muttered. 'Original line. Definitely didn't rehearse it on the way over.'

Instructor Vance raised his hand at the edge of the ring. His eyes were on me. Specifically on me. Not on Derrick Holt, Level 9, Mana 145.

On *me.*

'Stop looking at me like that. I'm mediocre. I have paperwork proving it.'

"Begin."

---

Minute one.

I have run away from things my entire gaming career. In Eschaton Hearts, I once kited a mid-game boss around a fountain for forty-five real minutes because my build was wrong and I was too proud to respec. Running away is my one transferable skill.

Derrick Holt swung. I stepped back.

He swung again. I stepped left.

He lunged — committed, hips forward, textbook overextension — and I simply wasn't where the textbook said I'd be.

"NNGH—!"

From the gallery, a sound began to build. That horrible, wonderful sound. The sound of three hundred teenagers recognizing a rerun.

"*He's doing it again!*"

"No, I'm not," I said, mid-backpedal, to absolutely no one.

'I'm doing something completely different! Last time I ended a fight with a fancy knife trick! This time my plan is to get hit and fall down! These are different genres! One is a thriller, one is a documentary about my mediocrity!'

Derrick's face was doing a slow color transition. Pink. Red. A deep, aristocratic maroon that his father probably wore to dinner parties.

Each missed swing fed the beast. Each miss made the crowd louder, and each decibel of crowd made his next swing harder, which made my next dodge more impressive-looking, which made the crowd louder.

A feedback loop. A doom spiral. I was trapped in an engagement-farming algorithm and Derrick Holt was the content.

"Stand. STILL."

"No," I said.

The word came out flat. Honest. The first fully truthful thing I'd said all day.

The gallery laughed.

'That wasn't a JOKE. That was my survival doctrine!'

---

Minute two.

Right. Time to execute the strat.

The plan was simple: slow down by five percent. Open my left side. Let him land one clean, controlled, tournament-legal strike on a meaty, well-padded, non-essential part of me. Go down. Stay down. Collect my median score of "adequate." Go home and live in blessed obscurity until midterms.

I slowed down by five percent.

I opened my left side.

And Derrick Holt's eyes did something.

You know that micro-expression thing, when someone's pupils contract and their mouth flattens and the entire decision-making apparatus of their brain compresses into a single stupid impulse? I'd seen it a hundred times in the game. It's the face a miniboss makes right before the fight goes to phase two.

The game called that animation *Bloodthirst_Triggered.*

His practice sword flared.

Golden-white. Real, actual mana crawling up the blunt practice steel like sunrise in fast-forward, the enchantments on the blade screaming against the input, the air around the edge going wavy like summer asphalt.

Practice swords at Stellaris are charmed to prevent lethal damage. That charm was designed around *students.* It was not designed around a Level 9 noble pouring his entire wounded pride into one swing.

'That's not a tournament strike.'

'That's a hospital strike. That's a six-to-eight-weeks strike. That's a "Your Honor, my client" strike.'

From the assessors' platform — movement. Faculty leaning forward. Somebody's chair scraping.

Too slow. Everyone was too slow. Physics does not wait for authority.

The System went the color of arterial blood.

```
[WARNING.]
[Incoming strike exceeds tournament force parameters by approximately 340%.]
[Projected outcome: Severe cranial trauma.]
[Recommendation: Move or die.]
[Note: The Evil Goddess has stopped laughing.]
```

And into my chest, into the small terrified animal that lives behind my ribs, came a single screaming instruction, older than games, older than kingdoms, older than the academy and the goddess and every smug sword genius in the galaxy:

*MOVE OR DIE.*

---

I dropped.

Not a roll. Not the Ashenveil Retreat. There was no angle, no window, no technique — Derrick's strike filled the entire dodge space like water fills a glass.

So I did the only move in the tree that required zero proficiency, zero mana, and zero training.

I fell on the ground like a dropped sandwich.

The blade passed over my skull.

I felt it. I don't mean the wind of it — I mean the *heat* of it, a line of searing air where a layer of reality had been briefly deleted. A lock of my hair drifted down past my nose, cleanly severed, ends curled and singed.

'Haircut: acquired. Complimentary. Would not recommend.'

No time.

Derrick pivoted — credit where due, the man had reflexes — and the second strike came down at my sprawled body like a headsman's axe.

I rolled. Left. Pure panic, elbows over skull, the universal fetal geometry of every player who has ever been caught by a boss's enrage timer.

*WHAM.*

The strike cratered the arena floor where my ribcage had been. Sandstone chips peppered my face.

Then the crowd went quiet.

Not impressed-quiet. *This-is-wrong* quiet. The sound of three hundred people simultaneously realizing they were watching a disqualification happen in real time, and then, with dawning horror, realizing it didn't matter, because rules don't stop momentum.

Derrick swung again.

I scrambled backward. On my hands. And knees. Like a toddler fleeing bath time. With the desperate, floor-humping, dignity-free locomotion of a man whose only build path is called *Not Dying.*

*WHAM.* Right. *WHAM.* Left. *WHAM* — tuck, roll, something in my shoulder went *crunch,* keep moving—

I slithered under a horizontal arc on my stomach. I crab-walked through his guard. I dodged an overhead strike by tripping over my own ankle, which — since we're being truthful, and the Goddess is watching anyway — I had not planned, and which nonetheless saved my spine by four inches.

Thirty seconds.

For thirty seconds, Lucen Gray, Mana Index 0.009, less magical than a broom closet, crawled, flopped, rolled, and oozed across the Grand Arena of Stellaris Royal Academy while a Level 9 noble attempted to put him in traction.

And every single strike missed.

Every. Single. One.

Not by feet. Not by the clean, cinematic margins that look good in portfolios. By *millimeters.* By the width of a prayer. By exactly one frame, every frame, thirty frames a second, hitbox precision, muscle memory of a man who had died to worse than Derrick Holt four hundred times before lunch and taken notes.

Somewhere in the gallery, a student laughed — one bright, disbelieving bark — and then strangled it, because you could see the moment they understood.

This wasn't comedy.

Every one of those strikes was hospitalization. Every one of those dodges was a coin flip landing on its edge. And the boy on the floor wasn't performing.

He was *surviving something that should have already killed him.*

Up in the stands, standing now, hands white on the railing, in the midst of all of it, some small drowned corner of my awareness registered:

'Ashford is watching. Of course Ashford is watching. The protagonist's passive skill is witnessing character development.'

And two rows above him, red hair like a lit fuse, Rhea Valtor was gripping her practice sword hard enough that I could hear the leather creak from forty meters away.

And higher. Higher. A window of gold.

*WHAM.*

"HOLT."

Instructor Vance's voice didn't rise. It *detonated.*

"STAND. DOWN."

The world turned to honey.

A barrier slammed into existence between me and Derrick's descending blade — a wall of hard, humming, translucent force that drank the strike and shuddered like a struck bell. Two more faculty barriers collapsed inward, pinning Derrick's arms, and suddenly the biggest boy in our class was a statue with seizure-inducing mana and nowhere to put it.

The practice sword clattered on stone.

Silence.

My cheek was pressed flat to the arena floor. I had one eye open. Sand. Blood in my mouth again — I'd bitten through the inside of my cheek somewhere around strike six. My palms were raw, skinned pink, little constellation of gravel embedded in them.

'Dodge count: flawless.'

'Dignity count: zero. F. Z-Minus. The grading scale does not reach.'

"...Match — stopped," the referee said, voice cracking on the sandpaper of his own disbelief. He swallowed. "Derrick Holt — disqualified. Excessive force. Lucen Gray wins by default."

There was no cheer.

That was almost worse. A cheer I understood. Cheers I'd had. What I got instead was a wave of sound that was somehow more alarming — a low, spreading, *buzzing* multiplication of whisper, three hundred students all talking at once in the grammar of rumors being born.

'I won by crawling.'

'I won. By crawling.'

'Two matches. One ended by falling down, one ended by sprinting away on all fours. My competitive record at the most prestigious academy in the Empire is 2-0, achieved entirely via floor.'

Somewhere far above, past the arena roof, past the indifferent clouds, past several layers of cosmic bureaucracy, a notification window bloomed over my vision, small and smug and faintly trembling.

```
[The Evil Goddess would like the record to show:]
[She recommends dodging ONE time.]
[You chose to interpret 'dodge' as a THIRTY-SECOND SLAPSTICK ROUTINE.]
[You have dodged a homicide successfully.]
[You have also dodged 'obscurity' permanently.]
[One of these was the goal. She will give you one guess which.]
```

'I'm aware,' I thought, into the blood-flavored dirt. 'Thank you. Noted. Filed.'

Two healers reached me. Gentle hands. Light, that warm green institutional light. Somewhere behind them, Instructor Vance was speaking to Derrick Holt in the special quiet voice men use when the shouting part is already over and what comes next is *paperwork.*

I stared at the arena ceiling.

Round Two: complete.

Plan: *compost.*

---

They made us wait while the panel deliberated.

The remaining matches ground on. I didn't watch them. I sat on the bench outside the ring with my hands wrapped in gauze and my soul wrapped in quiet hysteria, watching bracket after bracket of normal students have normal fights with normal outcomes.

Lucas Ashford won in fourteen seconds. Clean. Beautiful. Textbook protagonist form, textbook protagonist restraint. The crowd sighed like a satisfied cat.

'See? THAT is how you make a reputation at an academy, you freakishly competent golden boy. Short, decisive, photogenic. Not like SOME people, who've now appeared on the arena floor more times than most first-years appear at all, and both times horizontally.'

"Gray." An attendant. "The panel will see you."

---

The faculty chamber was exactly as intimidating as its architectural budget promised.

Vaulted ceiling. Long table. Seven people behind it, arranged in ascending order of how much my life was their business. Instructor Vance at the near end, arms crossed, face carefully blank in the specific way of a man choosing his words in advance. Instructor Kaelin beside him, quill parked, eyes doing calculations.

And at the center — a woman I did not know, in the deep blue of the Academy's Assessment Directorate, with reading glasses and the aura of someone who has personally ended four hundred academic careers and remembers the interesting ones.

'Chair of the panel. Final boss of the arc. *Of course.*'

"Lucen Gray," she said. Not a question.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Stand there." A mark on the floor. Naturally there was a mark on the floor. I stood on the mark on the floor.

Silence. Paper. The scratch of Kaelin's quill. They let it stretch — an interrogation technique older than the Empire, older probably than the gods: *say nothing, and let the guilty fill the silence with their own confession.*

Unfortunately for them, I am Korean in soul, unemployed in spirit, and have sat through seven-hour speedrun marathons on expired energy drinks. Silence holds no terrors for me. I contemplated the crack in the ceiling. It looked like the Sword Coast.

Finally, the Chair set down her pen.

"Two matches," she said. "Two victories. Zero conventional techniques. Zero demonstrated offense. Zero recorded mana expenditure. In the first, you ended the bout with a disarm performed from a prone position using a retention knife you were not briefed to carry. In the second, you evaded —" she glanced down, read the number with visible effort, "*—eleven consecutive strikes,* four of which exceeded sanctioned force by a factor of three, sustaining no injury beyond self-inflicted abrasions."

'Self-inflicted. Noted. Even the Empire blames me for my own face.'

"You understand," she said, "that this file is unusual."

"Yes, ma'am."

"At no point in either bout did you demonstrate the ability to attack."

"...Yes, ma'am."

That hung there. Vance's mouth twitched. Just once. A man swallowing an entire paragraph.

"Explain your combat philosophy," the Chair said.

'My combat philosophy.'

Here it was. The moment. Every speedrunner dreads it: the devs asking you, on stream, to please explain your route.

I chose my words the way a man crossing a frozen lake chooses his steps.

"Ma'am. I have a Mana Index of 0.009. Sword faculty — I lose. Spell faculty — I lose. Strength, reach, speed, endurance — I lose. In any fight where I try to *win,* I've already lost before it starts. The only fight I can actually compete in is the fight that hasn't hit me yet." I looked at my gauze-wrapped hands. "So I don't fight the opponent. I fight the attack."

Silence again. But a different breed of it.

Kaelin had stopped writing.

The Chair took off her reading glasses. Cleaned them. Put them back on. Delay tactics. I had made the final boss of the Assessment Directorate *cast Cleanliness* to buy time.

"The panel's verdict," she said at last, "is as follows."

I stood very straight.

"Lucen Gray demonstrates extraordinary evasive capability, advanced pattern recognition, and a defensive combat philosophy unprecedented in first-year candidates." A beat. "However. His offensive capabilities remain undemonstrated. Given the anomalous circumstances of both his matches, this panel cannot determine his true combat ceiling."

'Good. Excellent. Median-ish. Forgettable-adjacent —'

"Provisional enrollment: sustained."

My knees nearly did the thing. The gratitude thing. I locked them.

"Effective immediately: special monitoring status. The subject will be re-evaluated at midterms, under *expanded assessment criteria.*"

'Expanded assessment criteria.'

'That means they're going to build a bigger microscope. For me. A bespoke, artisanal, handcrafted microscope, with my name engraved on it, in a font.'

"Do you understand, Cadet Gray?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Dismissed."

I walked out of the chamber on steady legs, closed the door behind me with tremendous gentleness, walked ten paces down the corridor, and only then permitted my back to slide down the wall until I was sitting on extremely expensive marble with my gauze-mittens in my lap.

I passed.

I stayed.

I survived.

'And I am now officially classified as the single most interesting mystery at Stellaris Royal Academy.'

The System arrived like a florist delivering to a funeral — enormous, cheerful, and carrying something nobody wanted.

```
[First Evaluation Exam — COMPLETE.]
[Final Ranking: 19th of 38.]
[Median. Congratulations. You aimed for invisible and landed on 'mysterious.']
[Note: 'Mysterious' is 'interesting' wearing a trench coat. 'Interesting' is how routes start.]
[The Evil Goddess has lit a candle.]
[It is not a nice candle.]
```

'One problem at a time,' I told it. 'Please. Just one at a time.'

The System did not answer.

The System, I had learned, only ever answered when it was too late.

---

---

The arena emptied the way all great events do — slowly at first, then all at once, laughter and argument spilling down the tunnels and out into the gold of the late afternoon, carrying rumor in both arms.

Iris Aurelia did not leave.

She stood at the gallery rail where she had stood for twenty minutes, alone now, the last figure in a vast stone bowl, her guard waiting at a distance they had learned — recently, abruptly, and without being told — to maintain.

Below her, the arena floor was a mess. Craters. Scorch lines. Two distinct drag trails, one at each end of the ring, where a boy with a Mana Index lower than a training dummy had twice ended official matches lying in the dirt.

She looked at the nearer one. The crawl marks. You could still read them, actually read them, like calligraphy — here, a desperate roll; here, the long slither under the horizontal cut; here, four parallel smears where hands and knees had moved faster than hands and knees should.

A boy who broke measuring crystals by existing near them.

A boy who read dead languages the way other boys read serial novels.

A boy who knew a dead empress's tea, and mapped passages the Empire believed only the Imperial family knew, in a notebook, in pencil, like it was a grocery list.

A boy who fought — if you could even call it fighting — like water runs downhill. With no pride in it. No beauty in it. No stake in being seen as anything but *unhit.*

And the look on his face, afterward. When Vance's barrier came down and it was over —

Iris had spent her life in rooms where every face was a policy. She wore a mask so well she had begun, privately, in the small hours, to suspect there was nothing underneath it anymore.

But that boy had looked up from the arena floor with dust in his hair and blood on his palms, wearing an expression so unguarded, so simple, so blindingly *human* — relief, pure relief, the relief of a creature that had been allowed, once more, to keep breathing —

And something in her chest had cracked.

She knew the crack. She'd felt it once before. She was six years old, there was rain, and a kind-faced chamberlain knelt and told her it was alright to cry now, if she needed to.

Princesses don't cry. So she hadn't.

She'd never found out what was on the other side of that moment.

Until today, apparently, when it decided to resurface over a filthy, crawling, unkillable commoner with a lying mouth and terrified, honest eyes.

"Iris," her escort murmured, careful as a man approaching a bent blade. "The carriages—"

"In a moment."

She looked out over the wreckage of the First Evaluation Exam. His exam. His examination of her, really, though he didn't know it yet.

He knew things. He was hiding everything. Those two facts could not coexist forever.

"Lucen Gray," Iris Aurelia said softly, to the empty arena, to the drag marks, to the Empire entire.

"You're going to tell me everything."

---

Somewhere above the sky, in a place that was technically not a place, a goddess who had watched ten thousand bad endings like a woman rewatching her favorite tragedies got up, walked to her little shelf of small flames, and lit another candle.

Then she looked at the first candle. Looked at the second. Looked at the trajectory implied.

And began, very quietly, laughing — because there was nothing else left to do.
