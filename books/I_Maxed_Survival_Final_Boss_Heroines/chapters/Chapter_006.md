# Chapter 6: The Princess Asks a Question

The file was three pages long.

Iris Aurelia, third heir to the Solaris Throne, eldest daughter of Emperor Valeran the Seventh, holder of the Dawn's Mandate and seventeen separate military commendations she hadn't asked for, held three pages in her hand and read them for the fourth time.

She had reports on her desk that would determine grain tariffs for two million people. She had a border memo from the Eastern March with the words *urgent* underlined twice.

She was reading a three-page enrollment file at five in the morning instead.

---

*LUCEN GRAY. Provisional student. Clause 77-B.*

*Sponsor: Vice-Headmistress Voss.*

*Origin: Port town of Milhaven (unverified). Sole survivor of a bandit attack on the western road.*

*Family: None. Records: None. Mana certification: None.*

*Age: Seventeen, approximated.*

---

That was it.

That was the entire document.

Iris set it down on her desk with the sort of precision people used when placing evidence at a crime scene.

"Sera."

Her attendant appeared the way she always did — silently, from a direction Iris had failed to anticipate. After six years together, Iris had stopped being startled by this and started being quietly impressed.

"Your Highness."

"You looked at this yesterday. Your assessment."

Sera tilted her head, hands folded. She had a gift for making her opinions sound like weather reports — mild, inoffensive, delivered with the confidence of someone who would absolutely survive being ignored.

"A lucky commoner, Your Highness. Probably a farmer's boy. The road attack waivers aren't uncommon — Clause 77-B exists precisely for orphans with nowhere else to go. Voss sponsors a handful every decade. It's her hobby."

"Her hobby."

"The woman collects lost causes, Your Highness. Last year it was a boy who claimed he could talk to goats."

"Could he?"

"No, Your Highness. But the goats did seem to listen."

Iris almost smiled. Almost.

She picked up the file again and tapped one line with a single manicured finger.

"There were no bandit attacks on the western road in the last month."

Sera went very still.

"I checked the Imperial road patrol reports. All four districts. The last confirmed bandit activity on that road was fifty-two days before this boy's stated date of attack — and it was three merchants losing six crates of dried fish, not a massacre of travelers."

"...Perhaps the log was delayed."

"Logs are *always* delayed, Sera. They've never once been *wrong*." Iris leaned back in her chair. "So either Captain Mendel has been falsifying patrol records for four weeks, which would be treason, or—"

"Or the boy lied about the bandits."

"Or *someone* lied about them for him."

The dawn light crawled across the desk. Sera was quiet for a moment, the way she got when recalculating her weather.

"You know this how, exactly?"

"I read things."

"Most people read things, Your Highness. They don't cross-reference a provisional student's enrollment date against four districts of Imperial patrol logs before breakfast."

Iris said nothing.

Because the truth was embarrassing, so she would not say it aloud: there was no reason for this. There was no tactical value in a nameless commoner boy. She had a war council in nine days, a marriage proposal from the Second Prince of Velaire that she needed to deflect without causing a trade war, and a father who communicated mostly through imperial decrees these days.

And yet.

She had watched a mana assessment crystal — a *military-grade* assessment crystal, calibrated to measure the output of field generals — crack like a struck bell when a seventeen-year-old nobody put his hand on it.

She had spent thirteen years learning to read people the way other people read ledgers.

This boy did not read like a page.

This boy read like a page with the words *burned off.*

The walk to the morning lecture hall took eleven minutes.

Iris had timed it. She timed everything.

Eleven minutes, during which she passed ninety-four students, twelve staff, and one gardener, and during which exactly zero of them met her eyes.

They bowed instead. Deeply. Quickly. The younger students flattened themselves against walls like she was a passing avalanche. Two second-years abruptly discovered urgent business in a courtyard they had been walking *away* from.

The whispers followed at the regulation distance of fifteen meters.

*—the Dawn Tyrant—*

*—don't look at her, you idiot, don't look—*

*—Lord Castellan's boy, last spring, she didn't even blink—*

Iris kept her face smooth and walked at her measured pace.

She never corrected them.

What was she supposed to say? *He was smuggling siege components to the Redshire insurgency, and I was twelve, and the decree came from my father's desk, and I only read it aloud because he wanted to see if I would vomit, and I didn't, which was a mistake, because he was so proud.*

*I didn't sleep for eleven days.*

*Nobody asks if I enjoyed it.*

The thought surfaced, as it always did, and she slid it back into its drawer, as she always did.

She had a great many drawers.

"Your Highness." Vice-Headmistress Voss rose from behind her desk like a flower opening — smooth, practiced, arranged to be admired. "To what does my humble office owe the dawn's own light?"

Iris disliked Voss the way one dislikes a mirror that shows you something flattering.

"Your report on the mana assessment incident," Iris said, taking the offered seat. "The cracked crystal."

"A malfunction, surely."

"The crystals don't malfunction."

"Then the manufacturer will be hearing from my solicitors." Voss smiled. She had an excellent smile. Iris had catalogued it over three years: the smile reached her eyes when she was amused, thinned when she was angry, and did exactly *neither* when she was decided.

The smile did neither now.

"The boy," Iris said.

"Lucen." Voss's tone went soft and warm, like a blanket. "Yes. A charity case. Bandit survivor, no family, no prospects. Clause 77-B gives me discretion to sponsor the hopeless now and then. It keeps the treasury auditors from thinking I'm *entirely* useless."

"There was no bandit attack on that road."

Voss poured tea. She took her time about it.

"Then he lied about his past," she said at last, "to gain admission to a school that would feed him. If that's the worst crime on his record, Your Highness, I daresay the Empire will endure."

That was not an answer.

And Voss — Voss of the *excellent* smile, Voss who had deflected the Crown Prince's inquiries about the southern budget for forty minutes using nothing but honeyed irrelevance — did not deflect without *reason.* Deflection was expensive. Voss spent it like money.

She was spending it on a provisional student.

Iris filed that away.

"The crystal," Iris pressed. "Your professional opinion."

Voss's hand paused on the teapot for exactly one fourth of a second.

"Ask Instructor Kaelin," she said pleasantly. "He was there."

A dismissal, perfumed.

Iris stood.

"Thank you for the tea, Vice-Headmistress."

"But you haven't drunk it, Your Highness."

"No," said Iris. "I haven't."

Instructor Kaelin received her in the assessment hall, standing beside the ruined crystal as though keeping vigil at a deathbed.

He was one of maybe four people in the academy who looked at her with neither fear nor calculation. He looked at her like a puzzle that might someday be *finished,* which she respected.

"Give me your honest assessment, Instructor. Not the report version."

Kaelin was quiet for a long time. He was a man who treated silence as a professional tool.

"Two possibilities," he said finally. "The first is that the boy has mana reserves beyond our measurement capacity. The crystal didn't fail to *read* him. It was *overloaded.* Like weighing a mountain on a merchant's scale."

"And the second?"

Kaelin looked at the cracked shell of the crystal.

"The second is that whatever is inside that boy is something my instruments were never built to see at all. Not a mage's mana. Not a beast's aura. Something... adjacent to a category we don't have."

"Is he dangerous?"

"Your Highness." Kaelin rubbed the back of his neck, and there was something in his face she rarely saw there — genuine bafflement, almost embarrassed. "I've stood in front of that crystal with war mages who could boil a river. It has never so much as flickered. That boy looked at the crater he'd made like *he* wanted to apologize to *it.*"

"...I see."

"So. No. I don't know if he's dangerous." A pause. "But I've decided to stop being *certain* of things near him. It's less exhausting."

Morning classes passed the way morning classes always passed.

Iris sat in her assigned seat — first row, because no one dared sit behind her and she refused to let the empty space make her look *shunned.* She took perfect notes. She answered when called upon. She was aware, the entire time, of exactly one presence in the room behaving wrong.

Lucen Gray did not sit in the back.

The back was what normal students assumed was the invisible seat. It wasn't. Any instructor's lecturing sightline swept the back wall constantly; it was where attention *lingered.*

No — Lucen Gray sat second row from the front, dead center-ish, one seat off the geometric midpoint, beside another student broad enough to partially block the sightline from the door.

Iris knew this seat's properties because on her first day at this academy, three years ago, before anyone had shown her to a seat, she had spent fourteen minutes mapping every sightline, every exit, and every blind angle in the room, and she had selected her own position knowing exactly what each square meter of floor *meant.*

That seat was the single least observed position in the classroom.

A person sitting there could be looked *at* without ever being *seen.*

Coincidence, surely. A lucky commoner. A farmer's boy, choosing blind.

And on day one, when she had entered the room, that boy had looked up — directly at her, full-face, unguarded —

— and then *looked away.* Deliberately. Not with terror, not with awe. With the specific, practiced smoothness of a man choosing to become invisible *on purpose.*

In seventeen years of life — as princess, as prodigy, as the executioner's read-aloud voice, as the Dawn Tyrant, as a girl — no one had ever looked away from Iris Aurelia *by choice.*

People looked away because they were afraid.

People looked away because they were told.

He had looked away the way you put down a weapon you have decided not to use.

It had bothered her for three days.

It bothered her enough that she was now standing in the eastern courtyard after classes, on a route she had calculated from his walking patterns, holding absolutely nothing in her hands and feeling — for the first time in perhaps four years — like she was lying to herself about why she was somewhere.

*Reconnaissance,* she told herself.

*A Category A anomaly requires direct observation.*

*This is diligence.*

The little voice at the back of her skull, the one that sounded suspiciously like her mother, said: *You have on your good hairpins.*

*Shut up,* she told the voice, with tremendous fondness and total authority.

He came around the corner of the colonnade at exactly the time she had predicted.

Walking quickly. Head down. A worn leather satchel clutched against his ribs like a shield — actually clutched, the way men clutched things in ambush territory.

Iris stepped into his path.

He stopped so fast his shoes scraped.

Up close — *properly* up close, for the first time — she ran her assessment the way she always did, in the three heartbeats between someone noticing her and someone deciding what face to wear.

Build: unremarkable. No martial calluses she could see. Breath: elevated, but he'd been walking fast.

Eyes: gray, ordinary, unremarkable —

— and wide with fear.

But.

Iris had been feared her entire life. She had a taxonomy for it, memorized and indexed. Fear of her *power* flickered in the gut and showed in the hands. Fear of her *title* lived in the knees and the bow-deep spine. Fear of her *reputation* — the Castellan whisper-fear — came with averted eyes and a desire to be elsewhere, fast.

His fear was none of these.

It was *specific.* Sharpened to a point. Aimed at her, at her *face,* like she was a word in a book he had already read to the end.

As if he knew something about her that she had not yet done.

*That's not possible,* said the cooler part of her mind.

*It looks possible,* said the part that had read four districts of road logs before breakfast.

"Lucen Gray," she said. "You're the provisional student."

"...Yes, Your Highness." The bow was textbook. The voice was not. Most people addressed her from inside a sort of damp fog of deference. He sounded like a man reading his own charges at trial.

"Your crystal broke."

"It was... a malfunction, I think."

"Crystals don't malfunction."

A pause.

And here — here was the moment. She had seen this pause a hundred times. This was the pause where they flattered, or pleaded, or marshaled excuses, or tried to be impressive, or tried to be *nothing.* Everyone did something.

He did nothing.

He just stood there — terrified, visibly, sweat at his temple, heart hammering so hard she could see his collar move — and he looked her in the eye, and he said:

"Then I don't have an explanation, Your Highness. I'm sorry."

I'm *sorry.*

He's *sorry.*

For breaking a crystal. As if *that* were the offense. As if he were a guest who had cracked a teacup and not a boy who had just invalidated the measuring instruments of the Imperial magical establishment, and the entire scaffold of categories Iris used to understand what people *were.*

She stared at him.

He held it. He was afraid — she could count six separate tells of fear on him and she stopped counting only because it felt obscene — and still, his gray eyes did not slide away.

The last person who had looked her in the eye while visibly afraid had been her father.

It had been the morning he told her that her mother was dead, and he had needed her to see his face while he said it, so that she would know that even the Emperor could be made small, and that strength meant standing upright *anyway.*

Lucen Gray looked at her like that.

Like standing upright was something he was doing on purpose, at a cost.

Iris walked back toward the palace wing.

The whispers parted around her as they always did. *The Dawn Tyrant. Don't meet her eyes.*

She thought about gray eyes that hadn't looked away.

She thought about *I'm sorry,* said like a confession instead of an excuse.

She thought — briefly, traitorously, in the voice that sounded like her mother — about how long it had been since anyone had stood in front of her and simply been a *person,* afraid and upright, rather than a mirror showing her what she was.

The pressure sat on her shoulders as always — the court, the factions, the marriage offers piling on her father's desk like heads on a block, the whisper she had overheard at last winter's gala and had never told a soul: *they're wondering when she'll burn, like her grandmother did, better it be somewhere far from the capital.*

She had read her own history. Her line had a habit of *sunrises* — of women who shone too hot and were mourned from a safe distance.

She had decided long ago she would rather be feared than pitied.

But feared was a very cold thing to be at seventeen, and a boy with empty files and terrified eyes had just looked at her and — inexplicably, unforgivably, unforgettably —

— *not known what she was.*

"...Interesting," Iris Aurelia said, quietly, to no one at all.

It was the most dangerous word in her vocabulary.
