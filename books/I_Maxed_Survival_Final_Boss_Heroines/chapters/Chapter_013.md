# Chapter 13: Dawn Sparring with the Ashen Sword

Dawn came to the Eastern Training Grounds the way Rhea Valtor did — early, silent, and without excuses.

The mist lay across the packed dirt like a held breath. Frost clung to the wooden benches lining the practice rings. No birds yet. No students. No one anywhere, which was exactly the point.

Rhea had been here for forty minutes.

She had woken at four, as she had woken at four every day since she was nine years old. Her body no longer consulted her about it. The alarm bell of memory simply rang, and she rose.

The night the Imperial soldiers came for her father, they hadn't knocked.

Neither did she. Ever again. On doors, on mornings, on anything.

While she waited, she ran forms.

First form. Second form. Third through seventh. Her practice blade cut the fog into ribbons that re-formed behind her, patient as grief.

By the time the horizon went from black to slate, she had completed the full sequence twice.

By the time it went from slate to gray, she had completed it twice more.

She did not think while doing forms. That was the entire purpose of forms. The body moved, the blade sang, and the thing in her chest — the one she kept wrapped in seven years of iron discipline — stayed perfectly, reliably frozen.

Then she heard footsteps on the gravel.

Wrong footsteps.

She knew it before she turned around. The cadence was off — uneven weight distribution, slight heel-drag on the left, the pace of someone who had run most of the way and was currently negotiating with his lungs.

Rhea finished the ninth form before turning. She did not show anticipation. She had not shown anticipation since she was nine.

Lucen Gray stood at the edge of the training grounds, hunched forward, one hand braced on his knee.

He had dark circles under his eyes the color of old bruises. His hair looked like he'd wrestled a pillow and lost. He was five minutes late, breathing like a man who had sprinted here from a personal emergency, and he was carrying his practice sword—

Rhea blinked.

*Carrying* was generous. He held the practice sword at arm's length. Extended. Away from his body. The way one might carry a dead rat by the tail, or a letter containing catastrophic news, or a venomous snake one had been legally obligated to transport.

He was even slightly turned away from it. As if not looking at the sword would prevent the sword from doing anything.

Rhea's first thought arrived fully formed, in the flat, precise language she used for internal situation reports:

*This is the person who used the Ashenveil Retreat?*

She had seen the footage. A first-year had managed to record part of the bloody entrance examination on a projection crystal, and the clip had circulated through the noble study halls like a disease. She had watched it eleven times, alone, in the dark.

The boy in the footage had crossed thirty meters of broken terrain without being touched once. Every retreat step a perfect mirror of her father's.

The boy in front of her was staring at his own sword like it had personally offended his family.

"You're late," Rhea said.

"Sorry." Lucen straightened, wheezed once, managed: "I overslept. Which is insane, because I barely slept. Which is also why I overslept. The logic is airtight and I hate it."

Rhea looked at him for a long moment.

She had prepared for several possibilities regarding Lucen Gray. Trained operative. Grieving survivor's apprentice. Political plant. Some hidden scion of a disgraced military house.

She had not prepared for... this.

"Warm up on your own," she said. "Two circuits of the grounds. Then show me the footwork."

He jogged off with the physical enthusiasm of a man being escorted to a tax audit.

Rhea watched him round the first corner of the field and immediately checked her sword grip, which she had apparently tightened.

*Calm,* she told herself.

*He's a lead. Nothing more.*

---

Two circuits took him an embarrassing amount of time, but she noted what mattered: he didn't stop. His lungs were clearly filing formal complaints, his face had gone the color of bad porcelain, and still he kept the pace of a man who knew that stopping meant starting over.

Stubbornness. That, she could respect.

"Enough," she called. "Now. The footwork. Show me."

Lucen wiped his face with his sleeve, muttered something she didn't catch — it sounded like *"dodge mechanics, here we go"* — and set into a stance.

And Rhea Valtor forgot how to breathe for three full seconds.

*Wrong word. Not stance. Flow.*

He didn't stand in the Ashenveil Retreat. He *fell into* it — weight settling, hips dropping, the rear foot angling at exactly forty-seven degrees the way her father had drilled into her on the estate grounds when she was six years old and could barely reach his knee.

Then he moved.

First step: the Ashen Slip. Lateral, seamless, gravity-lean instead of a push.

Second: the Veil Turn. Pivoting on the ball of the foot, spine never breaking its line.

Third: the Ghost Step. A retreat that closed distance without ever advancing — the maneuver her father had invented himself, never written down, taught to exactly seven people in his entire life.

Lucen did all three in sequence.

Then he did them again, in reverse.

Then he chained them together through a pattern that Rhea had spent *eleven months* reconstructing from fragmented childhood memories, and he did it — she counted — *without a single deviation.*

Every angle. Every pivot. Every micro-transfer of weight from heel to toe at the precise instant a counterstrike would land in a real duel.

Geometrically flawless.

Not trained flawless. *Perfect* flawless. The kind of flawless that didn't come from ten thousand repetitions in a salle. The kind of flawless that came from knowing the answer before the question existed.

Rhea's hand had gone to the hilt of her own practice sword without her permission.

*That's my father's.*

*That's my father's and he's doing it like reading off a page that shouldn't exist.*

"Enough," she said. Her voice came out controlled. Good. "Now defend."

She raised her practice blade and delivered a single, slow test strike — chest height, telegraphed, moving at maybe thirty percent speed. A strike a moderately trained child could parry.

Everything above Lucen's ankles collapsed like scaffolding in an earthquake.

His shoulders shot up to his ears. He flinched *backward* — not with the retreat, just... backward, flailing, the wrong direction entirely. His grip went white-knuckled, knuckles stacked wrong, thumb on the wrong side. He opened his mouth and breathed through it like a drowning man, which wrecked everything the feet had built. His arms crossed in front of his face in a way that would have gotten him killed three times before breakfast against anyone with intent.

The practice blade tapped him gently on the forehead.

He blinked at it. "Ow."

"Barely touched you."

"Still counted it."

Rhea lowered the sword.

She stood there, and she did something she almost never did in a combat assessment: she stood in genuine, complete, system-wide confusion.

Because what she had just witnessed was impossible.

Her mind assembled the report automatically, the way her instructors had drilled her:

*Subject possesses perfect theoretical knowledge of advanced defensive technique with zero practical combat foundation. Execution of lower-body maneuvering: master-class. Execution of upper-body combat fundamentals: nonexistent. Breathing: catastrophic. Grip: catastrophic. Reactions: civilian.*

*Conclusion: This should be impossible.*

You cannot perfect footwork without sparring. You cannot perfect sparring-level footwork while having the sword grip of a man holding a venomous snake at arm's length. The two halves of the fighter in front of her could not coexist.

Unless.

*Unless he was never sparred.*

*Unless he was only ever shown.*

The thought arrived quietly and set up residence like it owned the place.

A survivor. One of the Seventh. Broken in the fall — burned, half-crippled, a man whose legs could no longer do what his memory still carried perfectly. Hiding somewhere. A farm, a mountain village, the bottom of an empire that had erased his name.

And a boy.

A boy watching. Day after day. A dying man demonstrating the Ashenveil Retreat one more time — *again, boy, eyes on the feet* — because it was the only thing left of three thousand soldiers, and he couldn't let it die with him, and he couldn't spar, couldn't teach past demonstration, could only *show*—

Years of observation without practice.

A legacy transferred through sight alone.

Rhea had been carrying a frozen lake in her chest for seven years. She maintained it the way monks maintain a vow — daily, deliberately, without exception.

Something in it cracked.

Not audibly. Not visibly. She would not permit that.

But inside, where no one had been allowed since the funeral she wasn't permitted to attend, something shifted a single half-step sideways, and she had to look away from Lucen Gray for three full seconds to get her breathing back under her own control.

He was watching her. Wary. The way small animals watch weather.

"Who taught you the footwork?" she asked.

His face did a thing. Several things. She catalogued them: fear, then something like exhausted resignation, then — absurdly — guilt.

"I told you," he said. "A book—"

"Stop lying."

It came out flat. She didn't do anger. Anger was fuel spent on nothing, and she had a fixed budget. But the word came out with weight anyway.

"Your feet know the Ashenveil Retreat the way my feet know it," she said. "Perfectly. Reflexively. At the level of bone. That is not a *book.* No book teaches that. I've read every surviving manual on the Seventh Legion's technique. I have memorized them. None of them contain the Ghost Step, because my father never wrote it down."

She took one step closer. He did not retreat. Interesting — the footwork was reflexive, but so was his refusal to use it on *her.*

"And yet you've never been in a real fight. Your hands don't know what your feet know. So tell me the truth."

Lucen opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Opened it again.

And Rhea watched him try — genuinely, visibly *try* — to produce an explanation. She saw him search for one the way a man searches a room for an exit. She saw the exact moment he confirmed that no exit existed.

"I can't," he said finally. Quietly. "I know how it looks. I know exactly how it looks. But I can't explain it, and I'm — " he exhaled, " — really, really sorry about that."

He couldn't explain it.

She saw the truth of that in his eyes — a strange, exhausted sincerity that didn't match anything in her threat assessment training. Not the blankness of a man reciting a cover story. The frustration of a man who knew the actual answer would sound worse than any lie.

The story her mind had constructed solidified into near-certainty.

*A dying soldier. A hidden place. A boy who was only allowed to watch.*

*Someone survived. Someone from the Seventh survived long enough to pass it on.*

Seven years. Seven years of one confirmed fact: **nobody survived.** The Empire's report was absolute. The Seventh Legion, annihilated to a man. Her father, executed as a traitor in front of the empty armor stands of men who were already dead.

And here was a boy whose feet said otherwise.

Rhea turned away. Walked three steps to the bench. Set her practice blade down with a precision that took actual willpower.

*Do not hope.*

*Hope is a debt you pay later.*

*Hope—*

She looked back at him. He was standing awkwardly in the middle of the grass, holding his sword wrong, looking like a man waiting to be told whether his execution had been rescheduled.

He was terrified of her. That much was obvious. And yet he'd shown up at dawn after two hours of sleep, run two circuits until his lungs failed, and performed her father's ghost in front of a stranger knowing full well what it might cost him.

*Why?*

*What does he get out of this?*

She couldn't find an angle. That unsettled her more than the footwork had.

Across the grounds, in the long silence, Rhea made her decision.

She was aware, distantly, that decisions made in the presence of that crack in the ice were not to be trusted. She made it anyway.

"I'll train you," she said.

Lucen's head came up. "What?"

"Upper body. Blade work. Breathing. Real combat." She picked her sword back up and faced him. "In exchange, you show me every piece of the Ashenveil form you know. All of it. Even the pieces you don't realize you know."

Lucen stared at her.

Then he said, very carefully, in the tone of a man confirming the terms of a contract with a devil he'd already signed with: "...Every piece. And you train me before the exam, so I don't die."

"Yes."

"You know there was never a survivor, right?" he said suddenly. "Whatever you're thinking — whatever story this—" he gestured vaguely at his own feet, "—is telling you. I just. I feel like I should say that up front. For. Moral reasons."

Rhea looked at him for a long moment.

"There was a book," she said dryly, letting him have the lie. "Raise your sword."

He raised his sword.

His grip was wrong. His knuckles were wrong. His shoulder had crept up toward his ear like it was seeking shelter.

Rhea set down her own blade, stepped in behind him, and reached around to his hand.

She adjusted his fingers one at a time. Thumb here. Loose, but not slack — *hold it like a bird, my father used to say, firm enough it cannot fly away, gentle enough you do not crush it* — her fingers working over his with seven years of solitary practice and eighteen years of inherited precision.

Her fingers were calloused, scarred at the second knuckle, and very sure.

His hand was shaking.

Not from the cold. The morning was cold, but not like that.

*He's afraid of me,* she registered. *Of course he is. He's a civilian-shaped person standing within arm's reach of the Valtor girl, alone, at dawn. Anyone sensible would shake.*

Something about that bothered her disproportionately. She filed it under *irrelevant* and let her grip linger half a second longer than biomechanics required.

Four seconds, contact lasted, in total.

"Now," she said, stepping back. "We begin."

---

She went easy on him.

By her standards. Which meant that over the course of one hour, Lucen Gray was tapped, checked, swept, corrected, and gently introduced to the ground seventeen times.

The strangest thing happened halfway through, though.

She drilled him on breathing — *in through the nose on the retreat, out through the mouth on the reset* — and on the nineteenth repetition, his shoulders finally dropped, and for exactly one exchange his entire body aligned.

Feet: perfect Ashenveil.

Hands: adequate.

Breathing: correct.

One exchange out of two hundred. But in that exchange, Lucen Gray looked, for a single heartbeat, like the ghost of the Seventh Legion standing in the morning mist.

Rhea's next strike stopped a quarter-inch from his throat on instinct alone.

She hadn't intended to stop. Her body had simply... declined to complete a strike against that silhouette.

"...Time," she said, lowering the blade. Her voice was even. Her pulse was not, and she assigned that to exertion, the way one assigns blame to a convenient suspect.

Lucen collapsed onto the bench like a puppet with cut strings.

"You're," he gasped, "scary good. I knew that. Intellectually. Now it's in my bones. Literally. You've rearranged them."

"You'll be sore tomorrow."

"I'm sore *now.* I'm sore in advance. I feel pain from Thursday that hasn't happened yet."

She almost — almost — let the corner of her mouth move. She suppressed it the way she suppressed everything, out of habit older than her grief.

"Same time. Every morning until the exam."

"Every morning," he repeated hollowly. "Yes. Of course. That was always going to be my fate."

He gathered himself, retrieved his sword — arm's length, venomous snake protocol resumed — and limped away across the fog-thinned grounds, muttering. She caught one fragment on the wind, in a tone of profound resignation:

"...Cassian Vale lasted six bouts and they called him *dishonorable*, they don't know anything, they weren't *there—*"

Then the mist took him.

Rhea Valtor stood alone in the empty training grounds exactly the way she had stood in them an hour before.

Everything was the same.

She walked to the bench. Sat down. And then, in a motion that belonged to no form in any manual, she pressed her hands against her face.

*He is a connection to my father's legacy.*

*Nothing more.*

She turned her own hand over. Looked at the calluses. Four seconds of contact, and her fingers still carried the memory of it — warmth, and a tremor that hadn't been cold.

*It has been seven years,* said a voice in her that she did not acknowledge and could not shut off, *since you touched another person voluntarily.*

"That is irrelevant data," she said out loud, to the empty field.

The field did not agree or disagree. Mist moved through the training rings like it was looking for someone.

*It is not fine.*

She knew this with the same certainty with which she knew all seventeen places she had struck him. Rhea Valtor's emotional architecture was a dam — she'd designed it herself, age nine, in a single night, while soldiers sorted her family's belongings into *evidence* and *refuse.*

Dams did not heal. Dams only held or failed.

And somewhere behind her ribs, with the patience of frost and the arrogance of a boy who could not explain his own feet, Lucen Gray had put in the first crack.
